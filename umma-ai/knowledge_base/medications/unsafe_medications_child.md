# unsafe_medications_child.md
# UMMA Medication File — Unsafe Medications for Children Under 5
# Category: Medications | Population: Children under 5
# Last reviewed: 2025
# NOTE: UMMA never gives doses. Always refer to doctor or pharmacist for all treatment decisions.

---

## OVERVIEW

Children under 5 are not small adults. Many medications that are safe for adults — and that are widely sold at patent medicine vendors in Nigeria — are harmful or potentially fatal for young children. This file equips UMMA to advise parents clearly about what not to give.

---

## UNSAFE MEDICATIONS FOR CHILDREN UNDER 5

### Aspirin
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Aspirin tablets, Disprin, some combination cold and headache tablets containing aspirin

**Why it is unsafe:**
Aspirin given to children with viral infections causes Reye syndrome — a rare but potentially fatal condition causing liver failure and brain swelling. It should never be used as a fever or pain medicine in children under 16.

**Safe alternative:** Paracetamol syrup for fever and pain

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Never give aspirin tablets to a child under 16 — it can cause serious liver and brain damage. Use paracetamol syrup instead for fever and pain. This applies even for adults' aspirin tablets broken in half."

**If already given:**
"If your child was given aspirin once for a fever, watch for signs of vomiting that does not stop, unusual sleepiness, or confusion. If any of these develop, take the child to hospital immediately and tell them aspirin was given. A single dose may not cause harm, but the doctor should know."

---

### Ibuprofen in children under 3 months
**Why it is unsafe:**
Ibuprofen is not recommended for children under 3 months — immature kidney function increases the risk of renal toxicity. In older children (over 3 months) ibuprofen can be used carefully but paracetamol remains the first choice.

**Note on older children:** Ibuprofen syrup in children over 3 months is used in many countries but is more likely to cause stomach irritation and kidney strain than paracetamol. In a child with malaria (where haemolysis is already occurring), ibuprofen may worsen renal impairment — paracetamol is preferred.

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Do not give ibuprofen to babies under 3 months. For older children, paracetamol is usually the safer choice especially if malaria is a concern."

---

### Chloroquine — for malaria
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Malaricin, Nivaquine, various chloroquine tablets and syrups

**Why it is unsafe:**
Chloroquine is no longer effective against Plasmodium falciparum malaria in Nigeria — over 95% resistance. Using chloroquine for malaria means the child is receiving ineffective treatment while the malaria progresses to severe disease. This kills children.

**Note:** Chloroquine is still used for P. vivax malaria (rare in Nigeria) and rheumatic conditions — but not for standard malaria treatment in Nigeria.

**Safe alternative:** ACT (Lonart, Coartem) prescribed at clinic

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Chloroquine (like Malaricin) no longer works for malaria in Nigeria. If your child has malaria, she needs the proper combination malaria medicine. Please go to the clinic or hospital for the right treatment — do not use chloroquine."

---

### Artemisinin monotherapy — artemether alone
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Artenam, various single-agent artemether capsules and syrups widely sold at PMVs

**Why it is unsafe:**
Artemether given alone (without a partner drug like lumefantrine) clears parasites initially but fails to eliminate the infection completely. Recrudescence (return of malaria) is very common. Single-agent artemisinin use also drives resistance development — a serious public health threat. WHO has banned artemisinin monotherapy.

**Safe alternative:** ACT (artemether-lumefantrine = Lonart, Coartem) — the combination

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Artemether alone is not enough to treat malaria properly — your child needs the combination tablet that includes both artemether and lumefantrine (like Lonart or Coartem). Ask the pharmacist or clinic for the combination."

---

### Antidiarrhoeal medicines — Loperamide (Imodium), Kaolin-morphine preparations
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Imodium, Kaolin-pectin preparations, various "stop diarrhoea" syrups including those containing morphine derivatives

**Why it is unsafe:**
- Loperamide (Imodium): contraindicated in children under 2 years; can cause ileus, abdominal distension, and paradoxical worsening; in very young children has caused fatal respiratory depression
- Kaolin-morphine: contains morphine derivatives; sedation and respiratory depression risk in young children; ineffective at preventing dehydration
- Anti-diarrhoeal medicines do not treat dehydration — they stop the gut moving while fluid continues to be lost into the bowel

**Safe alternative:** ORS + zinc + continued feeding

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Do not give Imodium or 'stop diarrhoea' medicines to young children — they are not safe and they do not actually help. The right treatment for diarrhoea is ORS sachets for fluid, zinc tablets for recovery, and continuing to feed and breastfeed. These are the treatments that save children's lives."

**If already given:**
"If your child was given one dose of Imodium, watch her closely. If she becomes very sleepy, stops passing wind or stool, or her tummy becomes bloated and hard, take her to the hospital straight away. Do not give more doses."

---

### Cough syrups containing codeine, dextromethorphan, or antihistamines
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Many combination cough syrups sold at PMVs — Benylin, Actifed, Piriton Syrup, Cosylan — many contain antihistamines, cough suppressants, and sometimes opioid derivatives

**Why it is unsafe:**
- Codeine: metabolised to morphine; causes respiratory depression and death in children; banned for use in children under 12 in many countries
- Dextromethorphan: in overdose causes hallucinations, seizures, respiratory depression in young children
- Antihistamines (chlorphenamine): cause sedation; in young infants can cause apnoea; paradoxical excitation in some children
- No cough syrup has proven benefit for acute cough in children under 6 — none are effective

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Cough syrups — including Benylin and Piriton — are not safe or helpful for children under 5 with a cough. They can cause serious side effects. For a cough with fever in a child, go to the clinic — the child may need an antibiotic if it is a chest infection. Do not give cough medicine from the chemist to a young child."

---

### Tramadol
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Tramadol capsules and tablets — very widely sold at PMVs in Kano

**Why it is unsafe:**
- Causes respiratory depression in children
- Extremely widely misused in Kano — sometimes given to children to "calm" them; this is dangerous
- Not approved for use in children under 12

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Tramadol is not safe for children and should never be given to a child under any circumstances. It can cause serious breathing problems and death in young children."

---

### Tetracycline antibiotics — Doxycycline, Tetracycline
**Why it is unsafe:**
Causes permanent staining and damage to developing teeth (brown discolouration) and impairs bone growth. Contraindicated in children under 8 years.

**Safe alternative antibiotics:** Amoxicillin, erythromycin, azithromycin as prescribed

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Doxycycline and tetracycline tablets damage children's teeth permanently and should never be given to children under 8. If your child needs an antibiotic, go to the clinic for one that is safe."

---

### Ciprofloxacin and other fluoroquinolones
**Why it is unsafe:**
Causes damage to developing joint cartilage in children. Not used in routine paediatric practice. Widely sold at PMVs in Kano for any infection — children are sometimes given adult doses.

**Safe alternative:** Amoxicillin or other appropriate paediatric antibiotic from clinic

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Ciprofloxacin (Cipro) is not safe for young children and should not be given without a specialist prescribing it. Go to the clinic for the right antibiotic."

---

### Traditional eye drops — Breastmilk, herbal drops, urine
**Why it is unsafe:**
Application of breast milk, herbal preparations, or urine to a child's eyes — common practice in parts of Kano for conjunctivitis — introduces bacteria and worsens infection. Can lead to corneal scarring and blindness. Neonatal gonococcal conjunctivitis is an emergency requiring systemic antibiotics — not eye drops alone.

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Do not put breast milk, herbal water, or any homemade liquid in your child's eyes. This can make the infection worse and damage the eye. Go to the clinic for proper eye drops."

---

### Herbal enemas and rectal preparations for fever
**Why it is unsafe:**
Herbal substances inserted rectally in children cause chemical colitis, perforation, electrolyte disturbances, and death. This practice is documented in northern Nigeria and in paediatric ward admission records in Kano.

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Never put herbal medicines or liquids inside your child's bottom for fever or illness. This is very dangerous and has caused serious harm and death in children. Use paracetamol syrup for fever and go to the clinic."

---

### Adult formulations given to children at reduced doses
**Why it is unsafe:**
Adult tablets broken in half, quarters, or dissolved in water for children is not a safe substitute for weight-appropriate paediatric formulations. Crushing or dissolving certain tablets (e.g. enteric-coated, modified-release) changes their absorption profile unpredictably. Some adult formulations contain excipients not safe for young children.

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Do not break adult tablets into pieces for your child. Children need medicines specifically made for their age and weight. Ask the pharmacist for the children's syrup, not the adult tablet."

## WHAT TO DO IF ALREADY GIVEN

### UMMA framework for unsafe medications already given to a child:

**Immediately dangerous — go to hospital now:**
- Child given loperamide/Imodium and is now sleepy, floppy, or not breathing normally
- Child given aspirin and developing vomiting + confusion + unusual sleepiness
- Child given tramadol — any amount — and appearing drowsy or breathing slowly
- Child given a herbal enema and appears in pain, bloated, or collapsed
- Child given adult overdose of any medication

**Concerning but monitor — attend clinic today:**
- Child given chloroquine for malaria and appears to be worsening — needs correct malaria treatment
- Child given doxycycline course — teeth staining risk but not immediately dangerous; mention at clinic
- Child given cough syrup with antihistamine once — monitor for unusual drowsiness; if very sleepy or breathing strangely, attend hospital

**Reassure and advise clinic at next visit:**
- Single dose of a borderline medication (e.g. ibuprofen in a child over 3 months who is not ill with malaria)
- One sachet of an inappropriate but non-acutely-toxic preparation

**Template response — already given unsafe medication:**
"What your child was given is not recommended for young children. Please watch her closely right now — if she becomes very sleepy and hard to wake, has trouble breathing, or seems in pain, take her to the hospital immediately and tell them what she was given. If she seems fine for now, please go to the clinic today so a nurse can check her over and give you advice on the safest medicines for her going forward."
