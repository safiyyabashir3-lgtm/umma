# UMMA Medication File — Safe Medications for Children Under 5
# Category: Medications | Population: Children under 5
# Last reviewed: 2025
# NOTE: UMMA never gives doses. Always refer to doctor or pharmacist for dosing decisions.
# Dosing in children is weight-based — always confirm with a clinician or pharmacist.

---

## OVERVIEW

Medications in children under 5 require careful attention because:
- Dosing is weight-based — the wrong dose can be dangerous or ineffective
- Many adult medications are not safe in young children
- Some medications have age restrictions (e.g. not under 2 months, not under 6 months)
- Children in this age group often cannot report side effects clearly

UMMA's role: confirm whether a medication category is generally safe for children, advise on what to avoid, and always direct to a clinician or pharmacist for the actual dose.

## SAFE MEDICATIONS FOR CHILDREN UNDER 5

### Paracetamol (Acetaminophen) — syrup or suppository
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Emzor Paracetamol Syrup, Panadol Baby, M&B Paracetamol Syrup, various generic syrups; paracetamol suppositories (less available)

**Why it is safe:**
Paracetamol is the first-line medication for fever and mild to moderate pain in children of all ages including newborns. It is safe at correct weight-based doses. It does not cause Reye syndrome (unlike aspirin). It does not cause stomach irritation (unlike ibuprofen).

**Important caveats:**
- Dose must be weight-based — a pharmacist or clinician should confirm the correct amount for this child's weight
- Do not double dose — check if paracetamol is already in any combination medicine being given
- Do not use alcohol-based paracetamol preparations in very young infants
- Overdose causes liver failure — more is not better
- Treats fever and discomfort but does not treat the underlying cause — malaria, pneumonia, etc. must still be diagnosed and treated

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Paracetamol syrup (like Emzor or Panadol Baby) is safe for children of all ages for fever and pain. The amount to give depends on your child's weight — ask the pharmacist or nurse to confirm the right amount. Give it every 4–6 hours only when needed, and do not give more than directed."

### Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS)
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Dioralyte, Electroral, WHO ORS sachets

**Why it is safe:**
ORS is safe for children of all ages including newborns and is the single most important treatment for diarrhoea and dehydration. It reduces child mortality from diarrhoeal disease dramatically. It replaces fluid and electrolytes lost in stool and vomiting.

**Important caveats:**
- Mix according to instructions — do not add extra sugar or salt
- Give small frequent sips — a young child who drinks too much too fast will vomit it back
- ORS does not stop diarrhoea — it prevents dehydration while the illness runs its course
- Continue breastfeeding alongside ORS in breastfed infants
- If child cannot keep ORS down, or has signs of severe dehydration (sunken eyes, very dry mouth, not urinating, limp): go to hospital for IV fluids

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"ORS sachets are the safest and most important medicine you can give a child with diarrhoea. Mix one sachet in the right amount of clean water — the packet shows how much. Give small sips very often. Keep breastfeeding if you are breastfeeding. If your child cannot keep any liquid down or seems very weak, take her to the clinic."

### Artemisinin-based Combination Therapy (ACT) — for malaria in children
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Lonart (artemether-lumefantrine), Coartem, Artequick — paediatric formulations or scored tablets

**Why it is safe:**
ACT is the recommended first-line treatment for uncomplicated malaria in children of all ages (including infants over 5 kg). It is safe and effective. The risk of untreated malaria — death from severe malaria, severe anaemia, cerebral malaria — vastly outweighs any risk from ACT.

**Important caveats:**
- Dose is weight-based — must be prescribed or dispensed by a clinician or pharmacist based on the child's current weight
- Artemisinin monotherapy (artemether alone without partner drug) is not safe or effective — always use the combination
- Chloroquine is no longer effective against falciparum malaria in Nigeria — do not use
- Full course must be completed — even if the child looks better after the first dose
- In children under 5 kg or under 3 months: specialist guidance required — refer to hospital

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"If your child has malaria, she needs the proper combination malaria medicine — like Lonart or Coartem. The amount depends on her weight, so go to the clinic or pharmacist who can check and prescribe the right amount. Do not buy just artemether alone — it must be the combination. And always finish the full course of tablets even if she seems better."

### Zinc supplementation — for diarrhoea
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Zincovit, Zinc sulphate syrup, various OTC zinc syrups

**Why it is safe:**
Zinc reduces the duration and severity of diarrhoea in children under 5 and reduces the risk of further diarrhoea episodes for 2–3 months after. WHO recommends zinc alongside ORS for all children with diarrhoea. It is safe.

**Important caveats:**
- Age restriction: zinc supplementation for diarrhoea is recommended for children over 2 months
- Give for the full recommended course (10–14 days) even when diarrhoea resolves
- Zinc should supplement ORS — not replace it

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Alongside ORS, zinc tablets or syrup are safe and helpful for children with diarrhoea — they help the child recover faster. Ask the pharmacist or clinic nurse for the zinc medicine and give it for the full 10 days even when the diarrhoea has stopped."

### Amoxicillin — for bacterial infections including pneumonia
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Amoxil, Wymox, various generic amoxicillin syrups and capsules

**Why it is safe:**
Amoxicillin is the first-line antibiotic for community-acquired pneumonia, ear infections, and some other bacterial infections in children. It is safe in children of all ages. It is on the WHO Essential Medicines List for children.

**Important caveats:**
- Requires prescription — UMMA should not advise purchasing without clinical assessment
- Dose is weight-based
- Full course must be completed — stopping early leads to treatment failure and antibiotic resistance
- Children with penicillin allergy should not receive amoxicillin — alternative antibiotics available
- Does not treat viral infections — no benefit for common cold or viral diarrhoea

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Amoxicillin syrup is the most common antibiotic used for chest infections and ear infections in children. It is safe. The clinic will prescribe the right amount for your child's weight. Always finish the full course — usually 5 days — even when your child seems better."

### Vitamin A supplementation
**Why it is safe:**
Vitamin A deficiency is common in under-5 children in northern Nigeria and increases severity of measles, diarrhoea, and respiratory infections. High-dose vitamin A is given at measles diagnosis and as a supplement to malnourished children — it is safe at recommended doses.

**Important caveats:**
- Very high doses of vitamin A are toxic — supplementation should be as recommended at clinic, not self-dosed repeatedly at home
- Routine supplementation schedule: 6 months, 12 months, 18 months, 24 months per NPHCDA schedule

### Oral Rehydration Salt + Zinc + continued feeding — the diarrhoea package
These three together are the complete safe home management of diarrhoea in children over 2 months:
1. ORS for dehydration
2. Zinc for recovery and prevention
3. Continue feeding — do not withhold food or breast milk

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"For a child with diarrhoea: give ORS for the loose stools, zinc for 10 days, and keep feeding and breastfeeding. These three together are the safest and best treatment. Do not give anti-diarrhoea medicines — they are not safe for young children."

### Rectal Artesunate Suppository — pre-referral severe malaria
**Brand names in Nigeria:** Rectocap, artesunate suppositories (available through some community health programmes and PHCs)

**Why it is safe:**
Pre-referral rectal artesunate is recommended by WHO for children with suspected severe malaria when IV treatment is not immediately available. It is safe and reduces mortality by bridging the gap between recognition of severe malaria and arrival at hospital. It is not a substitute for IV artesunate at hospital.

**Important caveats:**
- Given rectally — not orally
- Go to hospital immediately after giving it — it is a bridge, not a treatment
- Only for children who cannot swallow or are unconscious

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"If the health worker has given your child a suppository for severe malaria, that is a bridge to help until you reach hospital. Do not wait at home — go to the hospital immediately after it is given."

## WHAT TO DO IF ALREADY GIVEN

**General UMMA framework:**

If a child has been given a safe medication and the parent is concerned:
1. Reassure that the medication is safe
2. Confirm dose was appropriate for the child's weight — if clearly too high, advise calling a pharmacist or attending clinic
3. Advise mentioning at next clinic visit if there is any ongoing concern

**Example — parent gave paracetamol syrup for fever:**
"Paracetamol syrup is safe for children. As long as the amount was appropriate for your child's weight and you are not giving it too frequently, there is nothing to worry about. If you are unsure about the right amount, ask a pharmacist."
