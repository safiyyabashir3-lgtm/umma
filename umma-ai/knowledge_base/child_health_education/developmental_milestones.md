# Developmental Milestones in Children Under 5

**Topic:** How Children Grow and Develop — What to Expect and When to Be Concerned
**Guideline Source:** WHO Child Development Standards; FMOH Nigeria IMCI Guidelines; UNICEF ECD Framework
**Context:** Kano State, Northern Nigeria

---

## OVERVIEW

Every child grows and develops at their own pace. But there are general patterns — called developmental milestones — that most healthy children reach by certain ages. These milestones cover four areas:

- **Movement (motor skills)** — how the child moves their body
- **Communication (language)** — how the child communicates, listens, and speaks
- **Thinking (cognitive skills)** — how the child learns, plays, and solves problems
- **Social and emotional skills** — how the child relates to people around them

Knowing these milestones helps you notice if your child is developing well — or if something needs to be checked early. Early support for developmental concerns makes a big difference.

## KEY INFORMATION

### Milestones by Age

---

**Birth to 3 months**

*Movement:*
- Moves arms and legs when awake
- Lifts head briefly when placed on tummy
- Startles at loud sounds

*Communication:*
- Cries to communicate needs
- Makes small sounds (cooing)
- Responds to your voice — quiets or turns toward you

*Social:*
- Looks at faces
- Starts to smile at people around 6–8 weeks

---

**3 to 6 months**

*Movement:*
- Holds head steady
- Pushes up on arms when on tummy
- Brings hands to mouth
- Rolls from tummy to back

*Communication:*
- Makes more sounds — babbling, laughing
- Responds to their name

*Social:*
- Smiles at familiar people
- Shows interest in surroundings, looks around

---

**6 to 9 months**

*Movement:*
- Sits with some support, then without support
- Begins to crawl or shuffle
- Bears weight on legs when held standing

*Communication:*
- Babbles strings of sounds ("bababa", "mamama")
- Responds to their name being called
- Reacts differently to different tones of voice (e.g., sounds worried at an angry tone)

*Thinking:*
- Looks for a toy that is hidden (knows it still exists)
- Puts things in mouth to explore them

*Social:*
- May show fear of strangers
- Enjoys social games like peek-a-boo

---

**9 to 12 months**

*Movement:*
- Pulls to standing
- Walks while holding furniture (cruising)
- May take first steps alone toward 12 months
- Uses thumb and finger to pick up small objects (pincer grip)

*Communication:*
- Says "mama" or "dada" with meaning
- Understands simple words like "no" and their own name
- Points to things they want

*Thinking:*
- Imitates actions (clapping, waving)
- Explores objects by banging, dropping, shaking

*Social:*
- Shows attachment to caregivers — wants to be close to you
- Waves bye-bye when shown

---

**12 to 18 months**

*Movement:*
- Walks alone (usually by 12–15 months, some up to 18)
- Begins to climb stairs (with support)
- Scribbles with a crayon

*Communication:*
- Says 3–10 words or more
- Points to show you things
- Follows simple one-step instructions ("Give me the cup")

*Thinking:*
- Plays pretend (feeding a doll, using a spoon)
- Recognises familiar people in photos

*Social:*
- Shows affection — hugs, kisses
- Shows feelings — frustration, jealousy, happiness

---

**18 months to 2 years**

*Movement:*
- Runs (though still falls)
- Kicks a ball
- Carries or pulls toys while walking

*Communication:*
- Vocabulary grows rapidly — 50+ words by 24 months
- Begins to combine 2 words ("More milk", "Mama go")
- Points to pictures in books when named

*Thinking:*
- Sorts shapes and colours
- Completes simple puzzles
- Follows two-step instructions

*Social:*
- Plays alongside other children (not yet with them — called parallel play)
- Increasingly independent — may resist help

---

**2 to 3 years**

*Movement:*
- Climbs well
- Runs easily
- Jumps with both feet
- Begins to dress with help

*Communication:*
- Speaks in sentences of 2–3 words or more
- Strangers can understand about half of what they say
- Asks "what" and "where" questions

*Thinking:*
- Plays make-believe
- Understands concepts like "mine" and "his"
- Can follow 3-step instructions

*Social:*
- Interested in other children
- Shows wide range of emotions
- Can become frustrated easily (tantrums are common and normal at this age)

---

**3 to 5 years**

*Movement:*
- Hops, skips, climbs with ease
- Throws and catches a ball
- Draws recognisable shapes and figures (by age 4–5)

*Communication:*
- Speaks in clear sentences; strangers understand most of what they say
- Tells stories
- Asks many questions — "why", "how", "when"
- Knows their full name, age, and gender

*Thinking:*
- Counts to 10 or beyond
- Understands concepts of time (yesterday, today, tomorrow)
- Can name colours and shapes

*Social:*
- Plays cooperatively with other children
- Understands rules
- Shows empathy — comforts upset friends

## NIGERIAN CONTEXT

**Development and environment in Kano:**
Children develop through interaction, play, stimulation, and language. In many households in Kano, mothers and grandmothers naturally provide this through talking, singing, carrying, playing, and storytelling. This is extremely valuable — you do not need toys or special materials to support your child's development.

However, factors that are common in northern Nigeria can slow development:
- **Malnutrition** (especially iron deficiency) slows brain development
- **Frequent illness** (malaria, diarrhoea) interrupts development
- **Limited language stimulation** — children who are not talked to regularly develop language more slowly
- **Trauma or stress in the household** affects emotional and cognitive development

**Simple things you already do that support development:**
- Carrying and talking to your baby while you work
- Singing songs and telling stories (tatsuniya)
- Playing naming games with everyday objects
- Responding when your baby babbles or cries
- Allowing older babies to explore (safely) — crawling, touching, moving

## COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

**"Boys develop later than girls — it is normal for a boy to not speak yet."**
There is some evidence that girls develop language slightly earlier on average, but the difference is small. A child — boy or girl — who is not speaking any words by 18 months or not combining words by 24 months needs to be assessed. Do not wait, assuming it is normal for boys.

**"My child is quiet and not speaking much — this is just their character."**
Personality varies, but all children develop language on a predictable schedule. A quiet child who does not respond to their name, does not babble, and does not attempt words by 12–15 months is not just shy — they need to be evaluated for hearing problems or developmental concerns.

**"If I carry my baby too much, they will be spoilt and not learn to be independent."**
Babies cannot be spoilt by being held and carried. Responsive caregiving — holding, comforting, responding to cries — builds security and confidence. Babies who are responded to consistently actually become more confident and independent as toddlers, not less.

**"Walking and talking later runs in our family — it is normal for us."**
Family patterns can affect timing. But significant delays — not walking by 18 months, not saying 10+ words by 18 months, not combining words by 24 months — should always be assessed regardless of family history. Early support gives much better outcomes than waiting.

**"Playing is wasting time — children should sit quietly."**
Play IS how young children learn. A child playing is a child developing their brain, language, social skills, and problem-solving abilities. Encourage play actively. Safe, active, free play is one of the best things for a child's development.

## PRACTICAL ADVICE

**0–6 months:**
- Talk to your baby constantly — describe what you are doing, what you see
- Maintain eye contact during feeding
- Respond to cries promptly — this builds trust and security
- Do tummy time every day when the baby is awake and you are watching — this builds neck and shoulder strength

**6–12 months:**
- Name everything around you — "water", "hand", "cup"
- Play peek-a-boo and clapping games
- Give safe objects to explore — cups, spoons, soft cloths
- Let them try to feed themselves with soft finger foods

**1–2 years:**
- Read or look at picture books together — even if your child cannot read, naming pictures builds vocabulary
- Sing songs, clap rhythms, tell stories
- Let them help with simple tasks — stirring, carrying, handing you things
- Give simple choices — "Do you want the red cup or the blue cup?"

**2–5 years:**
- Ask open questions — "What did you see today?", "What happened?"
- Let them play with other children — social play is essential at this age
- Praise effort, not just results — "You worked so hard at that"
- Establish routines — children thrive on predictable schedules for meals, sleep, and activity

**All ages:**
- Protect your child from violence, shouting, and fear in the home — stress harms developing brains
- Ensure good nutrition — iron deficiency is the most common cause of preventable cognitive delay

## WHEN TO SEEK CARE

**Talk to your health worker if your child:**

By **3 months:**
- Does not follow moving objects with eyes
- Does not react to loud sounds
- Does not smile at people

By **6 months:**
- Does not laugh or make squealing sounds
- Does not reach for objects
- Does not show affection for familiar caregivers

By **9 months:**
- Does not babble
- Does not respond to their name
- Does not bear weight on legs when supported

By **12 months:**
- Is not crawling in any way
- Does not stand with support
- Does not say "mama" or "dada" with meaning
- Does not point or wave

By **18 months:**
- Is not walking
- Does not say at least 6–10 words
- Does not follow simple instructions

By **24 months:**
- Does not combine 2 words
- Does not point to things to show interest
- Has lost skills they previously had — this is always a concern

**At any age:** Loss of previously gained skills — stopped walking, stopped talking — is an urgent concern. Go to a clinic immediately.

Early assessment and early support give much better outcomes than waiting. You are not overreacting by asking for a developmental check.
