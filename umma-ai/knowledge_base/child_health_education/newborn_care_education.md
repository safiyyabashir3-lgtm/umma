# Newborn Care

**Topic:** Caring for Your Baby in the First 28 Days of Life
**Guideline Source:** FMOH Nigeria Newborn Health Guidelines; WHO Essential Newborn Care; UNICEF Nigeria
**Context:** Kano State, Northern Nigeria

---

## OVERVIEW

The first 28 days of life — called the **newborn period** — are the most dangerous days of a child's life. In Nigeria, a large proportion of child deaths happen in this first month. Most of these deaths can be prevented with simple, affordable care that every mother can provide.

This file explains the most important things you need to know to keep your newborn alive and well in those critical first weeks: warmth, feeding, hygiene, cord care, and recognising danger signs early.

---

## KEY INFORMATION

### 1. Warmth — Keep Your Baby Warm

Newborn babies cannot control their own body temperature the way adults can. They lose heat very quickly — especially small babies and babies born early. A cold baby can die.

**How to keep your baby warm:**
- Dry your baby immediately after birth — wet skin causes rapid heat loss
- Wrap your baby in a dry, clean cloth right away
- Cover the baby's head — babies lose a lot of heat through their head
- **Skin-to-skin contact:** Place your naked baby directly on your bare chest, then cover both of you with a blanket. This is the best way to keep a newborn warm. It also helps with breastfeeding and bonding.
- Keep the room warm — avoid cold drafts from open windows or fans blowing directly on the baby
- Do not bathe your baby for the first 24 hours — bathing too early causes dangerous heat loss

**If your baby feels cold to the touch:**
- Undress the baby and place them skin-to-skin on your chest
- Cover both of you with a warm cloth or blanket
- Breastfeed if possible
- If the baby does not warm up within 30 minutes, go to the clinic

### 2. Breastfeeding — Start Within the First Hour

Put your baby to the breast within the **first hour after birth**. The first milk — called **colostrum** — is thick and yellowish. It is extremely valuable. It contains antibodies and protection that your baby cannot get from any other source. Do not discard it.

Breastfeed your baby **on demand** — whenever they show feeding cues (rooting, sucking motions, bringing hands to mouth, fussing). In the first weeks, this is usually 8–12 times per day, including at night.

Do not give water, glucose water, formula, or pap. Breast milk is all your baby needs for the first 6 months.

For more detailed guidance, see the breastfeeding education file.

### 3. Cord Care — Keep the Umbilical Cord Clean and Dry

The umbilical cord stump falls off in 7–14 days. Until it falls off, keep it clean and dry.

**Correct cord care:**
- Do not put anything on the cord — no herbs, no shea butter, no ash, no animal dung, no binding cloth tightly over it
- Clean the base of the cord gently with a clean cloth if dirty
- Keep the cord open to the air — fold the nappy below the cord so it is not covered
- If you notice redness, swelling, pus, or bad smell from the cord — go to the clinic immediately

### 4. Hygiene — Protecting Your Newborn from Infection

Newborns have very little immunity. Infections that are minor for an adult can kill a newborn.

**Essential hygiene rules:**
- **Wash your hands** thoroughly with soap and water before touching your baby, before feeding, and after changing nappies
- Limit the number of people who handle your newborn in the first weeks
- Anyone who is sick with a cough, cold, or fever should not hold the baby
- Keep the baby's sleeping area clean
- Wash nappies and baby clothes thoroughly

### 5. Sleeping Safely

- Place your baby on their **back** to sleep — not on their stomach or side. Sleeping on the stomach increases the risk of Sudden Infant Death Syndrome (SIDS).
- Keep the sleeping area **smoke-free**
- Do not put pillows, heavy blankets, or soft toys in the baby's sleep space — these can cause suffocation
- If you share a bed (which many mothers do for warmth and feeding), be careful not to roll onto the baby. A safer option is a separate mattress next to yours.

### 6. The First Clinic Visit

Take your newborn to a PHC or hospital within **24–72 hours of birth** if you delivered at home. If you delivered at a facility, they will do newborn checks before you are discharged.

Your baby's first PHC visit should happen by **Day 3** and again at **Day 7** and **Day 28**. At these visits, the health worker will:
- Weigh your baby
- Check for danger signs
- Give BCG vaccine and first polio drops (if not given at birth)
- Support with breastfeeding
- Check the cord

## NIGERIAN CONTEXT

**Home deliveries and newborn care in Kano:**
Many women in Kano and northern Nigeria deliver at home with a traditional birth attendant (TBA). If you deliver at home:
- Ensure the cord is cut with a clean, new blade — never a shared or unclean instrument
- Do not tie the cord tightly with unclean material
- Go to a PHC within 24–72 hours for your baby's first check and vaccines
- Contact a health worker or TBA trained in essential newborn care if available

**Small babies:**
If your baby is born early (before 37 weeks) or born small (less than 2.5 kg / 5.5 lbs), they need extra care. Small babies are at high risk of cold, infection, and feeding difficulties. **Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC)** — continuous skin-to-skin contact — is proven to save small babies' lives. Ask your health worker about KMC if your baby is small. Some PHCs and hospitals in Kano provide KMC support.

**Traditional practices in northern Nigeria:**
Some traditional newborn practices are common in Kano that may be harmful. This file addresses them in the misconceptions section below. Health workers will not judge you — they want to support you to care for your baby safely.

## COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

**"Give the baby water, glucose, or sugar water before first breastfeed."**
This is not necessary and is harmful. Breast milk — especially the first colostrum — provides everything the baby needs including fluids. Giving water first delays breastfeeding, reduces the baby's interest in feeding, and introduces infection risk. Breastfeed first, before anything else.

**"Colostrum (the first yellowish milk) is dirty or bad for the baby — throw it away."**
Colostrum is not dirty — it is liquid gold. It is full of antibodies, nutrients, and protective factors that your baby cannot get from any other source. It is produced in small amounts because a newborn's stomach is tiny. Never discard it. Put your baby to the breast immediately.

**"Applying something to the cord — shea butter, oil, ash, herbs — will help it heal faster."**
This is not correct and can be dangerous. Anything applied to the cord can introduce bacteria and cause a serious infection called cord sepsis, which can kill a newborn. Keep the cord clean and dry. Leave it alone.

**"The baby needs to be bathed immediately after birth to clean them."**
Bathing within the first 24 hours causes dangerous heat loss that can kill a newborn. The white coating on the baby (vernix) is protective and should be left on. Delay bathing for at least 24 hours — and make sure the room is warm when you do.

**"The baby is crying because my breast milk is not enough — give formula or pap."**
Newborns cry for many reasons — hunger, cold, discomfort, needing closeness. Breast milk in the first days is produced in exactly the right amount for your baby's tiny stomach. The more you put the baby to the breast, the more milk your body makes. Formula or pap given in the first weeks reduces breastfeeding and reduces your milk supply.

**"Newborns should be kept bundled tightly with binding cloths."**
Tight swaddling and binding is common in northern Nigeria. Light swaddling can be comforting, but tight binding — especially around the chest and abdomen — can restrict breathing and cause problems. Wrap your baby snugly but not tightly. The baby should be able to breathe freely and move their legs.

## PRACTICAL ADVICE

1. **Dry and warm immediately at birth** — have a clean, dry cloth ready before delivery to dry and wrap the baby right away.

2. **Skin-to-skin from the first minutes** — this single practice reduces the risk of cold, supports breathing, encourages breastfeeding, and calms both mother and baby.

3. **Breastfeed within the first hour** — sit with a helper if needed. Ask a nurse or midwife to help you latch correctly.

4. **Never put anything on the cord** — clean and dry only.

5. **Wash your hands every single time before touching your baby** — especially before feeding.

6. **Go to a PHC within 3 days of a home delivery** — for vaccines, a newborn check, and support.

7. **Watch your baby closely for danger signs** — learn the list below and act immediately if you see any.

8. **Sleep with your baby skin-to-skin or nearby** — newborns need frequent feeds at night. Being close makes this easier and helps you notice if something is wrong.

9. **Rest as much as possible** — your body is recovering from birth. Accept help from family for household tasks so you can focus on your baby.

10. **Talk to your health worker about anything that worries you** — no concern is too small in the newborn period.

## WHEN TO SEEK CARE

**Go to a clinic or hospital IMMEDIATELY if your newborn:**

🔴 Is not breathing or is gasping  
🔴 Has convulsions (jerking, shaking of the body)  
🔴 Is very cold and not warming up with skin-to-skin contact  
🔴 Is yellow all over (jaundice covering the chest, belly, arms, legs) — mild yellowing of the face in first days can be normal, but spreading yellow needs urgent assessment  
🔴 Has a cord that is red, swollen, smells bad, or has pus  
🔴 Has not fed in 6 hours and cannot be woken to feed  
🔴 Is breathing very fast (more than 60 breaths per minute) or grunting with each breath  
🔴 Has a high fever (feels hot to touch, temperature above 37.5°C) OR is very cold  
🔴 Has swollen, red eyes with discharge in first days  
🔴 Is very floppy and not moving  

**Do not wait until morning. Do not try to treat at home. A newborn can deteriorate very fast.**

Tell the health worker: "This is a newborn — they are less than 28 days old." This should move you to be seen quickly.
