# Immunization in Nigeria

**Topic:** Childhood Vaccinations — What They Are, When to Give Them, and Why They Matter
**Guideline Source:** Nigeria National Routine Immunization Schedule (NPHCDA); WHO-UNICEF Immunization Guidelines
**Context:** Kano State, Northern Nigeria

---

## OVERVIEW

Immunization — also called vaccination — protects your child from serious diseases that can kill or permanently harm them. When a child is vaccinated, their body learns to fight a specific disease so that if they ever meet that germ, they are protected.

In Nigeria, vaccines are provided **free of charge** at government health facilities. The Federal Government of Nigeria, through the National Primary Health Care Development Agency (NPHCDA), provides a schedule of vaccines for every child from birth to 18 months.

Missing vaccines leaves your child unprotected. Many of the diseases vaccines prevent — like measles, meningitis, and whooping cough — are still common in northern Nigeria and can kill young children quickly.

## KEY INFORMATION

### Nigeria Routine Immunization Schedule

| Age | Vaccines Given |
|-----|---------------|
| At birth | BCG (tuberculosis), OPV 0 (polio), Hepatitis B birth dose |
| 6 weeks | OPV 1, Pentavalent 1 (DTP-HepB-Hib), PCV 1, Rotavirus 1 |
| 10 weeks | OPV 2, Pentavalent 2, PCV 2, Rotavirus 2 |
| 14 weeks | OPV 3, Pentavalent 3, PCV 3, IPV (injectable polio) |
| 6 months | Vitamin A (first dose) |
| 9 months | Measles-Rubella (MR) 1, Meningococcal A (MenAfriVac), Yellow Fever, Vitamin A |
| 12 months | — (check with your PHC for any catch-up vaccines) |
| 15–18 months | Measles-Rubella (MR) 2, Vitamin A |

**What each vaccine protects against:**
- **BCG** — tuberculosis (TB), which causes serious lung disease
- **OPV / IPV** — poliomyelitis, which can permanently paralyse a child
- **Hepatitis B** — liver disease caused by Hepatitis B virus
- **Pentavalent (DTP-HepB-Hib)** — diphtheria, tetanus, whooping cough, hepatitis B, and Hib meningitis — five diseases in one injection
- **PCV (pneumococcal)** — pneumonia and meningitis caused by pneumococcal bacteria (a leading killer of under-5 children)
- **Rotavirus** — severe diarrhoea caused by rotavirus (a major cause of child death in Nigeria)
- **Measles-Rubella** — measles (very dangerous, can cause blindness, brain damage, death) and rubella
- **Meningococcal A** — bacterial meningitis, which is common in the meningitis belt of northern Nigeria
- **Yellow Fever** — yellow fever virus infection
- **Vitamin A** — not a vaccine but given at clinics; prevents blindness and supports the immune system

### Your child's vaccination card
Every child should have a **vaccination card (immunization card)**. This card records every vaccine your child has received and when the next one is due. Keep it safe — this card may be needed for school enrollment and is important if you travel or move.

## NIGERIAN CONTEXT

**Where to get free vaccines in Kano:**
- All **Primary Health Care (PHC) centres** in Kano offer routine immunization — usually on fixed days each week
- **General hospitals** in all LGAs provide vaccination
- **Outreach immunization sessions** — mobile teams visit communities on scheduled days. Ask your CHEW (Community Health Extension Worker) for the schedule in your area

**Kano and the meningitis belt:**
Northern Nigeria — including Kano — sits in Africa's meningitis belt. This means meningococcal meningitis outbreaks happen, especially in the dry season (December to June). The **Meningococcal A vaccine** given at 9 months provides strong protection. Do not miss this vaccine.

**Polio eradication in Nigeria:**
Nigeria was declared polio-free, but the virus can return if vaccination rates drop. Every dose of OPV (oral polio drops) matters. During **National Immunization Days (NIDs)**, volunteers come door to door giving polio drops. Accept these drops — they are safe and protect your child even if they have had their scheduled doses.

**If your child missed a vaccine:**
It is not too late. Go to your nearest PHC and tell them which vaccines your child missed. Most missed vaccines can be caught up. Do not assume your child is permanently unprotected — ask for help.

## COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

**"Vaccines can cause the disease they are meant to prevent."**
This is not correct. Vaccines contain killed or weakened parts of germs — not live, active germs. They cannot give your child the disease. Some children feel slightly unwell for 1–2 days after a vaccine (mild fever, soreness at the injection site) — this is the immune system responding normally and is not a disease.

**"My child looks healthy — they don't need vaccines."**
A healthy-looking child has no defence against diseases like measles or meningitis if they have not been vaccinated. These diseases strike quickly and do not give warning. Vaccination protects a healthy child before they are ever exposed.

**"Vaccines contain haram substances."**
Major Islamic scholars and bodies — including the Nigerian Supreme Council for Islamic Affairs and global Islamic health authorities — have affirmed that vaccines are permissible (halal). Protecting children from disease is an Islamic duty. The Prophet Muhammad (SAW) said: "Make use of medical treatment, for Allah has not made a disease without appointing a remedy for it." Vaccines are a form of that remedy.

**"If my other children had measles and survived, my baby will survive too."**
Measles kills and permanently harms many children who get it. Survival is not guaranteed. Children can lose their sight, develop brain damage, or die from measles complications. The vaccine is safe, free, and prevents all of this.

**"The polio vaccine makes children infertile."**
This rumour has been thoroughly investigated and disproven. Oral polio vaccine contains only weakened polio virus and has no effect on fertility, now or in the future. Millions of Nigerian children have received it safely.

**"One injection is enough — I don't need to come back for more doses."**
Some vaccines need multiple doses to build full protection. For example, the polio and pentavalent vaccines each require 3 doses. Missing doses means your child is not fully protected. Always complete the full schedule.

## PRACTICAL ADVICE

1. **Start at birth** — make sure your baby receives BCG, Hepatitis B, and polio drops on the day of delivery or within the first days. If you deliver at home, visit a PHC within the first week.

2. **Keep the vaccination card safe** — put it in a plastic bag or envelope to protect it from water. Bring it to every clinic visit, not just vaccination appointments.

3. **Know your next vaccination date** — before leaving the clinic, confirm the date for the next vaccine. Write it on your wall or phone. Some PHCs give reminder slips.

4. **Do not skip vaccines because your child is sick with a mild illness** — a mild cold or low fever is not a reason to delay vaccination. Ask the health worker — they will assess whether it is safe to vaccinate.

5. **After vaccination — what to expect:**
   - Mild fever: Give paracetamol syrup (correct dose for weight) and continue breastfeeding
   - Redness or swelling at injection site: Normal; apply a cool cloth
   - A small lump at the BCG site that becomes a sore and heals slowly: This is normal

6. **Accept polio drops during outreach campaigns** — even if your child is up to date, extra doses increase protection.

7. **Tell family members about vaccination** — grandmothers and mothers-in-law often influence health decisions. Share what you know about vaccines and why they matter.

---

## WHEN TO SEEK CARE

After vaccination, most reactions are mild and normal. However, go to a clinic if your child has:
- Fever above 39°C that does not come down with paracetamol
- Continuous crying for more than 3 hours after vaccination
- Fits or convulsions after vaccination
- Difficulty breathing
- Severe swelling beyond the injection site
- Becomes very limp or unresponsive

These reactions are very rare but need to be evaluated.

**Also seek care if:**
- Your child develops any symptoms of the diseases above — high fever with rash (measles), stiff neck (meningitis), difficulty breathing (whooping cough or pneumonia) — whether or not they have been vaccinated
- You are unsure if your child is up to date — go to the PHC with the vaccination card and they will advise you
