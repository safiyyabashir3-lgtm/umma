# SYMPTOM FILE: Abdominal Swelling — Child
**Category:** symptoms | **Subcategory:** child | **Topic:** abdominal_swelling

---

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### Confirming the Symptom
- Caregiver notices the child's abdomen is visibly larger than normal
- Abdomen appears distended — protrudes more than expected for the child's build
- Child's belly feels hard, firm, or tense to caregiver's touch
- Clothes or waistbands no longer fit around the abdomen
- Child reports feeling bloated or full even without eating much
- Distinguish: is the whole abdomen swollen, or a specific area (localised mass)?

### Commonly Confused Symptoms
- **Normal toddler abdomen** — physiological pot-belly common in children under 3 years; distinguish by duration, onset, and associated symptoms
- **Obesity / nutritional overfeeding** — generalised fat distribution, no systemic illness
- **Gas and bloating** — temporary, resolves with passage of gas or stool, not persistent
- **Umbilical hernia** — localised protrusion at navel, soft, reducible; common in African children
- **Inguinal hernia** — swelling in groin, not abdomen proper
- **Abdominal mass** — a specific lump rather than generalised swelling; warrants urgent evaluation

### Critical Immediate Checks
- Is the distension sudden onset (hours) or gradual (weeks)?
- Is the abdomen tense and rigid, or soft?
- Is there associated fever?
- Is the child passing stool and gas normally, or is there no passage at all?
- Is there visible movement under the skin (peristalsis — suggests obstruction)?
- Is the child pale, lethargic, or showing signs of illness?
- Is there a palpable lump or mass?

---

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

*Escalate immediately if any of the following are present:*

1. Acute abdominal distension with no passage of stool or gas — intestinal obstruction, EMERGENCY
2. Bile-stained (green) vomiting with abdominal distension — obstruction, EMERGENCY
3. Rigid or board-like abdomen — peritonitis, EMERGENCY
4. Distension with signs of shock — pallor, cold extremities, rapid breathing, altered consciousness
5. Visible peristalsis through the abdominal wall — high-grade obstruction
6. Abdominal distension following trauma — internal injury
7. Rapidly increasing abdominal distension over hours
8. Distension with high fever in a very unwell child
9. Palpable hard mass in the abdomen — urgent evaluation needed (Wilms tumour, Burkitt lymphoma)
10. Distension with blood in stool — intussusception, volvulus, or dysentery emergency
11. Severe oedema throughout the body alongside abdominal swelling — severe malnutrition, nephrotic syndrome, or heart failure
12. Caregiver reports child is the sickest they have ever seen

---

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

---

### 1. SEVERE ACUTE MALNUTRITION (SAM) — Kwashiorkor
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
Kwashiorkor is one of the most common causes of abdominal distension in children in northern Nigeria. It is a medical emergency with high mortality.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Oedematous malnutrition — bilateral pitting oedema of the feet and legs is the hallmark
- Abdominal distension due to ascites (fluid) and enlarged liver (fatty liver)
- Skin changes — peeling, depigmented patches, flaky paint dermatosis
- Hair changes — thin, sparse, reddish or light-coloured hair
- Child appears miserable, apathetic, irritable
- Mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) below 11.5 cm
- Anorexia — the child is not eating despite hunger
- Frequent infections — immune system severely compromised

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Are the child's feet or legs swollen — does the swelling leave a dent when you press it?
- What has the child been eating? Is the diet mainly starchy food?
- Has the child been losing weight?
- Does the child have skin peeling or colour changes?
- Has the child been ill with infections frequently?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Abdominal distension + bilateral leg oedema + skin/hair changes + poor diet + apathy = kwashiorkor

**Uncertainty Note:**
Kwashiorkor with infection (especially pneumonia, sepsis, or malaria) has very high mortality. Any febrile SAM child must be referred immediately for inpatient management.

---

### 2. INTESTINAL WORMS (Helminthiasis) — Heavy Burden
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
Severe worm infestation, particularly Ascaris lumbricoides, is a major cause of abdominal distension in children in northern Nigeria.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Pot-belly appearance — generalised distension, particularly prominent umbilical area
- Visible worms in stool (Ascaris — large, round, pale pink)
- Chronic intermittent crampy abdominal pain
- Poor growth and failure to thrive
- Pallor from hookworm anaemia
- No fever unless secondary infection
- Child may vomit worms
- Heavy burden in communities without latrines or handwashing facilities

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Has anyone seen worms in the child's stool, or has the child vomited worms?
- How long has the child's belly been this big?
- Is the child growing and gaining weight properly?
- Does the child play in soil or eat soil?
- Does the household have a toilet or latrine?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Chronic distension + visible worms + poor growth + poor WASH environment + no systemic illness

**Uncertainty Note:**
A sudden onset of severe abdominal pain with distension in a child with known worm burden — consider worm bolus intestinal obstruction. This is a surgical emergency. Refer immediately.

---

### 3. MALARIA — Hepatosplenomegaly
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
Repeated malaria infections cause progressive enlargement of both the liver and spleen, presenting as visible abdominal distension particularly in the upper abdomen.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Enlargement is primarily in the upper abdomen — liver on the right, spleen on the left
- History of recurrent fevers or recurrent malaria treatment
- Pallor from chronic anaemia
- Spleen may be massive in hyperreactive malarial splenomegaly (HMS)
- Child may have low-grade ongoing fever, poor appetite, fatigue
- Abdominal distension may develop slowly over weeks to months

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Has the child had malaria many times before?
- Is the swelling mainly at the top of the belly?
- Is the child pale?
- Has the child been having on-and-off fevers for a long time?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Upper abdominal fullness + recurrent malaria history + pallor + chronic illness pattern

**Uncertainty Note:**
Massive splenomegaly in malaria carries risk of traumatic rupture. Advise caregiver to avoid abdominal contact sports or rough handling. Refer for evaluation.

---

### 4. INTESTINAL OBSTRUCTION
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH — EMERGENCY**
Can be caused by adhesions, volvulus, Hirschsprung disease, worm bolus, or intussusception.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Acute onset distension — develops over hours
- Absolute constipation — no passage of stool or flatus
- Bile-stained vomiting (green vomit) in complete obstruction
- Visible peristaltic waves through abdominal wall
- Abdominal pain — colicky, severe
- Child rapidly deteriorates if untreated

**Differentiating Questions:**
- How quickly did the belly swell — did it happen suddenly or over days?
- Has the child passed any stool or gas since the belly became big?
- Is the child vomiting — what colour is the vomit?
- Is the child in severe pain?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Sudden distension + no passage of stool or gas + vomiting + pain = obstruction — EMERGENCY

**Uncertainty Note:**
Any child with acute abdominal distension and no bowel movements must be treated as intestinal obstruction until proven otherwise. Do not observe at home.

---

### 5. NEPHROTIC SYNDROME
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH**
A common cause of oedema and abdominal swelling in Nigerian children, often secondary to malaria or other infections.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Generalised oedema — face (especially periorbital, puffy in morning), legs, abdomen (ascites)
- Frothy urine (proteinuria)
- Abdominal distension due to ascites
- No fever unless complicated by infection
- Child appears generally unwell — fatigue, pallor
- Urine output may be reduced

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is the child's face also puffy — especially in the morning when they wake up?
- Is there swelling in the legs and around the whole body, not just the belly?
- Has anyone noticed the child's urine looking foamy or frothy?
- Is the child passing less urine than usual?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Generalised oedema (face + legs + abdomen) + frothy urine + no fever = nephrotic syndrome — refer for urine testing

**Uncertainty Note:**
Nephrotic syndrome in Nigeria is frequently associated with quartan malaria (Plasmodium malariae). Both must be assessed together.

---

### 6. WILMS TUMOUR (Nephroblastoma)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH**
One of the most common abdominal tumours in children in Nigeria. Typically presents in children under 5. Outcome is better with early diagnosis.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Palpable, firm, unilateral flank mass — usually smooth and non-tender
- Often discovered incidentally by caregiver
- May cross the midline in large tumours
- Haematuria (blood in urine) in some cases
- Child may otherwise appear well initially
- Progressive abdominal distension as tumour enlarges

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Has the caregiver noticed a lump or mass inside the belly — not just general swelling?
- Is the swelling mainly on one side of the belly?
- Has there been any blood in the child's urine?
- Is the child otherwise eating and active?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Unilateral flank mass + child under 5 + otherwise reasonable clinical state = Wilms tumour until excluded — refer urgently

**Uncertainty Note:**
Any palpable abdominal mass in a child must be referred for urgent evaluation. Do not delay.

---

### 7. BURKITT LYMPHOMA
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH**
Endemic in sub-Saharan Africa including Nigeria. Strongly associated with Epstein-Barr virus and malaria co-infection. Presents as rapidly growing abdominal mass.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Rapidly growing abdominal mass — often bilateral
- Child may present with jaw swelling
- Ascites in some cases
- General systemic symptoms — fever, weight loss, night sweats
- Older children and school-age children most commonly affected
- Rapid progression over days to weeks

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Has the child's belly grown bigger very quickly — over days or a few weeks?
- Is there any swelling in the child's jaw or face?
- Is the child losing weight and having fevers?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Rapidly growing abdominal mass + jaw swelling + systemic symptoms + older child = Burkitt lymphoma until excluded — urgent referral

**Uncertainty Note:**
Burkitt lymphoma grows very rapidly. Delayed referral significantly worsens prognosis. Any rapidly growing abdominal mass must be referred urgently.

---

### 8. ASCITES (from any cause)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH**
Fluid accumulation in the abdominal cavity. Can result from severe malnutrition, liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, tuberculosis, or malignancy.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Generalised distension that is soft and fluctuant
- Flank fullness that shifts with position
- Associated with the underlying cause

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is the belly soft and fluid-filled feeling, or hard and tense?
- Is there swelling in other parts of the body as well?
- Has the child lost weight recently?
- Has the child been treated for tuberculosis?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Generalised soft distension + associated systemic illness = ascites — refer for cause evaluation

**Uncertainty Note:**
Ascites is a sign of a serious underlying condition. The cause must be identified — it cannot be managed without diagnosis.

---

## GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE

If abdominal swelling in a child cannot be clearly attributed to a benign cause, escalate to clinic if any of the following are present:
- Distension that is new and progressive
- Associated fever, pallor, or systemic illness
- Any palpable mass
- Associated oedema elsewhere in the body
- Child not growing or gaining weight normally
- No passage of stool or gas

**In northern Nigeria, severe acute malnutrition, heavy worm burden, and repeated malaria are the most common causes of abdominal distension in children. Wilms tumour and Burkitt lymphoma are disproportionately prevalent compared to high-income settings and must always be considered when a discrete mass is present.**
