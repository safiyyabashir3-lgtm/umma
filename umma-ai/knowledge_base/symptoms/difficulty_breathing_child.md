## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### Confirming the symptom
Breathing difficulty in a child is one of the most urgent presentations in paediatric care.
Caregivers in northern Nigeria may describe it in several ways without using the phrase
"difficulty breathing." They may say the child is breathing fast, the chest is moving
strangely, the child is making a noise when breathing, or the child cannot feed because
of the breathing. All of these descriptions warrant immediate assessment.

Establish the child's age immediately:
- Any breathing difficulty in a child under 2 months — this is an emergency.
  Escalate immediately. Do not proceed to disease reasoning.
- Any breathing difficulty in a child under 6 months — threshold for escalation is very low.

Distinguish from cough as the primary complaint. If the child has a cough but breathing
appears normal at rest — use cough_child.md. If breathing difficulty at rest is the
primary concern, regardless of whether cough is present, this file applies.

### Symptoms that may be confused with breathing difficulty
- Noisy breathing through a blocked nose — snuffling or snoring through nose only,
  feeding normally, chest not moving abnormally, child calm
- Fast breathing immediately after crying — resolves within a few minutes of the child
  settling; not true breathing difficulty
- Hiccups in a young infant — brief, self-resolving

### Local language and cultural context
- Matsalar numfashi — breathing difficulty / breathing problem
- Numfashi da wahala — breathing with difficulty
- Yana numfashi da sauri — breathing fast
- Kirji yana shiga ciki — chest is pulling in / chest indrawing (this is a red flag —
  caregivers who describe this should trigger immediate escalation)
- Bai iya numfashi ba — cannot breathe
- Yana yi sauti yana numfashi — making a noise when breathing (may describe stridor or wheeze)
- Bai iya sha ba saboda numfashi — cannot feed because of breathing (red flag)

Caregivers who observe chest indrawing or fast breathing are often acutely frightened.
Take their concern seriously — a caregiver who says the child cannot breathe properly
is rarely wrong about the severity.

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

Escalate immediately if any of the following are present. Do not proceed to disease reasoning.
Ordered from most dangerous to least dangerous.

- Any breathing difficulty in a child under 2 months — emergency regardless of all other findings
- Child unable to breathe — gasping, apnoeic episodes, or breathing stopping
- Lips, tongue, or fingertips turning blue or grey (central cyanosis)
- Child unconscious or very difficult to wake
- Chest indrawing — skin between ribs or below breastbone pulling inward with each breath
- Grunting with each breath out — especially in infants; sign of severe respiratory distress
- Stridor — harsh crowing or high-pitched noise when breathing in (inspiratory stridor)
- Child unable to drink, feed, or breastfeed at all due to breathing difficulty
- Breathing so laboured the child cannot complete a sentence or cry
- Nostril flaring with each breath — indicates significant breathing effort
- Child limp, exhausted, or not responding to surroundings alongside breathing difficulty
- Any breathing difficulty in a newborn under 1 month
- Breathing difficulty with convulsion
- Breathing difficulty with high fever and child looking very unwell
- Sudden onset breathing difficulty with possible foreign body inhalation —
  child who choked, was playing with small objects, or eating seeds or nuts

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

### Nigerian and Kano context
Breathing difficulty in a child under 5 in Kano is a clinical emergency until proven otherwise.
Pneumonia is the second leading cause of death in Nigerian children under 5. A child presenting
with difficulty breathing at rest has serious disease. Malaria with severe anaemia can cause
fast breathing that mimics pneumonia — both must be considered simultaneously.

Malnutrition, which affects over 60% of children under 5 in Kano, significantly worsens
respiratory illness. A malnourished child with breathing difficulty deteriorates faster and
classic signs such as fever may be absent. Indoor air pollution from cooking fires worsens
respiratory conditions. Bronchiolitis is common in infants. Foreign body inhalation is
common in the 6-month to 3-year age group and must never be missed. Cardiac causes of
breathing difficulty are underrecognised in Nigeria.

---

### DISEASE 1: PNEUMONIA — BACTERIAL OR VIRAL
Nigerian priority: HIGHEST for breathing difficulty in a child under 5

Key differentiating features:
- Fast breathing persistent at rest — the most reliable clinical sign
- Chest indrawing — ribs visible with each breath; severe pneumonia
- Grunting on expiration in infants — indicates severe disease requiring emergency care
- Cough — may be wet, dry, or minimal; absence does not exclude pneumonia
- Fever in most cases — may be absent in malnourished children
- Poor feeding due to breathing effort
- Child working hard to breathe — using neck and abdominal muscles
- Nasal flaring

Fast breathing thresholds by age (WHO IMCI):
- Child under 2 months — 60 breaths per minute or more
- Child 2 to 11 months — 50 breaths per minute or more
- Child 1 to 5 years — 40 breaths per minute or more

Differentiating questions:
- Is the child breathing faster than normal even when calm and not crying?
- Is there skin pulling in between the ribs or below the neck with each breath?
- Has the child had a cough, and how long?
- Is the child feeding or drinking much less than usual?
- Does the child have a fever?

Confirm towards pneumonia if:
- Fast breathing at rest + chest indrawing regardless of other findings
- Fast breathing at rest + fever + poor feeding
- Any grunting in an infant alongside fever and fast breathing

Uncertainty note:
- Severity of the breathing difficulty matters more than the cough
- In malnourished children fever may be absent even in severe pneumonia
- Chest indrawing of any degree in a febrile child — treat as severe pneumonia and escalate

---

### DISEASE 2: MALARIA WITH SEVERE ANAEMIA
Nigerian priority: HIGHEST alongside pneumonia — both must always be considered together

Key differentiating features:
- Fast breathing driven by severe anaemia — the low haemoglobin forces the body to breathe
  faster to compensate; this is not a primary chest problem
- Pallor — inner eyelids appearing pale pink or white instead of red; palms pale
- No chest indrawing in uncomplicated malaria anaemia — if chest indrawing present,
  consider pneumonia coexisting
- Fever with or without classic chills and sweating pattern
- Extreme weakness and pallor together
- Jaundice — yellowing of eyes in severe malaria
- Child very weak, not wanting to move or feed

Differentiating questions:
- Are the inside of the child's lower eyelids or the palms looking very pale?
- Has the child been shivering and shaking, then very hot and sweaty?
- Has the child been sleeping under a mosquito net?

Confirm towards malaria with severe anaemia if:
- Fast breathing + marked pallor + fever with no chest indrawing
- Fast breathing + pallor + weakness + poor mosquito net use

Uncertainty note:
- Malaria anaemia and pneumonia coexist very commonly — do not choose one over the other
- Pallor with any breathing difficulty — always serious, always needs hospital
- Severe malaria anaemia can only be treated at hospital — blood transfusion may be needed

---

### DISEASE 3: BRONCHIOLITIS
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM-HIGH — most important in infants under 12 months

Key differentiating features:
- Age under 12 months — bronchiolitis is predominantly a disease of infancy
- Gradual onset over 2 to 3 days — begins with runny nose then breathing worsens
- Wheeze — whistling sound on breathing out (expiratory wheeze)
- Fast breathing
- Chest indrawing as disease progresses
- Low-grade or no fever — high fever more consistent with bacterial pneumonia
- Poor feeding due to breathing difficulty
- No response to malaria treatment

Differentiating questions:
- How old is the child?
- Did the breathing difficulty start gradually, beginning with a runny nose a few days ago?
- Is there a whistling sound when the child breathes out?
- Does the child have a high fever or mild fever?

Confirm towards bronchiolitis if:
- Infant under 12 months + preceded by runny nose + progressive worsening over days
  + expiratory wheeze + low or no fever

Uncertainty note:
- Bronchiolitis and bacterial pneumonia are clinically very similar and can coexist
- Poor feeding in a bronchiolitic infant is a sign of severity — escalate
- Any infant under 3 months with wheeze and breathing difficulty — escalate immediately

---

### DISEASE 4: FOREIGN BODY INHALATION
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM — must never be missed; most common 6 months to 3 years

Key differentiating features:
- Sudden onset — child completely well, then suddenly began choking or breathing with difficulty
- No fever at onset
- Persistent breathing difficulty or cough that does not improve
- Unilateral wheeze — breathing sounds different on one side
- History of playing with small objects, seeds, groundnuts, or hard foods
- Episode of choking that appeared to resolve but breathing difficulty continues
- Child 6 months to 3 years at highest risk

Differentiating questions:
- Did the breathing difficulty start suddenly while the child was eating or playing with something?
- Was the child completely well immediately before this started?
- Has there been an episode of choking even if the child seemed to recover?

Confirm towards foreign body if:
- Sudden onset breathing difficulty with no fever + possible history of inhalation
- Child eating groundnuts, seeds, or playing with small objects just before onset

Uncertainty note:
- If foreign body is suspected at any confidence level — escalate immediately
- Partial obstruction may allow some breathing — do not be reassured by the fact
  the child can still breathe; the object may move and cause complete obstruction
- Do not attempt to remove at home

---

### DISEASE 5: SEVERE CROUP
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM

Key differentiating features:
- Stridor — harsh crowing or high-pitched noise when breathing in (inspiratory)
- Barking cough — seal-like or dog-bark sound
- Hoarse voice or cry
- Worsens at night
- Usually preceded by cold symptoms
- Low-grade or no fever
- Most common in children 6 months to 3 years
- Anxiety and agitation worsen stridor — a frightened child breathes harder

Differentiating questions:
- Is there a harsh crowing or high-pitched noise when the child breathes in?
- Does the cough have a barking or seal-like sound?
- Is the child's voice or cry hoarse?
- Are the symptoms worse at night?

Confirm towards severe croup if:
- Inspiratory stridor + barking cough + hoarse voice + nocturnal worsening

Uncertainty note:
- Stridor at rest in any child — escalate urgently regardless of suspected cause
- Mild croup with barking cough only and no stridor at rest — lower urgency but monitor closely
- Any stridor in a child under 6 months — escalate immediately
- Keep child calm — agitation worsens airway obstruction in croup

---

### DISEASE 6: CARDIAC CAUSE OF BREATHING DIFFICULTY
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM — underrecognised in Nigeria; must not be missed

Key differentiating features:
- Breathing difficulty that has been present since birth or from early infancy
- Fast breathing consistently present even without infection
- Poor feeding from birth or early infancy — tires quickly during feeds
- Poor weight gain from early infancy
- Sweating during feeds
- Recurrent chest infections
- Cyanosis — blue tinge to lips or fingertips particularly during feeds or exertion
- Heart murmur — a clinical sign not detectable at home; relevant for clinic referral
- Breathing difficulty not explained by infection or malaria

Differentiating questions:
- Has the child always breathed faster than other children — since birth or early infancy?
- Does the child sweat a lot and tire very quickly during feeds?
- Has the child had recurrent chest infections since infancy?
- Has the child ever had blueness around the mouth or fingertips?

Confirm towards cardiac cause if:
- Breathing difficulty present since birth or early infancy + poor weight gain + sweating
  during feeds + recurrent infections

Uncertainty note:
- Cardiac causes of breathing difficulty in children are often missed or attributed to
  repeated infections in the Nigerian context
- Any child with recurrent breathing difficulty since infancy without clear infection
  cause warrants hospital assessment for cardiac evaluation
- This does not require emergency escalation unless cyanosis or acute deterioration
  is present — but does need clinic referral

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE
- Any breathing difficulty in a child under 2 months — emergency, escalate immediately
- Fast breathing at rest in any child under 5 — treat as serious and escalate urgently
- Chest indrawing of any degree — escalate urgently regardless of other findings
- Malaria and pneumonia coexist frequently — always consider both
- Malnourished child with breathing difficulty — escalate sooner; classic signs may be absent
- When in doubt — escalate. Breathing difficulty in a young child is never a symptom
  to wait on.
