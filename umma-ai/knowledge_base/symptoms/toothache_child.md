# SYMPTOM FILE: Toothache — Child
**Category:** symptoms | **Subcategory:** child | **Topic:** toothache

---

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### Confirming the Symptom
- Child reports pain in a tooth, teeth, or the mouth
- Child is pointing to or holding a specific area of the mouth
- Caregiver notices child avoiding eating on one side, or refusing to eat hard foods
- Child cries during eating or when cold or hot food touches the mouth
- Visible swelling in the cheek, gum, or jaw
- Caregiver notices dark discolouration, broken, or missing tooth
- Child is drooling more than usual due to avoiding swallowing

### Commonly Confused Symptoms
- **Teething** — infants 4–24 months; drooling, gum swelling, mild fussiness; no tooth present yet or tooth just erupting; low-grade fussiness; no systemic illness
- **Ear pain** — pain referred to jaw or cheek from otitis media; ask child to point exactly to pain; pressing the ear causes pain
- **Jaw or facial pain from lymph node swelling** — tender submandibular or cervical nodes from throat or dental infection
- **Mouth ulcers (aphthous or herpetic)** — painful ulcers visible on mucosa; not related to a specific tooth
- **Sinus pain** — upper tooth pain can mimic sinusitis; pain in cheek and multiple upper teeth

### Critical Immediate Checks
- Is there visible facial or jaw swelling?
- Is the swelling spreading to the neck or under the chin?
- Is there fever alongside the tooth pain?
- Is the child able to open the mouth — or is there trismus (inability to open mouth)?
- Is the child able to swallow?
- Is there any difficulty breathing?

---

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

*Escalate immediately if any of the following are present:*

1. Swelling spreading to the floor of the mouth, neck, or under the chin — Ludwig's angina, EMERGENCY airway threat
2. Difficulty breathing or swallowing alongside dental pain and facial swelling — EMERGENCY
3. Trismus — child unable to open mouth — indicates deep space infection, EMERGENCY
4. Facial swelling with high fever and child appearing toxic — dental abscess with systemic spread
5. Eye swelling or proptosis from upper tooth infection spreading to orbit
6. Child with dental pain who cannot open mouth to eat or drink at all — dehydration risk
7. Dental infection in a child with known sickle cell disease — can trigger a crisis
8. Rapidly spreading facial swelling — cellulitis from dental origin

---

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

---

### 1. DENTAL CARIES (Tooth Decay) WITH PULPITIS
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
Dental caries is the most prevalent dental disease in Nigeria. Advanced caries causes pulpitis — inflammation of the tooth nerve — which is the most common cause of toothache in children.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Sharp or throbbing pain in a specific tooth
- Pain worse with sweet, cold, or hot food and drinks
- Visible dark spot, cavity, or broken tooth on inspection
- Pain may be constant (irreversible pulpitis) or triggered only by stimulus (reversible pulpitis)
- Child may point to one tooth clearly
- History of previous dental pain or untreated cavities
- High sugar or sweet drink intake common in the history

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Can the child point to which tooth hurts?
- Is there a dark spot or hole in the tooth?
- Does the pain come on with eating or drinking something cold or sweet?
- Is the pain there all the time, or only when eating?
- Has the child had this tooth pain before?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Localised tooth pain + visible cavity + triggered by hot/cold/sweet + no facial swelling

**Uncertainty Note:**
Irreversible pulpitis (constant pain, not just triggered) will progress to abscess without treatment. Advise dental assessment; paracetamol for pain relief in the interim.

---

### 2. DENTAL ABSCESS
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH — URGENT**
Untreated caries progresses to pulp death and then periapical abscess. Very common in Nigeria due to lack of dental care.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Throbbing, severe, constant pain
- Facial or cheek swelling on the side of the affected tooth
- Fever present when infection is active
- Gum swelling — a gumboil (parulis) may be visible
- Child may report bad taste in mouth if abscess has drained spontaneously
- Tooth may be discoloured (grey or black) if pulp has died

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is there swelling on the child's cheek or face?
- Is the pain constant and throbbing, not just during eating?
- Does the child have a fever?
- Has the child noticed a bad taste in the mouth?
- Is there a lump or pus-filled spot on the gum near the painful tooth?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Constant throbbing pain + facial swelling + fever + visible gumboil = dental abscess — refer for treatment

**Uncertainty Note:**
A dental abscess with facial swelling and fever must be seen at a clinic for antibiotics and drainage. Paracetamol for pain only while arranging referral.

---

### 3. PERICORONITIS (Gum Infection Around Erupting Tooth)
**Nigerian Priority: MEDIUM-HIGH**
Common in older children as permanent molars erupt. The gum flap overlying a partially erupted tooth becomes infected.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Pain and swelling at the back of the mouth — around the last erupting molar
- Gum flap visible over a partially erupted tooth
- Difficulty opening the mouth (trismus) in severe cases
- Bad breath and bad taste
- Swelling of the jaw or cheek on the affected side
- Fever in moderate or severe cases

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is the pain at the very back of the mouth?
- Is there a new tooth just starting to come through at the back?
- Is the child finding it hard to open the mouth fully?
- Is there bad breath or a bad taste?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Pain at back of mouth + partially erupted molar + swollen gum flap + trismus

**Uncertainty Note:**
Pericoronitis with trismus indicates significant infection. Refer for assessment — antibiotics and irrigation are usually needed.

---

### 4. HERPETIC GINGIVOSTOMATITIS
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH**
Primary herpes simplex infection of the mouth. Common in children aged 1–5 years. Frequently mistaken for toothache or teething.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Multiple painful ulcers throughout the mouth — lips, tongue, gums, palate
- Gums are bright red, swollen, and bleed easily
- High fever — can be 39–40 degrees
- Child refuses to eat or drink due to pain
- Excessive drooling
- Submandibular lymphadenopathy
- Resolves in 10–14 days in immunocompetent children

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Are there sores or ulcers all over the inside of the mouth — not just one tooth?
- Does the child have a high fever alongside the mouth pain?
- Is the child drooling excessively?
- Is the child refusing to drink anything?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Multiple oral ulcers + very sore bleeding gums + high fever + drooling + child under 5

**Uncertainty Note:**
If child is refusing all fluids due to mouth pain, dehydration is a serious risk. Refer if child cannot be kept hydrated at home.

---

### 5. DENTAL TRAUMA (Cracked or Injured Tooth)
**Nigerian Priority: MEDIUM-HIGH**
Falls, accidents, and fights are common causes of dental injury in children.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Acute onset after a fall, blow, or accident
- Visible chipped, cracked, displaced, or missing tooth
- Pain on biting or touching the tooth
- Possible bleeding from gum at site of injury
- Avulsed (completely knocked out) permanent tooth — a dental emergency

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Did the child have a fall or hit to the mouth?
- Is the tooth chipped, cracked, or has it been knocked out?
- Was the tooth a baby tooth or an adult tooth?
- Is there bleeding from the gum?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Trauma history + visible tooth damage + acute onset pain = dental trauma

**Uncertainty Note:**
A knocked-out permanent tooth can be reimplanted if the child reaches a dentist within 30–60 minutes. Keep the tooth moist (in milk or saliva, not water) and refer urgently.

---

### 6. NOMA (Cancrum Oris)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH — EMERGENCY**
Rapidly progressive gangrenous infection of the mouth and face. A disease of severe malnutrition and immune compromise. Nigeria has among the highest global burden. Fatal if untreated.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Starts as a small gum ulcer that rapidly progresses
- Destruction of facial tissue — cheek, lips, and bone — visibly necrotic, foul-smelling
- Child is severely malnourished and often immunocompromised
- Child has a history of recent severe illness (measles, malaria, severe malnutrition)
- Child appears very systemically unwell

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is there a wound or sore on the face or inside the mouth that is growing quickly?
- Is the child severely malnourished?
- Has the child recently had measles or severe malnutrition?
- Is there a bad smell coming from the mouth or face?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Facial necrotic lesion + severe malnutrition + recent severe illness + very unwell child = noma — EMERGENCY REFERRAL

**Uncertainty Note:**
Noma is rapidly fatal without hospital treatment. Any suspected noma must be referred immediately regardless of other findings.

---

## GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE

Toothache in a child that is accompanied by any of the following must be escalated to clinic:
- Facial or cheek swelling
- Fever
- Difficulty opening the mouth
- Difficulty swallowing
- Child unable to eat or drink at all

**In northern Nigeria, dental caries is highly prevalent and dental access is very limited. Many dental abscesses present late and at an advanced stage. Paracetamol in appropriate dose is safe for pain relief while seeking care. Noma, though uncommon, is a specific regional emergency linked to severe malnutrition and must be identified promptly.**
