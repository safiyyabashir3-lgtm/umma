# SYMPTOM FILE: Eye Discharge / Eye Redness — Child
**Category:** symptoms | **Subcategory:** child | **Topic:** eye_discharge

---

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### Confirming the Symptom
- Caregiver reports sticky, watery, or purulent discharge from one or both eyes
- Eye or eyes appear red or pink — sclera visibly injected
- Child rubbing eyes frequently
- Eyelids stuck together on waking (matted lids)
- Eye appears swollen — either the conjunctiva (chemosis) or the eyelids themselves
- Child reports pain, burning, or gritty feeling in eye (if old enough to communicate)
- Reduced vision or light sensitivity reported by caregiver

### Commonly Confused Symptoms
- **Normal newborn eye discharge** — small amount of clear discharge in first weeks of life can be nasolacrimal duct obstruction, not infection; distinguish by character and age
- **Allergic conjunctivitis** — bilateral, intense itch, watery discharge, no fever, seasonal or exposure-related
- **Periorbital cellulitis** — redness and swelling of the skin AROUND the eye, not the eye itself; distinguish carefully
- **Orbital cellulitis** — eye appears pushed forward; extremely serious
- **Corneal foreign body** — unilateral, acute onset, pain, tearing; ask about recent play or exposure
- **Measles-related eye involvement** — redness and discharge as part of a systemic febrile illness with rash

### Critical Immediate Checks
- Is the EYELID swollen (periorbital) or is the eye itself red?
- Is the eyeball protruding (proptosis)?
- Is vision affected — is the child squinting, avoiding light, not tracking normally?
- Is there fever alongside the eye symptom?
- Age — newborn with eye discharge requires urgent assessment
- Is this part of a febrile illness with rash?

---

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

*Escalate immediately if any of the following are present:*

1. Proptosis — eye appears pushed forward or bulging — orbital cellulitis, EMERGENCY
2. Eyelid swollen shut with fever and the child appears toxic — periorbital or orbital cellulitis
3. Newborn under 28 days with purulent eye discharge — neonatal ophthalmia, EMERGENCY
4. Child with reduced vision or complaining of not being able to see clearly
5. Severe eye pain — child unable to open eye, photophobia
6. Eye discharge as part of febrile illness with rash — possible measles, requires urgent assessment
7. Eye discharge with stiff neck or altered consciousness — meningitis flag
8. Chemical splash or injury to eye — rinse immediately, refer urgently
9. Corneal opacity visible — white or cloudy area on eye surface
10. Child appears systemically unwell — lethargic, febrile, not feeding
11. Caregiver is very worried or says child is the sickest they have ever seen

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

---

### 1. BACTERIAL CONJUNCTIVITIS
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
Most common cause of purulent eye discharge in children in Nigeria. Both Haemophilus influenzae and Streptococcus pneumoniae are common causative organisms.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Purulent or mucopurulent discharge — yellow or green
- Eyelids matted shut in the morning
- Conjunctival redness, usually bilateral (though may start unilateral)
- Minimal or no pain — gritty discomfort more than pain
- No significant visual loss
- No fever typically, or low-grade fever only
- Can spread rapidly between children in household or school

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is the discharge yellow or green, or is it clear and watery?
- Are both eyes affected or just one?
- Are the eyelids stuck together in the morning?
- Does the child have pain in the eye or just irritation?
- Are other children in the household also affected?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Purulent discharge + matted lids + bilateral + no fever + no vision change + no lid swelling

**Uncertainty Note:**
Unilateral purulent discharge in a neonate must be treated as gonococcal or chlamydial ophthalmia until proven otherwise — refer urgently.

---

### 2. NEONATAL OPHTHALMIA (Ophthalmia Neonatorum)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST — EMERGENCY**
Occurs in newborns under 28 days. Caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae or Chlamydia trachomatis acquired during delivery. Can cause blindness if untreated.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Onset within first 2–5 days (gonococcal) or 5–14 days (chlamydial) of life
- Profuse purulent discharge — may be copious and rapidly reaccumulating
- Severe eyelid swelling (chemosis)
- Gonococcal: extremely copious, rapid onset, risk of corneal perforation
- Chlamydial: less copious, may have associated pneumonia

**Differentiating Questions:**
- How old is the baby exactly?
- When did the discharge start — how many days after birth?
- Is the discharge large in amount or small?
- Were there any problems during the delivery?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Newborn under 28 days + purulent discharge + lid swelling = ophthalmia neonatorum until excluded — refer immediately

**Uncertainty Note:**
Any purulent eye discharge in a neonate must be treated as ophthalmia neonatorum. Do not observe at home.

---

### 3. VIRAL CONJUNCTIVITIS
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH**
Commonly associated with adenovirus. Highly contagious. Often accompanies or follows a viral URTI.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Watery or mucoid discharge — not thick pus
- Intense redness, often starts unilateral, becomes bilateral
- Significant itch and gritty sensation
- Often associated with recent cold, sore throat, or fever
- Preauricular lymph node may be palpable (anterior to ear)
- Does not respond to antibiotics
- Highly contagious in schools and households

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is the discharge watery and clear, or thick and yellow?
- Did the child have a cold or sore throat before the eye became red?
- Is the eye intensely itchy?
- Have other children in the family or school had similar symptoms?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Watery discharge + itching + recent URTI + preauricular node + bilateral spread = viral conjunctivitis

**Uncertainty Note:**
Viral and bacterial conjunctivitis cannot be reliably distinguished without examination. If fever is present, consider measles.

---

### 4. MEASLES (eye involvement)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
Measles remains endemic in northern Nigeria with recurrent outbreaks. Conjunctivitis is one of the earliest signs of measles and precedes the rash. Eye involvement in measles can lead to corneal ulceration and blindness — a major cause of childhood blindness in Nigeria.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Conjunctivitis as part of a febrile prodrome — coryza, cough, red eyes (the 3 Cs)
- Koplik spots may be visible (white spots on inner cheeks) before rash appears
- Rash appears after 3–4 days of fever — starts on face, spreads downward
- Child appears systemically unwell — toxic, high fever
- Vitamin A deficiency increases risk of corneal involvement and blindness

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Does the child have a rash anywhere on the body?
- Has the child had fever, cough, and runny nose together with the eye redness?
- Has the child been vaccinated — received measles vaccine?
- Are there any white spots visible inside the mouth?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Fever + cough + coryza + conjunctivitis + rash (face to body spread) = measles until excluded

**Uncertainty Note:**
Measles with eye involvement is a notifiable disease and a public health emergency. Refer for assessment. Do not observe at home.

---

### 5. ALLERGIC CONJUNCTIVITIS
**Nigerian Priority: MEDIUM-HIGH**
Common in Nigeria, particularly in dusty environments in the dry (harmattan) season.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Intense bilateral itch — the dominant symptom
- Watery discharge — clear, not purulent
- Bilateral and symmetrical
- Associated with other atopic features (asthma, eczema, nasal itch)
- Worse in harmattan season or after dust exposure
- No fever
- Recurrent — child has had similar episodes before

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is the main symptom itch rather than pain?
- Is it worse during the dry season or when it is dusty?
- Does the child have asthma or eczema as well?
- Has this happened before in the same way?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Intense bilateral itch + clear watery discharge + seasonal or dust-related + recurrent + no fever

**Uncertainty Note:**
Allergic conjunctivitis does not cause fever. If fever is present, re-evaluate for infectious cause.

---

### 6. PERIORBITAL CELLULITIS (Pre-septal Cellulitis)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH — URGENT**
Infection of the soft tissue AROUND the eye (eyelids and periorbital skin), anterior to the orbital septum. Distinct from orbital cellulitis but requires urgent treatment.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Eyelid is swollen, red, warm, and tender
- Eye itself (eyeball movement, vision) is typically normal
- Usually unilateral
- Associated fever, child appears unwell
- Often follows skin injury, insect bite, or sinusitis
- Eye is not proptotic (not pushed forward)

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is it the eyelid that is swollen, or the eye itself that is red?
- Can the child move the eye in all directions without pain?
- Is the child's vision normal?
- Was there a bite, scratch, or skin break around the eye recently?
- Does the child have fever?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Unilateral eyelid swelling + fever + normal eye movement + no proptosis

**Uncertainty Note:**
Periorbital and orbital cellulitis can be impossible to distinguish without clinical assessment. Any periorbital swelling with fever must be seen urgently — do not observe at home.

---

### 7. ORBITAL CELLULITIS
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH — EMERGENCY**
Infection posterior to the orbital septum. Can cause blindness and intracranial spread (meningitis, brain abscess).

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Proptosis — eye is pushed forward
- Painful or restricted eye movement (ophthalmoplegia)
- Child appears very unwell, high fever
- Visual changes
- Often follows sinusitis or facial skin infection

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is the eye being pushed forward or appearing to stick out?
- Can the child move the eye — does it hurt to move the eye?
- Is the child's vision affected?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Proptosis + restricted eye movement + fever + pain = orbital cellulitis — EMERGENCY REFERRAL

**Uncertainty Note:**
Any proptosis in a febrile child is an emergency. Do not wait.

---

### 8. TRACHOMA
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH — CHRONIC**
Chlamydia trachomatis infection. A leading cause of preventable blindness in northern Nigeria. Often in communities with poor water access and hygiene.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Chronic or recurrent red eyes with discharge
- Follicles visible on inner surface of upper eyelid (requires examination)
- Community-wide prevalence — multiple household members affected
- Scarring leads to trichiasis (inturned lashes) and corneal damage over time
- Associated with poor water and sanitation access
- Common in rural northern Nigeria

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Has the child had recurring or long-standing eye problems?
- Are other people in the household or community also affected?
- Is there access to clean water at home?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Chronic recurrent discharge + community prevalence + poor WASH access + northern rural setting

**Uncertainty Note:**
Active trachoma requires treatment of the whole household, not just the child. Refer for assessment and community management.

---

## GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE

If eye redness or discharge in a child cannot be clearly attributed to a benign cause, or if any of the following are present, escalate to clinic:
- Fever alongside the eye symptom
- Eyelid swelling (not just eye redness)
- Newborn or infant under 3 months
- Visual symptoms or photophobia
- Unilateral discharge that is copious or rapidly worsening
- Part of a systemic febrile illness

**In northern Nigeria, measles and trachoma are endemic and must always be considered in children with eye symptoms. Eye involvement in measles can lead to blindness within days if vitamin A deficiency is present.**
