# SYMPTOM FILE: Ear Pain / Ear Discharge — Child
**Category:** symptoms | **Subcategory:** child | **Topic:** ear_pain

---

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### Confirming the Symptom
- Child is pulling, tugging, or hitting one or both ears repeatedly
- Child cries or cries louder when lying flat or during feeding (increased pressure)
- Caregiver reports visible discharge or fluid from the ear canal
- Child flinches or cries when ear is touched or pressed on the tragus
- Child reports pain in ear (if old enough to communicate)
- Reduced hearing noticed by caregiver — child not responding to sounds normally
- In infants: unexplained irritability, poor feeding, disturbed sleep with no obvious other cause

### Commonly Confused Symptoms
- **Teething pain** — can cause ear rubbing in infants under 12 months; no fever typically, no discharge, gum swelling present
- **Foreign body in ear** — may cause pain and discharge; ask directly if child inserted something
- **Scalp or neck pain** — child may point vaguely toward side of head; clarify exact location
- **Insect in ear canal** — acute onset, intense distress, crawling sensation described; more common in Nigeria
- **Jaw or dental pain** — pain worse with chewing; no discharge

### Critical Immediate Checks
- Age of child — infant under 3 months with any fever alongside ear symptoms is an EMERGENCY
- Is there discharge? If yes: colour, smell, duration
- Is the child toxic-looking — lethargic, not responding normally, high fever?
- Is there swelling or redness BEHIND the ear (mastoid area)?

---

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

*Escalate immediately if any of the following are present:*

1. Child under 3 months with fever and any ear symptom — EMERGENCY regardless of severity
2. Swelling, redness, or tenderness behind the ear (mastoid swelling) — suggests mastoiditis, a surgical emergency
3. Child appears toxic: lethargic, limp, unresponsive, glazed eyes, cannot be roused
4. Ear discharge that is bloody or bloodstained
5. Stiff neck alongside ear pain and fever — possible meningitis
6. Bulging fontanelle in infant — sign of raised intracranial pressure
7. Seizures in the same illness episode
8. Facial asymmetry or drooping — possible facial nerve involvement
9. Ear discharge with high fever for more than 3 days with no improvement
10. Caregiver reports child is the sickest they have ever seen
11. Child completely refusing fluids and unable to swallow
12. Ear discharge that is foul-smelling and thick (especially chronic) — may indicate cholesteatoma or serious chronic infection

---

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

---

### 1. ACUTE OTITIS MEDIA (AOM)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
Most common cause of ear pain with fever in children in Nigeria. Frequently follows an upper respiratory infection.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Preceded by coryza, nasal congestion, or cough in days before
- Fever present, often 38°C or above
- Ear pain typically starts suddenly, worse at night and when lying down
- No visible discharge unless tympanic membrane has perforated
- If perforated: sudden relief of pain followed by ear discharge (often mucopurulent)
- Bilateral in younger children more often than older children

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Did the child have a cold or blocked nose before the ear pain started?
- Has anything come out of the ear — fluid, pus, blood?
- Did the pain suddenly get better after discharge started?
- Is the pain worse when the child lies down?
- Has the child had ear infections before?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Recent URTI + sudden ear pain + fever + night-waking + relief after discharge begins

**Uncertainty Note:**
If fever is very high (39°C+) and child appears unwell beyond expected, consider concurrent malaria — both can coexist. Do not assume AOM explains all symptoms.

---

### 2. MALARIA (co-existing or primary cause of fever)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
Malaria must always be considered when fever is present. AOM and malaria frequently coexist in Nigerian children.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Fever may be high, intermittent, or cyclical
- Child may have general malaise, poor appetite, vomiting, rigors
- Ear pain may be secondary — fever is the primary driver
- Splenomegaly if recurrent or chronic malaria
- Pallor — anaemia common

**Differentiating Questions:**
- How high has the fever been and how long has it lasted?
- Has the child been vomiting alongside the ear pain?
- Is the child pale or looking washed out?
- Has the child had malaria before?
- Is the child sleeping under a mosquito net?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Fever dominant + pallor + vomiting + area with known malaria transmission + no obvious ear discharge

**Uncertainty Note:**
When both AOM and malaria are plausible, treat as both present until proven otherwise. Do not allow ear pain to distract from systemic features of malaria.

---

### 3. OTITIS EXTERNA (Swimmer's Ear / External Canal Infection)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH**
Common in Nigeria given climate and water exposure. Can be caused by bacteria or fungi.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Pain is worse when the outer ear (pinna) is pulled or the tragus pressed
- Ear canal appears swollen or has discharge
- Usually no fever, or low-grade fever only
- History of water in ear — swimming, bathing, pouring water into ear (traditional cleaning)
- History of ear canal cleaning with sticks, cotton buds, keys — traumatic entry
- Itch may precede pain

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Does it hurt more when you pull the ear or press in front of the ear opening?
- Has the child been swimming or had water in the ear recently?
- Has anyone been cleaning the ear with a stick or cotton bud?
- Is there itching in the ear as well as pain?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Ear canal tenderness on traction + water exposure or cleaning history + no fever + no URTI history

**Uncertainty Note:**
If fever is present with otitis externa features, do not exclude AOM — both can coexist.

---

### 4. CHRONIC SUPPURATIVE OTITIS MEDIA (CSOM)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH**
Highly prevalent in northern Nigeria due to high rates of untreated or undertreated AOM, poverty, and limited access to care.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Ear discharge present for 2 weeks or longer (by definition)
- Discharge typically mucopurulent, may be foul-smelling
- Usually painless or mildly painful — pain suggests secondary infection or complication
- Hearing loss often present, may be significant
- No fever unless complicated
- History of recurrent ear infections or previous ear discharge

**Differentiating Questions:**
- How long has the ear been discharging?
- Is there a bad smell from the ear?
- Has the child had problems with hearing — not responding to name, turning TV up loud?
- Has the child had ear problems before, or ear infections that were not fully treated?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Discharge for 2+ weeks + foul smell + hearing loss + no fever + history of recurrent ear infections

**Uncertainty Note:**
Foul-smelling chronic discharge with hearing loss must raise concern for cholesteatoma — this requires ENT assessment and cannot be managed with antibiotics alone.

---

### 5. MASTOIDITIS
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH — URGENT/EMERGENCY**
A serious complication of AOM. More common in Nigeria due to delayed treatment of AOM.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Swelling, redness, and tenderness over the mastoid bone (behind and below the ear)
- Ear may be pushed forward and downward by mastoid swelling
- High fever, toxic appearance
- Preceded by treated or untreated AOM in days prior
- Severe pain

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is there swelling or a lump behind the ear?
- Is the ear sticking out more than usual?
- Was the child treated for an ear infection recently but is getting worse?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Postauricular swelling + displaced pinna + fever + recent AOM = mastoiditis until proven otherwise — refer urgently

**Uncertainty Note:**
Any postauricular swelling must be treated as mastoiditis until excluded by clinical assessment. Do not observe at home.

---

### 6. FOREIGN BODY IN EAR CANAL
**Nigerian Priority: MEDIUM-HIGH**
Common in young children, especially ages 1–5. May cause pain, discharge, or be asymptomatic.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Acute onset ear discomfort or pain, often witnessed by caregiver
- Child may report something in ear
- Discharge if object has been present for days and caused infection
- Insect in ear causes extreme acute distress — buzzing, crawling sensation described
- No fever unless secondary infection

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Did the child put anything in their ear, or did anyone see them do so?
- Did the pain start very suddenly?
- Is the child saying something is moving or buzzing inside the ear?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Acute onset + witnessed or suspected insertion + intense distress disproportionate to examination

**Uncertainty Note:**
Do not attempt blind removal without visualisation. Secondary infection can occur if object remains — refer if uncertain.

---

### 7. MENINGITIS (ear pain as part of systemic illness)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH — EMERGENCY if suspected**
Bacterial meningitis remains a significant cause of child mortality in northern Nigeria, including meningococcal meningitis.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Ear pain is NOT the primary symptom — systemic illness dominates
- Stiff neck, photophobia, high fever
- Altered consciousness, seizures, bulging fontanelle in infants
- Rash (non-blanching) in meningococcal disease — less common but important
- Neck stiffness may be subtle in infants — look for bulging fontanelle, high-pitched cry, opisthotonus

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is the child's neck stiff — unable to put chin on chest?
- Is the child sensitive to light?
- Has the child had any seizures?
- Is the child awake and responding normally?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Fever + stiff neck + altered consciousness + ear symptoms = treat as meningitis, refer immediately

**Uncertainty Note:**
If meningitis cannot be excluded, refer to hospital immediately. This is not a condition to observe at home.

---

## GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE

If ear pain or discharge in a child is accompanied by ANY of the following and the cause cannot be clearly identified from the conversation, escalate to clinic or hospital:
- Fever lasting more than 48 hours
- Child appearing unwell beyond the ear symptom
- Any sign of systemic illness (pallor, lethargy, poor feeding, vomiting)
- Discharge present for more than 2 days without improvement
- Infant under 12 months — lower threshold for escalation always applies

**In northern Nigeria, malaria and AOM frequently coexist. Never use the presence of ear pain to exclude malaria when fever is present.**
