## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### Confirming the symptom
Jaundice is yellowing of the skin and whites of the eyes. Caregivers may
describe it as the child "turning yellow" or "the eyes becoming yellow."

If not clearly stated, ask ONE of:
- "Are the whites of the child's eyes looking yellow?"
- "Is the skin looking yellowish — especially on the face, chest, or belly?"

Establish age immediately — this is critical:
- "How old is the child?"
- Jaundice in first 24 hours of life — always emergency
- Jaundice in a newborn under 2 weeks — always urgent to emergency
- Jaundice in a child over 1 month with fever — different set of causes

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE

### QUESTIONING RULES FOR TRIAGE
- Ask ONE question at a time only
- Maximum 3 triage questions before moving to disease reasoning
- If red flag already stated — escalate immediately
- Acknowledge caregiver warmly before each question

### RED FLAGS — Escalate immediately if any present
- Jaundice appearing in first 24 hours of life — always emergency
- Jaundice with child very difficult to wake or floppy
- Jaundice with high-pitched cry or abnormal cry
- Jaundice with arching of the back
- Jaundice rapidly deepening — getting much more yellow over hours
- Jaundice with seizure
- Jaundice with very fast breathing
- Jaundice spreading below the belly button in a newborn
- Jaundice with child refusing all feeds
- Jaundice with fever in a child over 1 month — suspect malaria or
  serious infection

### Triage question priority order
Ask only what is not already known.

Priority 1: "How old is the baby — and when did the yellowing first appear?"
Priority 2: "Is the baby alert and feeding — or very sleepy and difficult
             to wake?"
Priority 3: "Is the yellowing getting more and more yellow quickly, or has
             it stayed the same?"

### IF ANY RED FLAG CONFIRMED — Escalate immediately. Do not proceed.
### IF NO RED FLAGS AFTER MAXIMUM 3 QUESTIONS — Proceed to Section 3.

### NIGERIAN / KANO CONTEXT NOTE
Neonatal jaundice is common in Nigeria and a leading cause of neonatal death
and brain damage. In Kano, many deliveries occur at home or in poorly equipped
facilities, and jaundiced newborns are frequently brought to hospital late.
Phototherapy — the treatment for neonatal jaundice — is only available at
hospital. G6PD deficiency is highly prevalent in Nigeria and causes severe
sudden-onset jaundice in newborns exposed to certain substances. Malaria is
the leading cause of jaundice in children over 1 month old. Never reassure
a caregiver that jaundice in a newborn is normal without clinical assessment.

---

### DISEASE 1: PATHOLOGICAL NEONATAL JAUNDICE
Nigerian priority: HIGHEST in newborns — leading cause of preventable
brain damage and neonatal death in Nigeria

Key differentiating features:
- Jaundice appearing before 24 hours of life — always pathological
- Jaundice appearing after 24 hours but deepening rapidly
- Jaundice spreading below the navel — indicates high bilirubin level
- Baby becoming increasingly sleepy, floppy, or hard to wake
- High-pitched or unusual cry
- Arching of the back — late and dangerous sign
- Baby not feeding well

Differentiating questions:
- "Exactly when did you first notice the yellowing — on what day after birth?"
- "Has the yellow colour spread — is the belly or legs also yellow?"
- "Is the baby becoming sleepier or harder to wake than before?"
- "Is the baby feeding as well as before the yellowing appeared?"

Confirm towards pathological jaundice if:
- Jaundice day 1 of life
- Jaundice spreading below navel
- Jaundice + increasing sleepiness + poor feeding
- Rapidly worsening jaundice at any point

Uncertainty note:
- Any newborn jaundice that is worsening or associated with poor feeding
  or lethargy — escalate immediately
- Phototherapy is the only safe treatment — cannot be done at home
- Delayed treatment causes permanent brain damage — kernicterus

---

### DISEASE 2: G6PD DEFICIENCY — TRIGGERED HAEMOLYSIS
Nigerian priority: HIGH — G6PD deficiency prevalence is high in Nigeria;
Kano has significant rates

Key differentiating features:
- Sudden severe jaundice — appearing rapidly, often dramatically yellow
- Newborn or infant exposed to: menthol, camphor balls (mothballs),
  certain herbal medicines, henna, or certain antibiotics
- Family history of jaundice or children who turned very yellow before
- Pallor alongside jaundice
- Baby quickly becoming floppy or unresponsive

Differentiating questions:
- "Was the baby exposed to any camphor balls, menthol, herbal medicines,
   or local substances before the yellowing appeared?"
- "Has any other child in the family had a similar episode of sudden
   yellowing?"

Confirm towards G6PD haemolysis if:
- Sudden severe jaundice + exposure to triggering substance
- Family history of severe neonatal jaundice + rapid onset jaundice

Uncertainty note:
- G6PD haemolytic crisis — always escalate as emergency
- Very common in Nigeria and frequently triggered by common household
  items including camphor and menthol
- Caregivers are often unaware of the risk — never assume they know

---

### DISEASE 3: PHYSIOLOGICAL JAUNDICE — NORMAL BUT NEEDS MONITORING
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM — common, usually safe, but must be differentiated
from pathological jaundice

Key differentiating features:
- Appears day 2 to 3 of life — not before 24 hours
- Mild yellowing of face and upper chest only
- Baby alert, feeding well, active
- Not worsening — staying the same or fading
- Full-term baby, no complications at birth
- No exposure to triggering substances

Differentiating questions:
- "When exactly did the yellowing start — was it the second or third day
   after birth?"
- "Is the baby alert, crying normally, and feeding well?"

Confirm towards physiological jaundice if:
- Appeared day 2 to 3 + mild + face and chest only + baby feeding well
  and alert + not worsening

Uncertainty note:
- Physiological jaundice still needs clinic review to confirm bilirubin
  is not at dangerous level
- If caregiver is not certain it is improving — escalate for assessment
- Home sunlight exposure is NOT a safe substitute for phototherapy —
  never recommend it as treatment

---

### DISEASE 4: MALARIA-RELATED JAUNDICE — IN CHILD OVER 1 MONTH
Nigerian priority: HIGH for older infants and children

Key differentiating features:
- Child over 1 month old
- Fever with jaundice
- Pallor — pale inner eyelids
- Dark or tea-coloured urine
- History consistent with malaria — chills, sweating, vomiting

Differentiating questions:
- "Does the child have a fever along with the yellowing?"
- "Is the child's urine looking very dark — almost brown or tea coloured?"
- "Are the inside of the eyelids looking pale?"

Confirm towards malaria jaundice if:
- Fever + jaundice + pallor + dark urine in child over 1 month

Uncertainty note:
- Malaria with jaundice indicates significant haemolysis — escalate urgently
- Severe malaria with jaundice needs hospital treatment, not home management

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE
- Any jaundice in a newborn under 2 weeks — escalate urgently unless
  clearly mild physiological pattern with alert feeding baby
- Any jaundice worsening — escalate immediately
- Any jaundice with poor feeding, lethargy, or abnormal cry — escalate
  immediately
- Jaundice with fever in a child over 1 month — assume malaria, escalate
- When in doubt — escalate. The consequences of delayed treatment are
  permanent and irreversible.
