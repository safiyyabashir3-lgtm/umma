# UMMA Symptom File: Vaginal Bleeding in Pregnancy

---

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

**Confirm the symptom:**
- Ask whether she is seeing blood from the vagina — confirm it is from the vagina and not from urine or rectum
- Ask how much blood: spotting (a few drops), light bleeding, heavy bleeding, or clots
- Ask gestational age — the differential diagnosis changes significantly by trimester
- Ask whether the bleeding is associated with pain or is painless — this is a critical differentiating question
- Ask whether she has passed any tissue

**Commonly confused with:**
- Bloody urine (haematuria) — ask whether the blood is seen in the toilet water or on wiping from the vaginal area specifically
- Blood from haemorrhoids — common in pregnancy; ask about rectal pain and whether blood is seen on the toilet paper or mixed with stool
- Normal implantation spotting — only relevant in very early pregnancy (around 6–8 weeks), very light, resolves within 1–2 days

**Critical immediate checks:**
- Ask immediately: how heavy is the bleeding — is she soaking pads or passing clots?
- Ask: does she have abdominal pain alongside the bleeding?
- Ask: is the baby moving (if she is in second or third trimester)?
- Any heavy bleeding in pregnancy is an emergency regardless of cause

---

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

Escalate immediately if any of the following are present:

1. Heavy vaginal bleeding at any stage of pregnancy — soaking a pad in one hour or passing large clots
2. Any vaginal bleeding with severe abdominal pain — placental abruption or ectopic rupture
3. Any vaginal bleeding with signs of shock: dizziness, fainting, rapid breathing, pallor, cold sweat
4. Vaginal bleeding after 28 weeks — any amount, always escalate (placenta praevia or abruption)
5. Vaginal bleeding with absent or reduced fetal movement
6. Vaginal bleeding with fever — infected miscarriage or chorioamnionitis
7. Painless heavy bleeding in second or third trimester — placenta praevia emergency
8. Vaginal bleeding with severe one-sided abdominal pain in early pregnancy — ectopic pregnancy until excluded
9. Passage of tissue or products of conception
10. Bleeding in a woman who has had a previous caesarean section — uterine rupture risk
11. Bleeding in a woman with a known low-lying placenta
12. Any bleeding in a woman who has had more than 4 previous deliveries (grand multipara — uterine atony and placenta praevia risk)
13. Woman or family is very frightened or reports bleeding is more than normal

---

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

---

### DISEASE 1: PLACENTA PRAEVIA
**Nigerian priority level: HIGHEST**

Placenta praevia (low-lying placenta covering or near the cervical opening) is a life-threatening cause of bleeding in the second and third trimester. It causes painless, often heavy bleeding. Emergency delivery may be required.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Painless bright red vaginal bleeding — this is the hallmark
- Occurs in second or third trimester, typically after 28 weeks
- Bleeding may stop and restart
- Known low-lying placenta on previous ultrasound increases risk
- Grand multiparity is a risk factor
- No abdominal pain or tenderness

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is there any pain accompanying the bleeding, or is it completely painless?
- How many pregnancies and deliveries has she had before?
- Has she had an ultrasound this pregnancy — was she told the placenta was low?
- Has this bleeding happened before and stopped?
- Is the blood bright red?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Painless bright red bleeding after 28 weeks
- Known low-lying placenta
- Grand multiparity
- Recurrent episodes of painless bleeding
- No abdominal pain or uterine tenderness

**Uncertainty note:**
Any painless bleeding after 28 weeks must be treated as placenta praevia and escalated immediately. Internal examination must never be performed — it can cause catastrophic haemorrhage. Escalate without delay.

---

### DISEASE 2: PLACENTAL ABRUPTION
**Nigerian priority level: HIGHEST**

Placental abruption is premature separation of the placenta from the uterine wall. It is immediately dangerous to both mother and fetus. It may present with painful or concealed bleeding.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Painful vaginal bleeding — abdominal pain is constant, severe, and may be out of proportion to visible bleeding
- Uterus may feel hard and tender (board-like rigidity)
- Fetal movements may be reduced or absent
- Bleeding may be concealed (no external blood visible despite internal haemorrhage)
- Risk factors: hypertension, trauma to abdomen, previous abruption, smoking, cocaine use
- Shock may develop rapidly even with modest visible bleeding

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is she having severe abdominal pain alongside the bleeding?
- Does the pain come and go, or is it constant?
- Is her abdomen very hard or tender to touch?
- Is the baby moving normally?
- Has she had any trauma or fall recently?
- Has she been told she has high blood pressure?
- Has she had abruption in a previous pregnancy?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Painful vaginal bleeding with constant abdominal pain
- Hard, tender uterus
- Reduced fetal movement
- Hypertension present
- Rapid onset with signs of shock

**Uncertainty note:**
Abruption with concealed bleeding can be more severe than visible blood suggests. Any woman with abdominal pain and bleeding — even small amounts — must be escalated immediately. Do not reassure based on small visible blood loss.

---

### DISEASE 3: THREATENED OR INEVITABLE MISCARRIAGE
**Nigerian priority level: HIGH**

Miscarriage occurs in approximately 10–15% of recognized pregnancies. It is most common in the first trimester. Threatened miscarriage (bleeding with closed cervix) may continue to viability; inevitable miscarriage involves an open cervix and cannot be stopped.

**Key differentiating features:**
- First trimester bleeding (under 13 weeks) most commonly
- Cramping lower abdominal pain accompanying bleeding
- Passage of tissue or clots
- Previous miscarriage increases risk
- Bleeding may range from spotting to heavy

**Differentiating questions:**
- How many weeks pregnant is she?
- Is she having cramping pain in her lower abdomen?
- Has she passed any tissue, clots, or anything that looked different from blood?
- Has she had a miscarriage before?
- Has she had any fever alongside the bleeding?

**Confirm towards if:**
- First trimester bleeding with cramping
- Passage of tissue
- Previous miscarriage history
- Bleeding started after pain

**Uncertainty note:**
Threatened miscarriage requires facility assessment to confirm fetal viability by ultrasound. Heavy bleeding with cramping and tissue passage in the first trimester may indicate complete or incomplete miscarriage, which requires urgent management. Never manage at home.

---

### DISEASE 4: ECTOPIC PREGNANCY
**Nigerian priority level: HIGHEST**

Ectopic pregnancy (pregnancy implanted outside the uterus, usually in the fallopian tube) is a life-threatening emergency. Tubal rupture causes sudden severe internal bleeding and maternal death can occur within minutes.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Typically presents before 12 weeks of pregnancy
- Severe one-sided lower abdominal pain — sharp and constant
- Vaginal bleeding — may be dark and scanty, not necessarily heavy
- Signs of internal bleeding: dizziness, fainting, shoulder tip pain (referred from diaphragm)
- Positive pregnancy test confirmed
- History of previous ectopic, pelvic infection, IUD use, or tubal surgery increases risk
- Rupture: sudden severe pain, collapse, pallor — emergency

**Differentiating questions:**
- How many weeks pregnant is she?
- Is she having severe pain on one side of her lower abdomen?
- Does she feel dizzy or has she fainted?
- Is there pain in the tip of her shoulder?
- Has she had a previous ectopic pregnancy or pelvic infection?
- Has she had any surgery on her fallopian tubes?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Early pregnancy (under 12 weeks) with one-sided severe abdominal pain
- Vaginal bleeding (even if scanty)
- Dizziness, fainting, or shoulder tip pain
- Risk factors for ectopic pregnancy

**Uncertainty note:**
Ectopic pregnancy can rupture without warning. Any woman in early pregnancy with one-sided abdominal pain and any bleeding must be escalated as an emergency, even if she appears stable. Stability can deteriorate within minutes.

---

### DISEASE 5: CERVICAL CAUSES (CERVICITIS, CERVICAL ECTROPION, CERVICAL POLYP)
**Nigerian priority level: MEDIUM**

Cervical causes of bleeding are common in pregnancy and are usually benign. Cervical ectropion (eversion of cervical cells) is especially common and can cause post-coital spotting. Cervicitis from infection can cause bleeding. These are diagnoses of exclusion — serious causes must be ruled out first.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Light spotting or bleeding, not heavy
- Often occurs after sexual intercourse (post-coital bleeding)
- No abdominal pain
- Occurs at any gestational age
- No fever (unless cervicitis from STI with secondary infection)

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the bleeding light spotting or more significant?
- Did the bleeding start after sexual intercourse?
- Is there any pain associated with the bleeding?
- Has she had similar spotting before in this pregnancy?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Post-coital spotting, light and brief
- No pain
- No fever
- Occurred previously and resolved

**Uncertainty note:**
Cervical causes cannot be confirmed without examination. Even if the history suggests a benign cervical cause, any bleeding in the second or third trimester must be assessed at a facility. Do not reassure based on history of post-coital bleeding alone after 20 weeks.

---

### DISEASE 6: VASA PRAEVIA
**Nigerian priority level: HIGHEST**

Vasa praevia is a rare but catastrophically dangerous condition in which fetal blood vessels run across the cervical opening. When membranes rupture, fetal vessels tear and the fetus can bleed to death within minutes. It presents with bright red painless bleeding at the time of membrane rupture.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Sudden bright red painless bleeding at the time membranes rupture (water breaks)
- Associated with immediate fetal distress or absence of fetal movement
- More common with velamentous cord insertion or low-lying placenta (often detected on ultrasound)
- Fetal blood loss — extremely rapid deterioration of fetal condition

**Differentiating questions:**
- Did the bleeding start at the same time her waters broke?
- Has fetal movement stopped suddenly?
- Is there bright red blood immediately after the waters broke?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Bleeding at time of membrane rupture
- Sudden cessation of fetal movement
- Bright red blood with ruptured membranes

**Uncertainty note:**
Vasa praevia is an extreme emergency. Any bleeding at the time of membrane rupture must be escalated immediately without delay.

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE — VAGINAL BLEEDING IN PREGNANCY

Any vaginal bleeding in pregnancy beyond light first-trimester spotting requires facility assessment. Heavy bleeding at any stage, any bleeding after 28 weeks, any bleeding with abdominal pain, any bleeding with fever, and any bleeding with reduced fetal movement are emergencies. When the cause cannot be identified with certainty, or when there is any doubt about severity, escalate immediately. Vaginal bleeding in pregnancy is never safe to observe at home without facility evaluation.
