# SYMPTOM FILE: Excessive Crying — Child
**Category:** symptoms | **Subcategory:** child | **Topic:** excessive_crying

---

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### Confirming the Symptom
- Caregiver reports the child is crying more than usual — either in duration, frequency, or intensity
- Infant crying that cannot be consoled with normal soothing (feeding, holding, rocking)
- Crying that is different from the child's normal cry — higher-pitched, weaker, more continuous
- Crying accompanied by drawing up of legs, arching of back, or facial grimacing
- Older child crying persistently and unable or unwilling to say why
- Duration: clarify how long the crying has been going on (hours vs. days)

### Commonly Confused Symptoms
- **Normal infant fussiness** — predictable evening fussiness common in infants under 3 months; settles with normal soothing; no systemic illness
- **Hunger** — crying resolves immediately and completely with feeding
- **Wet or soiled nappy** — resolves immediately with change
- **Temperature discomfort** — too hot or too cold; resolves with adjustment
- **Wind/gas** — passage of gas or stool resolves symptoms; belly hard temporarily
- **Colic** — defined as crying for more than 3 hours per day, more than 3 days per week, in a healthy infant; diagnosis of exclusion only — serious causes must first be excluded

### Critical Immediate Checks
- Age of child — infant under 3 months with unusual crying is high priority
- Is the cry high-pitched, weak, or different from normal?
- Is the child consolable — does anything stop the crying?
- Is the child febrile?
- Is the child lethargic or unresponsive between crying episodes?
- Is the abdomen distended or tense?
- Are there any visible injuries — check whole body including skin, eyes, scalp
- Is there any possibility of trauma or harm to the child?

---

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

*Escalate immediately if any of the following are present:*

1. High-pitched, shrill, or weak cry — indicates neurological or serious systemic illness
2. Infant under 3 months with unusual or inconsolable crying and any fever — EMERGENCY
3. Child completely inconsolable for more than 2–3 hours with no identifiable cause
4. Crying with altered consciousness or child becoming limp between episodes
5. Bulging fontanelle in infant — raised intracranial pressure
6. Seizures before, during, or after the crying episode
7. Suspicion of non-accidental injury — bruising in unusual places, multiple injuries, injury inconsistent with developmental stage or stated history
8. Crying with signs of shock — pale, cold, rapid breathing, not responding normally
9. Scrotal or groin swelling in a boy with acute-onset inconsolable crying — testicular torsion
10. Crying with bilious (green) vomiting and abdominal distension — intestinal obstruction
11. Infant with episodic screaming, drawing up knees, then going quiet — intussusception
12. Caregiver is very worried or says child is the sickest or most distressed they have ever seen

---

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

---

### 1. MALARIA
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
Malaria causes significant discomfort and distress in children — fever, headache, body aches, and vomiting all contribute to persistent crying and irritability. Severe malaria can cause neurological symptoms that produce dramatic behavioural changes.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Fever present — may be high
- Child generally unwell, irritable, and miserable — not just episodically distressed
- Pallor from anaemia
- Vomiting common
- In severe malaria: extreme irritability, high-pitched cry, seizures, altered consciousness
- Child has been feeding less than usual alongside the crying
- May have had malaria before

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Does the child have fever alongside the crying?
- Is the child vomiting?
- Is the child pale?
- Is the child irritable and miserable all the time, or does the crying come and go?
- Has the child had malaria before?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Persistent crying + fever + pallor + vomiting + malaria-endemic area = malaria until excluded

**Uncertainty Note:**
Cerebral malaria can present as irritability and crying that evolves into altered consciousness or seizures. Any febrile child with unusual neurological behaviour must be assessed urgently.

---

### 2. MENINGITIS / SERIOUS CNS INFECTION
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST — EMERGENCY**
Meningitis in infants frequently presents as unexplained inconsolable crying rather than classic symptoms.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- High-pitched, unusual cry — different from normal cry
- Extreme irritability — child cries more when picked up or touched (hyperaesthesia)
- Bulging fontanelle in infants
- Fever — may be high or variable
- Stiff neck (may be subtle or absent in young infants)
- Photophobia
- Seizures
- Rapid deterioration from irritable to lethargic to comatose

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is the cry high-pitched or different from normal?
- Does the baby cry more when picked up or touched?
- Is the soft spot on the baby's head bulging or tense?
- Is the child's neck stiff?
- Has the child had any seizures?
- Is the child sensitive to light?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Inconsolable crying + high-pitched cry + bulging fontanelle + fever + stiff neck or seizures = meningitis — EMERGENCY

**Uncertainty Note:**
Meningitis must be excluded in any infant with unexplained inconsolable crying and fever. Do not observe at home.

---

### 3. OTITIS MEDIA (Ear Infection)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
Acute otitis media is the most common specific cause of crying in infants and young children. Pain is significant and often worse at night.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Crying worse when lying flat — increased ear pressure in horizontal position
- Child pulling, tugging, or hitting ear repeatedly
- Preceded by a cold or upper respiratory infection
- Fever present, often moderate to high
- Crying worse at night and during feeding
- In infants without localising signs: crying + fever + irritability after URTI = otitis media until excluded

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is the child pulling or touching their ear?
- Is the crying worse at night or when the child is lying down?
- Did the child have a cold or runny nose before the crying started?
- Does the child have fever?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Crying + ear pulling + fever + preceded by URTI + worse lying down

**Uncertainty Note:**
AOM and malaria frequently coexist. Presence of ear pulling does not exclude malaria when fever is present.

---

### 4. INFANTILE COLIC
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH (diagnosis of exclusion)**
Colic is defined as prolonged inconsolable crying in an otherwise healthy infant. It must only be considered after dangerous causes have been excluded.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Infant under 5 months
- Crying for more than 3 hours per day, more than 3 days per week
- Child is otherwise healthy — growing normally, feeding well between episodes
- Crying typically occurs at the same time of day — often late afternoon or evening
- Infant draws up legs, appears to strain, passes gas
- No fever, no systemic illness
- Resolves spontaneously by 3–4 months in most infants
- Weight gain is normal

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Does the crying happen at the same time each day?
- Is the baby growing and feeding normally between crying episodes?
- Does the baby seem well and normal between episodes?
- Is there any fever?
- How old is the baby?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Infant under 5 months + predictable timing + otherwise healthy + no fever + normal growth = colic (after exclusion of serious causes)

**Uncertainty Note:**
Colic is a diagnosis of exclusion. Do not apply this label until malaria, otitis media, urinary tract infection, and other causes have been considered.

---

### 5. URINARY TRACT INFECTION (UTI)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH**
UTI is a frequently missed cause of unexplained crying and fever in infants and young children who cannot localise symptoms.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Fever without obvious source
- Unexplained irritability and crying in an infant
- Feeding may be reduced
- Foul-smelling urine
- Frequency or pain on urination (if old enough to report)
- No diarrhoea, no ear pulling, no respiratory symptoms to explain the fever

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Does the child's urine smell bad or look different?
- Does the child cry or seem to strain when passing urine?
- Is there fever with no other obvious cause?
- Is the child passing urine more often than usual?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Unexplained fever + irritability + foul urine + no other localising signs = UTI until excluded — refer for urine testing

**Uncertainty Note:**
UTI cannot be diagnosed clinically in infants. Urine testing is required. Refer if UTI is suspected.

---

### 6. INTUSSUSCEPTION
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH — EMERGENCY**
Classic presentation of intussusception in infants is episodic severe screaming. One of the most important diagnoses not to miss.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Infant 3 months to 2 years — peak at 6–18 months
- Episodic screaming — child screams intensely for 2–5 minutes, then becomes quiet and limp between episodes
- Drawing up knees during episodes of pain
- Pallor during pain episodes
- Vomiting — may become bile-stained
- Red-currant jelly stool — blood and mucus mixed
- Progressive lethargy between episodes as condition worsens

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Does the crying come in episodes — screaming then going very quiet?
- Does the baby draw up the legs during the crying episodes?
- Has the baby passed any stool that looks red or like jam?
- Is the baby becoming more limp and sleepy between crying episodes?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Infant + episodic screaming with quiet intervals + drawing up knees + red stool + progressive lethargy = intussusception — EMERGENCY

**Uncertainty Note:**
The classic triad of intussusception is present in only a minority of cases. Episodic inconsolable crying in an infant with any associated signs should raise this diagnosis.

---

### 7. NON-ACCIDENTAL INJURY (Child Abuse)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH — SAFEGUARDING**
Non-accidental injury is underdiagnosed in Nigeria. Crying is a common trigger for abusive injury. It must always be considered when crying is unexplained and the history does not fit.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Bruising in unusual locations — cheeks, torso, back, buttocks, behind ears
- Bruising in a pre-mobile infant (not yet rolling or crawling)
- Long bone fractures in young infants
- Rib fractures
- History does not match the injury — inconsistent, changing, or implausible explanation
- Caregiver seems evasive or appears very stressed
- Multiple previous unexplained injuries

**Differentiating Questions (approach with care):**
- Has the baby had any falls or accidents recently?
- Has anyone else been looking after the baby?
- Are there any marks or bruises on the baby's body?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Unexplained crying + bruising in unusual locations + history inconsistent with findings + very young infant

**Uncertainty Note:**
If non-accidental injury is suspected, the child's safety is the priority. Advise urgent clinical assessment. Do not advise to go home and observe.

---

### 8. TESTICULAR TORSION (in boys)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH — EMERGENCY (time-critical)**
Acute onset of inconsolable crying in a boy with groin or scrotal swelling. A surgical emergency — testis viable only within 4–6 hours of torsion.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Acute onset, sudden, severe
- Boy refuses to be touched in groin area
- Scrotal swelling or redness on one side
- May have lower abdominal pain referred from groin
- No urinary symptoms, no fever in early torsion
- Child is extremely distressed, cannot be consoled

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is the child a boy?
- Is there swelling or redness in the scrotum — the sac under the penis?
- Is the child refusing to be touched in the groin area?
- Did this start very suddenly?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Boy + sudden severe onset crying + scrotal swelling or tenderness + no other explanation = testicular torsion — EMERGENCY

**Uncertainty Note:**
Testicular torsion is a time-critical emergency. Every hour of delay reduces the chance of saving the testis. Do not observe at home.

---

## GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE

Excessive or inconsolable crying in a child that cannot be attributed to a clearly benign cause must be escalated if any of the following are present:
- Fever of any degree
- Cry that is high-pitched, unusual, or different from normal
- Child cannot be consoled at all
- Infant under 3 months
- Any neurological sign — seizure, altered consciousness, bulging fontanelle
- Visible swelling anywhere on the body
- Episodic pattern with lethargy between episodes
- Any suspicion of injury

**In northern Nigeria, malaria and otitis media are the most common serious causes of acute unexplained crying in children. Meningitis, intussusception, and testicular torsion are time-critical emergencies that must not be missed. Colic is a diagnosis of last resort — apply it only after dangerous causes have been excluded.**
