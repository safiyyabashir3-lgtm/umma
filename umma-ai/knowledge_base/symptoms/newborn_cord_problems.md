# SYMPTOM FILE: Newborn Cord Problems
**UMMA Knowledge Base | Child Health | Newborn**
**File ID:** symptom_newborn_cord_problems
**Version:** 1.0

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### What counts as a newborn cord problem?
Any concern reported by a caregiver about the umbilical cord stump of a newborn, including:
- Redness, swelling, or warmth around the cord or surrounding skin
- Discharge from the cord — pus, foul smell, or excessive wetness
- Bleeding from the cord
- Cord that has not fallen off at the expected time
- Soft flesh or pink/red tissue visible at the cord site after the cord falls off (umbilical granuloma)
- Cord or surrounding area that looks black or has skin discolouration spreading away from the cord

### Normal cord appearance and timeline
- Normal cord: yellowish-green at birth, dries to brown or black, shrivels and falls off in 7–14 days (range 5–21 days)
- Normal minimal moistness at the base of the cord is expected
- A small amount of dried blood or crust at the base is normal
- A slight smell as the cord dries is normal
- Normal cord separation may be accompanied by a small amount of blood-tinged moisture

### Commonly confused symptoms — verify carefully
- **Normal cord drying vs omphalitis** — normal cord darkens and dries; omphalitis causes spreading redness on the surrounding skin (periumbilical erythema spreading onto the abdomen), warmth, swelling, and smell distinct from normal drying
- **Normal moistness at cord base vs purulent discharge** — ask about colour, smell, and whether it has increased
- **Umbilical granuloma vs omphalitis** — granuloma is a small, moist, pink fleshy nodule appearing after cord separation in an otherwise well infant, without surrounding redness or fever; omphalitis involves spreading skin redness and systemic illness
- **Cord bleeding at time of cutting vs later bleeding** — normal at time of delivery; any significant bleeding after the first day is abnormal
- **Umbilical hernia** — a soft bulge under the skin at the umbilicus after cord separation; normal and common in Nigerian newborns; not dangerous but may alarm caregivers

### Critical immediate checks
- **Age of newborn** — omphalitis in the first 28 days of life can cause neonatal sepsis and death within hours
- **Is there redness spreading onto the abdominal skin?** — spreading periumbilical redness is the single most important sign of omphalitis
- **Does the baby look sick?** — lethargy, poor feeding, fever, cold to touch — systemic illness with cord problems is an emergency
- **How was the cord cut and what was applied to it?** — in northern Nigeria, traditional substances are commonly applied to the cord (ash, ghee, shea butter, oil, toothpaste, animal dung); these are major risk factors for neonatal tetanus and omphalitis

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

Listed from most to least dangerous:

1. **Redness spreading from the cord onto the surrounding abdominal skin** — omphalitis progressing to necrotising fasciitis; life-threatening emergency
2. **Baby is limp, unresponsive, or cannot be woken** — neonatal sepsis; immediate emergency
3. **Baby is not feeding at all or has stopped feeding** — neonatal sepsis danger sign
4. **Baby has fever (feels hot) or is abnormally cold** — systemic infection; immediate emergency in any newborn
5. **Baby has stiffness of the body, jaw, or inability to open the mouth or suck** — neonatal tetanus emergency
6. **Skin around the cord is black or has dark discolouration** — necrotising fasciitis or severe omphalitis with tissue necrosis
7. **Foul, very offensive smell from the cord with redness** — infected cord with possible spreading infection
8. **Purulent (pus-like) discharge from the cord** — omphalitis; requires urgent assessment even if no skin spreading yet
9. **Active bleeding from the cord stump that does not stop with gentle pressure** — possible clotting problem or trauma
10. **Cord has not separated by 3 weeks of age with other signs of illness** — consider immune deficiency (leukocyte adhesion deficiency)
11. **Traditional substance applied to the cord (ash, dung, oil, toothpaste, unknown paste)** — high-risk exposure for tetanus and infection; flag for assessment even if no current symptoms
12. **Caregiver is very worried about the cord** — uncertainty always escalates in neonates

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

### 3.1 Omphalitis
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGHEST — EMERGENCY**
Omphalitis (infection of the umbilical cord stump and surrounding tissues) is a major cause of neonatal death in Nigeria. It affects newborns in the first 2–3 weeks of life and can progress to sepsis, necrotising fasciitis, and death within hours. The application of traditional substances to the cord is a critical risk factor in northern Nigeria. *Staphylococcus aureus*, *E. coli*, streptococci, and anaerobic organisms are common causative agents. Early omphalitis may appear mild but can deteriorate catastrophically.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Redness (erythema) at the cord base or surrounding periumbilical skin — this is the cardinal sign
- Spreading of redness onto the abdominal wall — urgent danger sign
- Warmth and swelling around the cord
- Purulent or foul-smelling discharge
- Baby may or may not yet show systemic signs — systemic illness can lag behind local signs
- Fever, poor feeding, or lethargy as infection spreads
- History of home delivery or traditional cord care practice

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the skin around the cord red, and is that redness spreading outwards?
- Is the skin warm or swollen around the cord?
- Is there pus or a very bad smell from the cord?
- Was anything applied to the cord after birth — any oil, ash, paste, or other substance?
- How was the cord cut — what instrument was used?
- Is the baby feeding normally?
- Does the baby have fever or feel cold?
- Does the baby seem less active or harder to wake than usual?

**Confirm towards if:**
Periumbilical redness + warmth + purulent or foul discharge = omphalitis regardless of systemic signs

**Uncertainty note:**
Any spreading redness around the umbilicus in a newborn is omphalitis until proven otherwise. Do not wait for systemic illness to develop. Escalate immediately with any suspicion of omphalitis.

### 3.2 Neonatal Tetanus
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGHEST — EMERGENCY**
Neonatal tetanus remains a significant cause of newborn death in northern Nigeria. It results from contamination of the umbilical cord with *Clostridium tetani* spores, typically through application of contaminated traditional substances (ash, soil, animal dung, ghee) or use of unsterile cutting instruments. Incubation period is typically 3–14 days. Nigeria has one of the highest burdens of neonatal tetanus in the world, concentrated in northern states where maternal TT vaccination coverage is lower.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Onset typically day 3–14 of life
- Baby was feeding and crying normally then stops being able to suck or open the mouth (trismus — lockjaw)
- Progressive generalised muscle stiffness
- Spasms — sudden episodes of whole body stiffness or arching triggered by light, sound, or touch
- Baby cannot open mouth; jaw feels locked; unable to breastfeed
- Face may show a fixed grimace (risus sardonicus)
- Cord may look normal or may be infected
- Mother may not have received tetanus toxoid vaccination

**Differentiating questions:**
- Can the baby open its mouth and suck normally?
- Has the baby's feeding suddenly stopped or become difficult after feeding normally?
- Is the baby's body stiff — arms, legs, or whole body?
- Does the baby have sudden episodes of stiffening or arching — especially with noise or handling?
- Did anything get applied to the cord — ash, oil, paste, animal substance?
- Did the mother receive tetanus vaccination during this pregnancy?
- Where was the cord cut and with what instrument?

**Confirm towards if:**
Inability to suck or open mouth + progressive body stiffness + onset day 3–14 of life + cord exposure history = neonatal tetanus — IMMEDIATE EMERGENCY

**Uncertainty note:**
Any newborn that was feeding normally and then suddenly stops being able to open the mouth or suck must be treated as neonatal tetanus until excluded. This is a time-critical emergency. Do not reassure.

### 3.3 Neonatal Sepsis (from Cord as Entry Point)
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGHEST**
The umbilical cord is one of the commonest entry points for pathogens causing neonatal sepsis. Cord infection can seed bacteraemia with rapid progression to neonatal sepsis and death. Neonatal sepsis may present with very non-specific signs — poor feeding, temperature instability, lethargy — making it difficult to detect without vigilance.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Cord may appear infected (redness, discharge) OR may appear relatively normal while sepsis is already spreading
- Baby looks unwell: not feeding, lethargic, difficult to wake, abnormally quiet or abnormally irritable
- Temperature instability: fever (feels hot) OR hypothermia (feels cold and clammy)
- Poor colour: pale, yellow, or blue-grey (mottled)
- Rapid breathing or grunting
- May have abdominal distension
- Fontanelle may be bulging

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the baby feeding as well as usual or has feeding become difficult?
- Is the baby unusually sleepy or hard to wake for feeds?
- Does the baby feel hot or cold?
- Is the baby breathing fast or making unusual noises?
- Does the baby's skin look pale, yellow, or mottled?
- Is the abdomen swollen?
- Is the top of the head (soft spot) bulging?

**Confirm towards if:**
Cord problem + any of: poor feeding, lethargy, temperature instability, rapid breathing, poor colour = neonatal sepsis — IMMEDIATE EMERGENCY

**Uncertainty note:**
A newborn with a cord problem who is not feeding well or who looks unwell in any way must be treated as neonatal sepsis. In neonates, clinical deterioration is rapid. Do not reassure a sick-looking newborn with cord problems.

### 3.4 Umbilical Granuloma
**Nigerian Priority Level: MEDIUM**
Umbilical granuloma is a benign overgrowth of tissue (granulation tissue) at the umbilical cord site after separation. It is the most common cause of persistent umbilical moisture or discharge after the cord has fallen off in an otherwise well infant. Importantly, it is distinguished from omphalitis by the absence of surrounding redness, fever, or systemic illness.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Appears after cord separation — cord has already fallen off
- Pink or red fleshy nodule or moist patch at the umbilicus
- May be moist, weeping clear fluid, or slightly blood-tinged
- No spreading redness on surrounding skin
- No foul smell
- Baby is well — feeding, active, no fever
- May have been present for days or weeks

**Differentiating questions:**
- Has the cord already fallen off?
- Is there a small pink or red fleshy lump remaining at the umbilicus?
- Is the skin around the umbilicus normal in colour — no redness spreading outwards?
- Is there any fever?
- Is the baby feeding well and behaving normally?

**Confirm towards if:**
Post-cord-separation pink moist nodule + no periumbilical redness + no fever + well baby = umbilical granuloma

**Uncertainty note:**
Granuloma can become secondarily infected. If any redness develops around the granuloma, or if the baby becomes unwell, reassess for omphalitis.

### 3.5 Cord Bleeding
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGH if more than trivial**
Small amounts of blood at the cord base during separation are normal. Significant or ongoing cord bleeding is abnormal and may indicate inadequate clamping, trauma, or a bleeding disorder such as vitamin K deficiency bleeding (VKDB), also known as haemorrhagic disease of the newborn.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Active bleeding or blood that soaks cloth or pad
- Bleeding that does not stop with gentle pressure
- In VKDB: cord bleeding may be accompanied by bleeding from other sites — skin bruising, blood in stool, vomiting blood, or fontanelle bulge
- Baby may not have received vitamin K at birth (common in home deliveries in Nigeria)
- Bleeding from cord AND other sites = systemic coagulopathy

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is blood actively coming from the cord, or is it a small amount of old dried blood?
- Does it stop with gentle pressure?
- Is there bruising on the skin, blood in the stool or vomit, or bleeding from anywhere else?
- Did the baby receive an injection at birth (vitamin K)?
- Where was the baby delivered — hospital or home?

**Confirm towards if:**
Active cord bleeding that does not stop + no vitamin K at birth + possible bleeding elsewhere = vitamin K deficiency bleeding — ESCALATE

**Uncertainty note:**
Any ongoing cord bleeding that does not stop with pressure, or any cord bleeding combined with bleeding elsewhere on the body, must be escalated immediately.

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE
Cord problems in newborns must be assessed with the highest level of caution. The newborn immune system is immature; what begins as a localised cord infection can become fatal neonatal sepsis within hours. In northern Nigeria, traditional cord care practices (application of ash, oil, dung, shea butter) make neonatal tetanus and omphalitis significantly more likely. When there is any doubt about whether a cord sign is normal or abnormal, or when the baby is not feeding well alongside any cord concern — escalate immediately. Uncertainty in newborn cord problems always escalates.
