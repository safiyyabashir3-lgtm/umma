## For UMMA AI Clinical Reasoning — Nigerian Context (Kano / Northwest Priority)
### Awaiting Clinician Verification

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### Confirming the symptom
Caregivers in northern Nigeria may describe cough as "tari" (Hausa direct term), or describe
the child as making a sound in the chest, struggling to breathe, or having a "wet" or "barking"
sound. Some may not use the word cough at all but describe the child's breathing as noisy or
laboured. Treat any of these as cough until confirmed otherwise.

Note: cough_child.md covers cough as the primary complaint. If breathing difficulty is the
primary complaint — fast breathing, chest indrawing, difficulty at rest — use
difficulty_breathing_child.md. In practice cough and breathing difficulty overlap significantly
and both files may be retrieved together during disease reasoning.

Establish age immediately:
- Any cough in a child under 2 months — escalate urgently. Do not attempt disease reasoning.
- Child 2 to 12 months — threshold for escalation is lower throughout.

### Symptoms that may be confused with cough
- Noisy breathing through a blocked nose — snuffling or snoring sound, no chest sound,
  feeding normally
- Gagging or choking during feeds in a young infant — not cough; may suggest reflux
  or feeding difficulty
- Throat clearing — brief, no respiratory distress, child otherwise well

### Local language and cultural context
- Tari — cough (direct Hausa term, universally understood)
- Tari mai tsanani — severe cough
- Yana tari kullum — coughing all the time
- Yana tari da dare — coughing at night (night-predominant cough — relevant for croup and asthma)
- Kirji yana yi masa ciwo — chest is hurting (may accompany pneumonia or severe cough)
- Numfashi da wahala — difficulty breathing alongside cough

Caregivers in Kano commonly treat cough at home with herbal preparations, honey, or patent
medicine vendor syrups before presenting. Always ask what has already been given and for how long.
A cough that has not improved after home treatment for several days needs clinic assessment.

Indoor cooking smoke from wood-burning or kerosene stoves is a significant cause of recurrent
cough and respiratory illness in young children in northern Nigerian households. Ask about the
cooking environment.

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

Escalate immediately if any of the following are present. Do not proceed to disease reasoning.
Ordered from most dangerous to least dangerous.

- Any cough in a child under 2 months — emergency
- Child unable to drink, feed, or breastfeed at all due to breathing or coughing
- Chest indrawing — skin pulling in between ribs or below breastbone with each breath
- Grunting sound with each breath — especially in infants; indicates severe respiratory distress
- Stridor — harsh crowing or high-pitched noise when breathing in (not out)
- Child's lips, tongue, or fingertips appearing blue or grey
- Child looks severely unwell — limp, exhausted, not responsive to surroundings
- Breathing so fast the child cannot complete a feed
- Cough with high fever and child looking very unwell
- Sudden onset of cough in a previously well child with no fever — possible foreign body inhalation,
  especially children 6 months to 3 years
- Cough with coughing up of blood — any amount
- Cough persisting more than 3 weeks without improvement — possible tuberculosis
- Caregiver says this is the worst the child has ever been

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

### Nigerian and Kano context
Cough in a child under 5 in Kano must be taken seriously. Pneumonia is the second leading
cause of death in Nigerian children under 5. Acute respiratory infection affects approximately
38% of under-5 children in Kano. Malnutrition affects over 60% of children under 5 in Kano
and significantly worsens the severity of any respiratory illness — a malnourished child with
pneumonia deteriorates faster and needs earlier escalation.

Malaria and pneumonia frequently coexist. A child with fever and cough may have both. Indoor
air pollution from cooking fires is a significant environmental risk factor in this population.
Tuberculosis is endemic in Nigeria and must be considered for any prolonged cough. Never treat
cough in a child under 5 as trivial.

---

### DISEASE 1: PNEUMONIA — BACTERIAL OR VIRAL
Nigerian priority: HIGHEST for cough in a child under 5

Key differentiating features:
- Fast breathing persistent at rest — most reliable clinical sign
- Chest indrawing — ribs visible with each breath; indicates severe pneumonia
- Cough — may be wet, dry, or minimal; severity of cough does not predict severity of pneumonia
- Fever present in most cases but may be absent in malnourished children
- Poor feeding due to breathing effort
- Grunting in infants — sound with each breath out, indicates severe disease
- Child looks tired, unwell, working hard to breathe

Fast breathing thresholds by age (WHO IMCI):
- Child under 2 months — 60 breaths per minute or more
- Child 2 to 11 months — 50 breaths per minute or more
- Child 1 to 5 years — 40 breaths per minute or more

Differentiating questions:
- Is the child breathing faster than normal even when calm and not crying?
- Is there any pulling in of the skin between the ribs or below the neck with each breath?
- How long has the cough been present?
- Is the child feeding or drinking much less than usual?
- Does the child have a fever?

Confirm towards pneumonia if:
- Cough + fast breathing at rest + fever + poor feeding
- Cough + chest indrawing regardless of other findings
- Fast breathing persistent at rest regardless of other findings

Uncertainty note:
- Severity of cough does not predict severity of pneumonia — a child with mild cough
  and fast breathing is more seriously ill than a child with loud cough and normal breathing
- In malnourished children fever may be absent even in severe pneumonia
- Malaria and pneumonia coexist frequently in Nigerian children — consider both

---

### DISEASE 2: MALARIA WITH RESPIRATORY INVOLVEMENT
Nigerian priority: HIGH — malaria is the first differential for any febrile illness
including febrile illness with cough

Key differentiating features:
- Fever prominent — with or without classic chills and sweating pattern
- Fast breathing driven by severe anaemia from malaria — not primary chest infection
- Pallor of inner eyelids and palms — from malaria-related anaemia
- Cough present but not the dominant complaint
- No chest indrawing in uncomplicated malaria — if indrawing present, consider pneumonia also
- Child looks pale and weak rather than primarily showing respiratory distress
- Vomiting commonly present

Differentiating questions:
- Has the child been shivering or shaking, then getting very hot, then sweating?
- Are the inside of the child's eyelids or the palms looking very pale?
- Has the child been vomiting?
- Has the child been sleeping under a mosquito net?

Confirm towards malaria with respiratory involvement if:
- Fever + chills + pallor + fast breathing with no chest indrawing
- Fever + pallor + vomiting + cough

Uncertainty note:
- Malaria and pneumonia coexist frequently — do not exclude one when the other is suspected
- Pallor with fast breathing in a febrile child is serious regardless of cause
- Never rule out malaria in a Nigerian child with cough and fever

---

### DISEASE 3: BRONCHIOLITIS
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM-HIGH — most common in infants under 12 months

Key differentiating features:
- Age under 12 months — bronchiolitis is predominantly a disease of infancy
- Starts as runny nose and mild cough, then breathing worsens progressively over 2 to 3 days
- Wheezing — a whistling sound when the child breathes out (not in)
- Fast breathing with or without chest indrawing as disease progresses
- Low-grade fever or no fever — high fever suggests bacterial pneumonia instead
- Poor feeding due to breathing difficulty
- No response to malaria treatment

Differentiating questions:
- How old is the child?
- Did this start with a runny nose before the cough and breathing difficulty developed?
- Is there a whistling sound when the child breathes out?
- Has the breathing been getting gradually worse over the past 2 to 3 days?

Confirm towards bronchiolitis if:
- Infant under 12 months + started with runny nose + progressive worsening over days
  + wheeze on breathing out + low or no fever

Uncertainty note:
- Bronchiolitis can become severe quickly in infants — poor feeding is a key warning sign
- Any infant under 3 months with respiratory distress — escalate immediately regardless
  of suspected cause
- Bronchiolitis and bacterial pneumonia can coexist and be indistinguishable clinically

---

### DISEASE 4: CROUP — VIRAL LARYNGOTRACHEOBRONCHITIS
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM

Key differentiating features:
- Barking cough — distinctive seal-like or dog-bark sound, usually unmistakeable
- Stridor — harsh crowing noise when breathing in (inspiratory stridor)
- Hoarse voice or hoarse cry
- Symptoms worse at night — characteristic pattern
- Usually preceded by 1 to 2 days of cold symptoms and runny nose
- Fever low-grade or absent
- Most common in children 6 months to 3 years

Differentiating questions:
- Does the cough have a barking or seal-like sound?
- Is there a harsh crowing or high-pitched noise when the child breathes in?
- Is the child's voice or cry hoarse?
- Are the symptoms worse at night?
- Did this start with a cold a day or two before?

Confirm towards croup if:
- Barking cough + inspiratory stridor + hoarse voice + worse at night

Uncertainty note:
- Mild croup with barking cough, no stridor at rest, child drinking normally —
  lower urgency but still needs monitoring with clear safety net
- Stridor present at rest — escalate urgently
- Child unable to drink due to croup — escalate urgently
- Any stridor in a child under 6 months — escalate immediately regardless of cause

---

### DISEASE 5: FOREIGN BODY INHALATION
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM — must never be missed; most common in children 6 months to 3 years

Key differentiating features:
- Sudden onset — child was completely well, then suddenly began coughing or choking
- No fever at onset — fever may develop later if airway obstruction causes secondary infection
- Persistent cough that does not improve over days
- Wheeze that is localised — may sound different on one side of the chest
- History of playing with small objects, eating nuts, seeds, or hard foods
- May follow an episode of choking that seemed to resolve — partial obstruction
- Child 6 months to 3 years at highest risk — age of mouthing objects

Differentiating questions:
- Did the coughing start suddenly while the child was eating or playing with something small?
- Was the child completely well just before the coughing started?
- Has the cough been present since a specific moment rather than developing gradually?

Confirm towards foreign body if:
- Sudden onset cough with no fever + possible history of inhalation
- Child eating nuts, seeds, or playing with small objects just before coughing began
- Persistent unexplained cough that does not fit other patterns

Uncertainty note:
- If foreign body is suspected at any level — escalate immediately; do not wait
- A child who choked and appeared to recover but continues coughing still needs
  urgent assessment — the object may be lodged partially in the lower airway
- Do not attempt to dislodge at home

---

### DISEASE 6: TUBERCULOSIS
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM — endemic in Nigeria; commonly missed or delayed in children

Key differentiating features:
- Cough persisting more than 2 to 3 weeks without improvement
- Gradual weight loss or failure to gain weight
- Night sweats
- Prolonged low-grade fever not responding to malaria treatment
- Known contact with an adult who has tuberculosis or prolonged cough in the household
- Child appears chronically unwell rather than acutely ill
- BCG scar absent — increases risk
- More common in malnourished children and HIV-exposed children

Differentiating questions:
- How long has the cough been present?
- Has the child been losing weight or not growing well?
- Is there anyone in the household with a long-standing cough or diagnosed tuberculosis?
- Has the child been having fevers or night sweats for many weeks?

Confirm towards tuberculosis if:
- Cough more than 3 weeks + weight loss or poor growth + household TB contact
- Prolonged fever not responding to malaria treatment + chronic cough + weight loss

Uncertainty note:
- Tuberculosis in young children is difficult to confirm without clinic investigation
- A prolonged cough in a child in Kano always warrants clinic assessment — do not manage at home
- TB and HIV coexist — HIV-exposed children are at significantly higher risk

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE
- Any cough in a child under 2 months — escalate immediately without further reasoning
- Cough with fast breathing in any child under 5 — treat as serious and escalate urgently
- Cough with poor feeding in an infant — escalate same day
- Prolonged cough more than 3 weeks without improvement — always needs clinic assessment
- Malnourished child with any cough — escalate sooner; illness is always more severe
- Malaria and pneumonia coexist frequently — one does not exclude the other
- When in doubt — escalate. Uncertainty is never a reason to reassure about cough in a
  Nigerian child under 5.
