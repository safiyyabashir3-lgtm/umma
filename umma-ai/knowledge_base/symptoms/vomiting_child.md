## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### Confirming the symptom
Caregivers in northern Nigeria describe vomiting as "amai" — this is the direct Hausa term
and is universally understood. Confirm that the caregiver means actual vomiting — forceful
expulsion of stomach contents — rather than spitting up or posseting, which is normal in
young infants. Vomiting is almost never an isolated diagnosis in a Nigerian child under 5.
It is a symptom of an underlying condition. The clinical task is always to identify what is
causing it.

Establish the child's age immediately:
- Any vomiting in a child under 2 months — treat as urgent. Pyloric stenosis must be
  considered. Do not apply adult or older child frameworks.
- Infant under 6 months — threshold for escalation is lower throughout.

Clarify if not already stated:
- How many times has the child vomited?
- Is the child able to keep any fluid down at all?
- Is there any blood or bile (green colour) in the vomit?
- Does the child also have diarrhoea or fever?

### Symptoms that may be confused with vomiting
- Posseting or spitting up in a young infant — small amounts of milk coming back up after
  feeds without distress; normal in infants under 6 months; no forceful ejection
- Coughing causing gag and bring-up — follows a coughing fit; not primary vomiting
- Nasal secretions causing gagging in infants — associated with blocked nose, not the stomach

### Local language and cultural context
- Amai — vomiting (direct Hausa term)
- Na yi amai — I vomited / the child vomited
- Amai da zawo — vomiting and diarrhoea together (dehydration risk escalates rapidly)
- Amai da zazzabi — vomiting with fever (strongly suggests malaria in a Nigerian child)
- Yana amai kullum — vomiting repeatedly / keeps vomiting
- Bai iya sha komai ba — cannot keep anything down (red flag)
- Yana fitar da kore — bringing up green (bilious vomiting — red flag for bowel obstruction)

Vomiting in a child is commonly attributed in northern Nigeria to teething, "stomach problems,"
or the evil eye. Do not dismiss these framings — acknowledge them and continue clinical assessment
respectfully. In many cases vomiting with fever is malaria and is genuinely serious regardless
of the cultural framing applied to it.

Caregivers may have stopped feeding the child to try to stop the vomiting. This is counterproductive
and can worsen dehydration — address this directly.

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

Escalate immediately if any of the following are present. Do not proceed to disease reasoning.
Ordered from most dangerous to least dangerous.

- Child unconscious or very difficult to wake
- Child unable to keep any fluid down at all for more than 4 to 6 hours — emergency dehydration
- Signs of severe dehydration — sunken eyes, very dry mouth, no tears, no urine for 8 hours,
  skin pinch returns slowly
- Bile-stained (green) vomiting — possible bowel obstruction; emergency in any age
- Blood in vomit — red blood or coffee-ground appearance
- Vomiting with severe abdominal pain and distended (swollen) abdomen
- Vomiting with convulsion — emergency
- Vomiting in a child under 2 months — always urgent; consider pyloric stenosis or serious infection
- Projectile vomiting in an infant 2 to 8 weeks — forceful vomiting immediately after every feed
  (pyloric stenosis — escalate urgently)
- Vomiting with stiff neck — possible meningitis
- Vomiting with severe headache — possible meningitis or raised intracranial pressure
- Vomiting following a head injury — possible intracranial injury
- Vomiting in a child who looks extremely unwell, limp, or pale

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

### Nigerian and Kano context
Vomiting in a child under 5 in Nigeria is most commonly a feature of malaria, gastroenteritis,
or both. Malaria causes vomiting in almost all significant cases in young children — it is
often the first and most prominent symptom. In a febrile child with vomiting in Kano, malaria
must always be the first differential.

Dehydration from vomiting combined with diarrhoea is a life-threatening emergency in young
children. Malnourished children, who represent over 60% of under-5s in Kano, have less
physiological reserve and dehydrate faster. Bilious vomiting at any age and projectile
vomiting in a young infant are red flags for surgical causes that must never be missed.

---

### DISEASE 1: MALARIA
Nigerian priority: HIGHEST — vomiting is one of the most common presentations of malaria
in Nigerian children under 5

Key differentiating features:
- Fever alongside vomiting — the combination of fever and vomiting in a Nigerian child
  is malaria until proven otherwise
- Chills and sweating episodes — when present, strongly support malaria; may be absent
  in children under 2
- Pallor — inner eyelids pale from malaria anaemia
- Child looks unwell beyond just the vomiting
- Repeated vomiting — not just one or two episodes
- Headache — older children may report; younger children show as irritability
- Poor appetite

Differentiating questions:
- Does the child have a fever alongside the vomiting?
- Has the child been shivering or sweating?
- Are the inside of the child's lower eyelids looking pale?
- Has the child been sleeping under a mosquito net?
- How many days has the vomiting been going on?

Confirm towards malaria if:
- Vomiting + fever regardless of other findings in a Nigerian child under 5
- Vomiting + pallor + fever
- Vomiting + fever + no clear gastrointestinal cause

Uncertainty note:
- Never rule out malaria in a febrile child with vomiting in Nigeria
- Vomiting from malaria may prevent oral antimalarial treatment — injectable treatment
  may be needed; this requires hospital or clinic
- Malaria and gastroenteritis can coexist — one does not exclude the other

---

### DISEASE 2: GASTROENTERITIS — VIRAL OR BACTERIAL
Nigerian priority: HIGH — rotavirus is the leading cause of vomiting with diarrhoea
in children under 2 in Nigeria

Key differentiating features:
- Vomiting prominent especially early in the illness
- Diarrhoea follows or accompanies vomiting — watery stools
- Low-grade or moderate fever
- No blood in vomit or stool in viral gastroenteritis
- Rapid dehydration — young children lose fluid quickly
- Spreads in households — other family members may be affected
- Rotavirus most common under 2 years

Differentiating questions:
- Does the child also have diarrhoea?
- How many times has the child vomited and over how many hours?
- Is the child able to keep any fluid down at all?
- Has anyone else in the family had similar symptoms?
- Is there blood in the vomit or stool?

Confirm towards gastroenteritis if:
- Vomiting + diarrhoea + low to moderate fever + no blood + no clear malaria pattern

Uncertainty note:
- Viral gastroenteritis and malaria are clinically similar and frequently coexist
- The key clinical question is always hydration status, not the precise cause
- Do not wait for a clear distinction before escalating if the child cannot keep fluids down

---

### DISEASE 3: PYLORIC STENOSIS
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM — important to recognise in infants 2 to 8 weeks

Key differentiating features:
- Age 2 to 8 weeks — almost exclusively presents in this window
- Projectile vomiting — forceful, shoots away from child, occurs immediately or shortly
  after every feed
- Hungry again immediately after vomiting — child vomits then tries to feed again
- No fever — this is not an infection
- Progressive weight loss — child failing to gain weight or losing weight
- Constipation — small hard stools
- Male infants more commonly affected but occurs in females too
- Visible gastric peristalsis — wave of movement across the upper abdomen during feeding
  (clinical sign, not detectable by caregiver at home)

Differentiating questions:
- How old is the child?
- Does the vomiting happen after every feed and shoot out forcefully?
- After vomiting does the child seem hungry and want to feed again immediately?
- Is there any fever?
- Has the child been losing weight?

Confirm towards pyloric stenosis if:
- Infant 2 to 8 weeks + projectile vomiting after every feed + hungry after vomiting
  + no fever + weight loss

Uncertainty note:
- Pyloric stenosis is a surgical emergency — escalate urgently
- It causes metabolic imbalance that worsens rapidly — do not delay
- Any infant under 2 months with repeated forceful vomiting and no fever needs urgent assessment

---

### DISEASE 4: MENINGITIS
Nigerian priority: HIGH — must not be missed; vomiting is an early feature

Key differentiating features:
- Vomiting that is projectile or comes without nausea — not preceded by feeling sick
- Severe headache in older children — infants show high-pitched cry and extreme irritability
- Stiff neck — may be absent in infants under 12 months
- Bulging fontanelle in infants
- Photophobia — child turns from light
- Fever
- Rapid deterioration — child worsening over hours
- Petechiae or purpura — red spots that do not fade under pressure (emergency sign)

Differentiating questions:
- Does the child have a severe headache alongside the vomiting?
- Is the child's neck stiff — does the child resist bending the head forward?
- Is the child extremely irritable and impossible to comfort?
- In an infant — is the soft spot on the head pushed outward?
- Are there any spots on the skin that do not fade when pressed?

Confirm towards meningitis if:
- Vomiting + stiff neck + fever regardless of other findings
- Vomiting + bulging fontanelle + fever in an infant
- Vomiting + petechiae or purpura — immediate emergency

Uncertainty note:
- If meningitis is suspected at any confidence level — escalate immediately
- The window between early signs and death in bacterial meningitis can be hours
- Vomiting as a sole finding does not suggest meningitis — it must accompany neurological signs

---

### DISEASE 5: SURGICAL ABDOMEN — INTUSSUSCEPTION, OBSTRUCTION
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM — must not be missed; surgical causes are time-critical

Key differentiating features:
- Intussusception — most common in infants 3 months to 2 years
  - Intermittent episodes of severe abdominal pain — child draws knees to chest, screams,
    then appears well between episodes
  - Vomiting associated with pain episodes
  - Later: blood and mucus in stool — "redcurrant jelly" stool
  - Child becomes progressively more unwell
  - Abdominal mass may be felt — not detectable without examination
- Bowel obstruction — any age
  - Bilious (green) vomiting — the most important sign; indicates obstruction below
    the stomach
  - Abdominal distension — abdomen visibly swollen and tense
  - No passage of stool or flatus
  - Severe abdominal pain

Differentiating questions:
- Is the vomiting green or bile-coloured?
- Does the child have episodes of severe cramping pain where they draw their legs up and
  then seem to recover, then the pain comes back?
- Is the abdomen swollen and hard?
- Has the child passed any stool or wind in the last several hours?
- Has there been any blood or jelly-like material in the stool?

Confirm towards surgical cause if:
- Green (bilious) vomiting at any age — escalate immediately
- Vomiting + episodic severe abdominal pain + child drawing knees up + later bloody mucus stool
- Vomiting + abdominal distension + no stool passage

Uncertainty note:
- Bilious vomiting in any child at any age is a surgical emergency until proven otherwise
- Do not wait for blood in stool to act — intussusception must be treated before bowel
  becomes necrotic
- Intussusception can be missed because the child appears well between pain episodes

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE
- Vomiting with inability to keep any fluid down for more than 4 to 6 hours — escalate urgently
- Green (bilious) vomiting at any age — escalate immediately as surgical emergency
- Vomiting with fever in a Nigerian child — always consider malaria first
- Vomiting with neurological signs — stiff neck, bulging fontanelle, extreme irritability —
  escalate immediately
- Malnourished child with vomiting — dehydration is more dangerous and faster; escalate sooner
- When in doubt — escalate. A child who cannot keep fluids down is at serious risk
  of dehydration in any Nigerian context.
