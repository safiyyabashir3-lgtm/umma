# UMMA Symptom File: Abdominal Pain in Pregnancy

---

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

**Confirm the symptom:**
- Ask where the pain is located: lower abdomen, upper abdomen, one side, all over, or under the ribs
- Ask the character of the pain: cramping (comes and goes), constant, sharp, dull, or pressure-like
- Ask gestational age — the differential changes significantly by trimester
- Ask whether the pain is associated with vaginal bleeding, fever, vomiting, or changes in fetal movement
- Confirm it is abdominal pain and not chest pain, back pain alone, or pain on urination

**Commonly confused with:**
- Round ligament pain — sharp, brief, one-sided lower abdominal pain that occurs on sudden movement; common and benign but must not be assumed without excluding dangerous causes
- Back pain described as abdominal pain — ask the woman to point to exactly where the pain is
- Braxton Hicks contractions — irregular, painless or mildly uncomfortable tightening of the uterus; no true pain; more common in third trimester
- Constipation — very common in pregnancy; cramping lower abdominal pain with no bowel movement for several days

**Critical immediate checks:**
- Is there vaginal bleeding alongside the pain? If yes — ectopic, abruption, or miscarriage emergency
- Is there fever? If yes — infection, chorioamnionitis, or appendicitis
- Is the pain constant and severe? — escalate without delay
- Is she in the third trimester? — pre-eclampsia (epigastric pain) and preterm labour must be immediately considered

---

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

Escalate immediately if any of the following are present:

1. Severe constant abdominal pain at any gestational age
2. Abdominal pain with vaginal bleeding — ectopic pregnancy, placental abruption, or miscarriage
3. Abdominal pain with fever — appendicitis, chorioamnionitis, pyelonephritis, or sepsis
4. Epigastric or right upper quadrant pain after 20 weeks — pre-eclampsia with HELLP syndrome
5. Severe one-sided lower abdominal pain in early pregnancy — ectopic pregnancy until excluded
6. Abdominal pain with dizziness, fainting, or signs of shock
7. Abdominal pain with reduced or absent fetal movement
8. Regular painful contractions before 37 weeks — preterm labour
9. Abdominal pain with shoulder tip pain — ectopic rupture with internal bleeding
10. Abdominal pain with rigid or board-like abdomen
11. Pain in a woman with a previous caesarean scar — uterine rupture risk
12. Abdominal pain that is worsening progressively rather than coming and going
13. Pain with inability to walk, stand, or move normally
14. Caregiver or woman is very frightened or reports pain is the worst she has ever felt

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

---

### DISEASE 1: ECTOPIC PREGNANCY
**Nigerian priority level: HIGHEST**

Ectopic pregnancy is the most dangerous cause of abdominal pain in the first trimester and must be the first consideration in any woman under 12 weeks with one-sided pain. Tubal rupture causes rapid fatal haemorrhage.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Under 12 weeks gestation
- Severe one-sided lower abdominal or pelvic pain
- Vaginal bleeding — may be scanty or dark
- Dizziness, fainting, or shoulder tip pain indicating internal bleeding
- History of previous ectopic, pelvic infection, IUD, or tubal surgery
- Positive pregnancy test

**Differentiating questions:**
- How many weeks pregnant is she?
- Is the pain on one side?
- Has she fainted or does she feel very dizzy?
- Does she have pain at the tip of her shoulder?
- Has she had a previous ectopic pregnancy or pelvic infection?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Under 12 weeks with severe one-sided pain
- Any vaginal bleeding
- Dizziness, fainting, or shoulder tip pain
- Risk factors present

**Uncertainty note:**
Any woman in early pregnancy with one-sided abdominal pain must be treated as possible ectopic pregnancy and escalated immediately, even if she appears stable. Rupture can occur without warning.

---

### DISEASE 2: PLACENTAL ABRUPTION
**Nigerian priority level: HIGHEST**

Placental abruption is premature separation of the placenta. It causes severe abdominal pain, uterine rigidity, and can rapidly kill the fetus and cause life-threatening maternal haemorrhage — even when external bleeding is minimal.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Constant, severe abdominal pain — does not come and go like labour
- Uterus hard and tender — board-like rigidity
- Vaginal bleeding may be present but bleeding can be concealed
- Reduced or absent fetal movement
- Occurs in second or third trimester
- Risk factors: hypertension, trauma, previous abruption, cocaine use

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the pain constant or does it come and go?
- Is there any vaginal bleeding alongside the pain?
- Is her abdomen very hard or tender to touch?
- Is the baby moving normally?
- Has she had any trauma or fall?
- Has she been told she has high blood pressure?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Constant severe abdominal pain in second or third trimester
- Uterine rigidity or tenderness
- Associated bleeding or reduced fetal movement
- Hypertension or trauma history

**Uncertainty note:**
Concealed abruption can be more severe than visible bleeding suggests. Constant severe abdominal pain in the second or third trimester is abruption until proven otherwise. Escalate immediately.

---

### DISEASE 3: PRE-ECLAMPSIA WITH HELLP SYNDROME
**Nigerian priority level: HIGHEST**

Epigastric or right upper quadrant pain after 20 weeks is a sign of HELLP syndrome — a severe complication of pre-eclampsia involving liver involvement, haemolysis, and low platelets. It is a maternal emergency.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Pain in the upper abdomen — epigastric or right side under the ribs
- After 20 weeks gestation
- Associated headache or visual changes
- Nausea and vomiting
- Known or suspected hypertension
- Swelling of face or hands

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the pain in the upper part of her abdomen or under her right ribs?
- How many weeks pregnant is she?
- Does she have a headache or visual changes alongside the pain?
- Has her blood pressure been measured recently — was it high?
- Is her face or hands swollen?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Upper abdominal pain after 20 weeks
- Associated headache, visual changes, or swelling
- Known or suspected hypertension

**Uncertainty note:**
Epigastric pain after 20 weeks is HELLP syndrome until excluded by facility assessment. Never attribute upper abdominal pain in the third trimester to indigestion without blood pressure measurement and clinical assessment.

---

### DISEASE 4: PRETERM LABOUR
**Nigerian priority level: HIGH**

Preterm labour (labour before 37 weeks) presents with regular painful contractions and is a major cause of neonatal mortality in Nigeria. It may be triggered by infection, cervical incompetence, or occur without clear cause.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Regular cramping pains that come and go at intervals — becoming more frequent and more intense
- Before 37 weeks gestation
- May be associated with low back pain
- May be associated with mucus or bloody show (small amount of blood-tinged mucus)
- Pressure in the pelvis or vagina
- Contractions becoming regular — every 10 minutes or more frequently

**Differentiating questions:**
- How many weeks pregnant is she?
- Are the pains coming regularly — every few minutes?
- Are the pains getting stronger and closer together?
- Does she have low back pain alongside the abdominal pain?
- Has she noticed any mucus or blood from the vagina?
- Does she feel pressure or heaviness in her lower pelvis?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Regular painful contractions before 37 weeks
- Increasing frequency and intensity
- Pelvic pressure or bloody show
- Low back pain accompanying contractions

**Uncertainty note:**
Any regular painful contractions before 37 weeks must be escalated urgently. Preterm labour may progress to delivery within hours. Do not observe at home.

---

### DISEASE 5: APPENDICITIS
**Nigerian priority level: HIGH**

Appendicitis occurs in pregnancy at the same rate as in the general population but is harder to diagnose because the growing uterus displaces the appendix upward, shifting the pain location. It carries high risk of perforation in pregnancy.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Pain that starts around the navel then shifts to the right side — but in pregnancy the pain may be in the right upper abdomen rather than right lower abdomen due to uterine displacement
- Fever alongside abdominal pain
- Nausea and vomiting
- Loss of appetite
- Pain worsens with movement
- Pain is constant and progressive, not cramping

**Differentiating questions:**
- Did the pain start around the navel and move to the right side?
- Is there fever alongside the pain?
- Is she nauseated or vomiting?
- Does the pain get worse when she moves or when her abdomen is pressed?
- Is the pain constant rather than coming and going?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Constant pain on the right side of the abdomen
- Fever accompanying pain
- Nausea, vomiting, and loss of appetite
- Pain worsens with movement or palpation

**Uncertainty note:**
Appendicitis in pregnancy is easily missed because the pain location shifts. Fever with constant right-sided abdominal pain at any gestational age must be escalated for surgical evaluation. Perforation in pregnancy carries very high maternal and fetal mortality.

---

### DISEASE 6: URINARY TRACT INFECTION / PYELONEPHRITIS
**Nigerian priority level: HIGH**

Pyelonephritis (kidney infection) causes significant abdominal and flank pain in pregnancy. It can trigger preterm labour and progress to sepsis.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Loin or flank pain — below the ribs on one or both sides, often described as back pain
- Fever — often high
- Burning or frequent urination
- Nausea and vomiting
- Cloudy or offensive-smelling urine
- Tenderness over the kidney area (costovertebral angle)

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the pain in the back or side below the ribs, rather than the front of the abdomen?
- Does it burn or hurt when she passes urine?
- Is she passing urine more often than usual?
- Does she have fever alongside the pain?
- Does her urine look cloudy or smell strongly?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Loin or flank pain with fever
- Urinary symptoms present
- Cloudy or offensive urine
- Prior UTI during this pregnancy

**Uncertainty note:**
Pyelonephritis in pregnancy can rapidly progress to sepsis and trigger preterm labour. Any fever with flank pain must be escalated for treatment, not managed at home.

---

### DISEASE 7: ROUND LIGAMENT PAIN
**Nigerian priority level: MEDIUM**

Round ligament pain is a common benign cause of sharp lower abdominal pain in pregnancy, caused by stretching of the ligaments supporting the uterus. It is a diagnosis of exclusion and must only be applied after dangerous causes have been excluded.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Sharp, brief, one-sided lower abdominal pain
- Occurs on sudden movement: standing up quickly, rolling over in bed, coughing, or sneezing
- Lasts only seconds to a few minutes then resolves completely
- No fever, no bleeding, no fetal movement changes
- Usually in second trimester
- Not constant and not progressive

**Differentiating questions:**
- Does the pain happen when she moves suddenly, stands up quickly, or coughs?
- Does it last only a few seconds and then go away completely?
- Is there any fever, bleeding, or change in fetal movement?
- Is the pain between 14 and 28 weeks of pregnancy?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Brief sharp one-sided pain triggered by movement
- Resolves within seconds to minutes
- No associated symptoms
- Second trimester

**Uncertainty note:**
Round ligament pain must never be assumed if the pain is constant, severe, or associated with any other symptom. If there is any doubt, escalate. Do not use this diagnosis to reassure in the third trimester without facility assessment.

---

### DISEASE 8: MALARIA
**Nigerian priority level: HIGHEST**

Malaria commonly causes abdominal pain in pregnancy, particularly in severe malaria. Splenic enlargement and hepatomegaly from malaria cause left and right upper quadrant discomfort. Malaria should always be in the differential for abdominal pain with fever.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Fever present alongside abdominal pain
- Upper abdominal fullness or pain from spleen or liver involvement
- Accompanied by chills, headache, and body aches
- Cyclical fever pattern
- Anaemia signs

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is there fever alongside the abdominal pain?
- Does the pain come with chills and sweating?
- Is the pain in the upper part of the abdomen?
- Has she had malaria previously with similar symptoms?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Fever with abdominal pain
- Upper abdominal component
- Cyclical fever pattern
- Anaemia signs present

**Uncertainty note:**
Malaria and appendicitis, malaria and pyelonephritis, and malaria and chorioamnionitis can coexist. Fever with abdominal pain must always trigger facility evaluation regardless of malaria suspicion.

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE — ABDOMINAL PAIN IN PREGNANCY

Severe, constant, or worsening abdominal pain in pregnancy is an emergency until proven otherwise. Any abdominal pain associated with fever, vaginal bleeding, reduced fetal movement, or signs of shock requires immediate escalation. Benign causes such as round ligament pain and constipation must only be considered after dangerous causes have been excluded at a facility. When the cause is unclear or cannot be safely excluded remotely, escalate.
