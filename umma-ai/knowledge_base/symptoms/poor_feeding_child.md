# SYMPTOM FILE: Poor Feeding — Child
**Category:** symptoms | **Subcategory:** child | **Topic:** poor_feeding

---

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### Confirming the Symptom
- Caregiver reports child is not feeding as usual — reduced intake compared to baseline
- Infant refusing breast or bottle — pulling away, not latching, feeding for short periods only
- Older child refusing meals or eating significantly less than normal
- Child takes a few sips or bites then stops
- Caregiver has noticed the child is losing weight or not gaining weight
- Child previously feeding well but has stopped — acute onset vs. gradual onset matters

### Commonly Confused Symptoms
- **Normal appetite variation** — children have day-to-day variation; clarify duration and degree of change
- **Teething** — infant fussy with feeding around 4–7 months; drooling, gum swelling, mild fussiness; no systemic illness
- **Food refusal (behavioural)** — selective eating in toddlers; child eats preferred foods but refuses others; growing and active
- **Breastfeeding difficulty** — poor latch, low milk supply, painful nipples; distinguish from infant rejecting feeds due to illness
- **Vomiting** — child may feed but vomit immediately; confirm whether refusal precedes or follows vomiting

### Critical Immediate Checks
- Age of child — infant under 3 months with any feeding problem is high priority
- Is there fever alongside the feeding problem?
- Is the child able to swallow — or does swallowing appear painful?
- Is the child lethargic, limp, or unresponsive?
- Signs of dehydration: sunken fontanelle, sunken eyes, dry mouth, no tears when crying, reduced urine
- Has the child passed urine in the last 6–8 hours?
- Newborn: has the child ever fed well? Or has feeding been a problem from birth?

---

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

*Escalate immediately if any of the following are present:*

1. Child is unable to feed at all — not swallowing anything — EMERGENCY
2. Child is lethargic, limp, or cannot be woken normally
3. Infant under 3 months with any feeding difficulty and fever — EMERGENCY
4. Signs of severe dehydration — sunken eyes, sunken fontanelle, no urine for 8+ hours, dry mouth, skin tenting
5. Child with rapid breathing alongside poor feeding — possible pneumonia
6. Child appears to be in pain when swallowing
7. Visible difficulty breathing while feeding — possible cardiac or respiratory cause
8. Newborn who has never fed well since birth — possible congenital problem or neonatal sepsis
9. Child with poor feeding after a period of illness — suggests prolonged illness with nutritional deterioration
10. Signs of severe acute malnutrition — visible severe wasting, oedema of feet and legs, MUAC below 11.5 cm
11. Caregiver reports child is the sickest they have ever seen
12. Poor feeding with persistent vomiting and diarrhoea — high dehydration risk

---

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

---

### 1. MALARIA
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
Malaria is one of the most common causes of acute reduction in feeding in children in Nigeria. The combination of fever, malaise, vomiting, and general misery suppresses appetite dramatically.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Fever present — may be high, may be cyclical
- Child is generally unwell — not just refusing food but lethargic, miserable
- Vomiting common — reduces ability and willingness to feed
- Pallor from haemolytic anaemia
- Child has been well previously; acute onset over 1–3 days
- May have had malaria before

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Does the child have fever alongside the feeding problem?
- Is the child vomiting as well?
- Is the child pale — are the lips and inside the eyelids pale?
- How quickly did the feeding problem start?
- Has the child had malaria before?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Acute poor feeding + fever + vomiting + pallor + malaria-endemic area

**Uncertainty Note:**
In a febrile child who suddenly stops feeding, malaria must be excluded first. Do not attribute poor feeding to other causes until malaria has been assessed.

---

### 2. PNEUMONIA
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
Pneumonia frequently presents with poor feeding in children, particularly in infants, as feeding requires significant respiratory effort.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Rapid breathing — critical sign: over 60 breaths per minute in infants under 2 months, over 50 in 2–12 months, over 40 in children 1–5 years
- Chest indrawing — lower chest pulls inward with each breath
- Fever present
- Cough — may or may not be present; pneumonia can occur without obvious cough in young infants
- Child becomes breathless while feeding — pulls away from breast or bottle
- Nasal flaring, grunting

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is the child breathing faster than normal?
- Does the chest pull inward when the child breathes in?
- Does the child get more breathless or sweaty during feeding?
- Is the child coughing?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Poor feeding + fast breathing + fever + chest indrawing = pneumonia — urgent referral

**Uncertainty Note:**
Respiratory rate is the most sensitive sign of pneumonia in children. Fast breathing with poor feeding in a febrile child must be treated as pneumonia until proven otherwise.

---

### 3. SEVERE ACUTE MALNUTRITION (SAM)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
Anorexia is a hallmark of SAM — children with kwashiorkor or marasmus lose the desire to eat.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Progressive decline in feeding over weeks to months
- Visible severe wasting (marasmus) — skin hanging loosely, visible ribs
- OR oedema with distension (kwashiorkor) — bilateral foot oedema, abdominal swelling
- Child appears apathetic, withdrawn, not interested in surroundings
- MUAC below 11.5 cm
- Poor growth — caregiver may report child has not gained weight for months
- Frequent intercurrent infections

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Has the child's appetite been declining over a long time — weeks or months?
- Is the child visibly thinner than before?
- Are the child's feet swollen?
- What does the child eat — what is a typical day of food?
- Has the child been gaining weight normally?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Chronic progressive anorexia + visible wasting or oedema + poor diet + growth faltering = SAM

**Uncertainty Note:**
SAM with concurrent infection (malaria, pneumonia, sepsis) has very high mortality. Any febrile child with SAM and poor feeding must be referred immediately for inpatient management.

---

### 4. NEONATAL SEPSIS / SERIOUS BACTERIAL INFECTION (Young Infant)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST — EMERGENCY**
In infants under 2 months, poor feeding is one of the cardinal signs of serious bacterial infection (SBI) or neonatal sepsis.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Age under 2 months — particularly neonates under 28 days
- Infant who was feeding normally and has suddenly stopped or is feeding poorly
- May have fever OR hypothermia — either is dangerous in a neonate
- Lethargic, floppy, not moving normally
- May have umbilical redness, discharge, or foul smell (omphalitis)
- May have skin pustules
- Fast breathing or convulsions

**Differentiating Questions:**
- How old is the baby exactly?
- Was the baby feeding normally before and has suddenly changed?
- Does the baby have fever or does the baby feel cold?
- Is the baby limp or floppy?
- Is the area around the belly button red or smelly?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Infant under 2 months + poor feeding + fever or hypothermia OR lethargy = neonatal SBI — EMERGENCY REFERRAL

**Uncertainty Note:**
In an infant under 2 months, poor feeding alone (without fever) can be the only sign of serious infection. Do not wait for other signs to develop before referring.

---

### 5. ORAL THRUSH (Candidiasis)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH**
Common in infants, particularly those who are immunocompromised, malnourished, or have received antibiotics.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- White patches on tongue, inner cheeks, and palate — cannot be wiped off easily
- Infant pulls away from breast or bottle during feeding and cries
- Feeding was normal before onset
- No fever unless secondary infection
- Mother may also have nipple pain from candidal mastitis
- Common in HIV-exposed or HIV-positive infants

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Are there white patches inside the baby's mouth — on the tongue or cheeks?
- Does the baby pull away and cry during feeds as if in pain?
- Has the baby recently had antibiotics?
- Is the mother having nipple pain during breastfeeding?

**Confirm Towards If:**
White oral patches + pain on feeding + pulling away = oral thrush

**Uncertainty Note:**
Recurrent or severe oral thrush in an infant should prompt assessment for HIV exposure and immunosuppression.

---

### 6. GASTROENTERITIS WITH DEHYDRATION
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
Acute gastroenteritis is a leading cause of childhood mortality in Nigeria. Poor feeding combined with ongoing fluid losses rapidly leads to severe dehydration.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Vomiting and diarrhoea present alongside poor feeding
- Signs of dehydration: sunken eyes, dry mouth, reduced urine, sunken fontanelle, slow skin return
- Child is thirsty or cannot drink due to vomiting
- Fever may be present
- Progression can be rapid in young children

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is the child having diarrhoea or vomiting alongside the feeding problem?
- How many times has the child passed loose stool today?
- Are the child's eyes sunken?
- Is the child passing urine normally?
- Are the child's lips and mouth dry?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Poor feeding + vomiting + diarrhoea + dehydration signs = gastroenteritis with dehydration — urgent management

**Uncertainty Note:**
A child who cannot keep any fluid down is at risk of severe dehydration and requires urgent clinical assessment. Encourage ORS by small sips; if vomiting continues, refer.

---

### 7. MENINGITIS / SERIOUS CNS INFECTION
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH — EMERGENCY**
Meningitis in young children often presents subtly, with poor feeding being one of the early signs before more obvious neurological features emerge.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- High fever with poor feeding and marked irritability or lethargy
- Bulging fontanelle in infants
- High-pitched cry in infants
- Neck stiffness (may be subtle or absent in young infants)
- Photophobia
- Seizures in the illness episode
- Rapid deterioration from looking mildly unwell to critically ill

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is the child unusually difficult to wake or not responding normally?
- Is the soft spot on the baby's head bulging or tense?
- Is the child's cry different from normal — high-pitched or weak?
- Has the child had any convulsions?
- Is the child sensitive to light?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Poor feeding + fever + bulging fontanelle OR stiff neck OR high-pitched cry OR seizures = meningitis — EMERGENCY

**Uncertainty Note:**
Meningitis can be difficult to distinguish from other serious infections in young infants. If meningitis cannot be excluded in a febrile infant with poor feeding and neurological signs, refer immediately.

---

## GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE

Poor feeding in a child that is accompanied by any of the following must be escalated to clinic or hospital:
- Fever of any degree
- Rapid breathing or visible respiratory effort
- Lethargy or altered responsiveness
- Visible severe wasting or oedema
- Signs of dehydration
- Age under 3 months with any feeding concern
- Duration more than 24 hours without improvement

**In northern Nigeria, malaria and pneumonia are the two most common serious causes of acute poor feeding in children and must always be considered first. SAM with anorexia is common and carries high mortality if complicated by infection.**
