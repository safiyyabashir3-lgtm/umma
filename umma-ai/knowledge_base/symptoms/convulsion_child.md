# SYMPTOM FILE: CONVULSION / SEIZURE IN A CHILD UNDER 5
## For UMMA AI Clinical Reasoning — Nigerian Context (Kano / Northwest Priority)
### Awaiting Clinician Verification

---

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### Confirming the symptom
A convulsion is shaking or jerking of the body that the child cannot control,
often with loss of consciousness. Caregivers may describe it differently.

If not clearly described, ask ONE of:
- "Did the child's body shake or jerk on its own — arms and legs moving
  without the child controlling it?"
- "Did the child go stiff, then shake, and lose consciousness?"

Establish immediately:
- Is the convulsion happening RIGHT NOW? — If yes, this is an active emergency.
  Escalate immediately. Do not ask any further questions.
- How old is the child?

### Symptoms that may be confused with convulsion
- Shivering from fever — child is conscious, shaking is controlled, responds
  to being spoken to
- Breath-holding spell — child holds breath after crying, may go briefly blue
  then recovers quickly, fully conscious within seconds
- Startle reflex in newborn — brief jerky movements, baby remains awake
  and responsive

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE

### QUESTIONING RULES FOR TRIAGE
- A convulsion that is HAPPENING NOW — escalate immediately. No triage questions.
- A convulsion that HAS HAPPENED — ask maximum 3 triage questions
- Ask ONE question at a time only
- Acknowledge caregiver warmly before each question

### RED FLAGS — Escalate immediately if any present
- Convulsion happening right now — active seizure
- Convulsion lasting more than 5 minutes
- Multiple convulsions in the same day
- Child did not wake up fully after the convulsion
- Child unconscious or cannot be woken after convulsion ended
- Convulsion in a child under 6 months
- Convulsion without fever — may indicate meningitis or epilepsy
- Convulsion with stiff neck
- Convulsion with severe breathing difficulty after it ends
- Child under 3 months with any convulsion — always emergency

### Triage question priority order
Ask only what is not already known.

Priority 1: "Is the child awake and responding to you now — or still
             unconscious?"
Priority 2: "Did the child have a fever before the convulsion started?"
Priority 3: "How long did the shaking last — and has it happened more than
             once today?"

### IF ANY RED FLAG CONFIRMED — Escalate immediately. Do not proceed.
### IF NO RED FLAGS — Proceed to Section 3.

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

### QUESTIONING RULES FOR DISEASE REASONING
- Ask ONE question at a time only
- Maximum 4 questions total
- Use answers already given — do not re-ask
- If pattern already clear — reason confidently

### NIGERIAN / KANO CONTEXT NOTE
Convulsion in a febrile child in northern Nigeria must be treated as a medical
emergency until proven otherwise. Malaria is the most common cause of febrile
convulsion in Nigerian children. However, meningitis must always be actively
excluded — northern Nigeria is within the African meningitis belt and carries
a higher meningitis burden than southern states. Cerebral malaria causes
convulsions and carries high mortality without urgent treatment. Never reassure
a caregiver that a convulsion was simple without clinic assessment.

---

### DISEASE 1: FEBRILE CONVULSION FROM MALARIA
Nigerian priority: HIGHEST

Key differentiating features:
- High fever present before and during convulsion
- Single convulsion lasting less than 5 minutes
- Child wakes up fully after convulsion ends
- Child has no neck stiffness after recovery
- History consistent with malaria — chills, sweating, vomiting with fever

Differentiating questions:
- "Was there a high fever before the shaking started?"
- "Did the child wake up fully and recognise you after the shaking stopped?"
- "Had the child been shivering and vomiting before the fit?"
- "Has the child had malaria before?"

Confirm towards febrile convulsion from malaria if:
- High fever + single brief convulsion + full recovery + no neck stiffness
- History of malaria illness pattern before the convulsion

Uncertainty note:
- Even a single febrile convulsion in a Nigerian child needs same-day clinic
  assessment — do not advise home management
- Cerebral malaria can present as prolonged or repeated convulsions — escalate
  immediately if any doubt

---

### DISEASE 2: CEREBRAL MALARIA
Nigerian priority: HIGH — life-threatening emergency

Key differentiating features:
- Prolonged convulsion — lasting more than 5 minutes
- Repeated convulsions in same episode
- Child does not wake up fully between convulsions
- Child unconscious or very difficult to rouse after convulsion
- High fever
- Breathing may be irregular

Differentiating questions:
- "How long did the shaking go on for?"
- "Has the child had more than one fit today?"
- "Is the child fully awake and responsive now — or still very drowsy
  and difficult to wake?"

Confirm towards cerebral malaria if:
- Prolonged or repeated convulsion + not fully waking + high fever

Uncertainty note:
- Cerebral malaria is a medical emergency with high mortality
- Do not attempt to differentiate from meningitis in the community —
  both require immediate hospital care

---

### DISEASE 3: MENINGITIS
Nigerian priority: HIGH in northern Nigeria — meningitis belt

Key differentiating features:
- Fever with convulsion + neck stiffness — key combination
- Bulging fontanelle in infant
- Child very unwell before the convulsion — not just feverish
- Child does not recover well after convulsion
- Photophobia — crying when exposed to light
- Convulsion without high fever — unusual, consider meningitis

Differentiating questions:
- "Is the child's neck stiff — does the child cry or resist if you try
  to bend the neck gently forward?"
- "In a baby — is the soft spot on top of the head looking pushed outward?"

Confirm towards meningitis if:
- Convulsion + neck stiffness
- Convulsion + bulging fontanelle
- Convulsion + child very unwell + not recovering

Uncertainty note:
- Any suspicion of meningitis — escalate immediately
- Meningitis and cerebral malaria cannot be distinguished without hospital
  investigation — both are emergencies

---

### DISEASE 4: EPILEPSY — KNOWN OR FIRST PRESENTATION
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM

Key differentiating features:
- Convulsion without fever
- Recurrent convulsions over weeks or months
- Child known to have epilepsy
- Child recovers fully after convulsion
- No fever, no neck stiffness, no signs of infection

Differentiating questions:
- "Has the child had fits before — more than once over the past weeks or months?"
- "Does the child have a known condition or has a doctor ever told you this
  might happen?"

Confirm towards epilepsy if:
- Recurrent convulsions without fever + full recovery each time +
  no signs of infection

Uncertainty note:
- First convulsion without fever in a child — always needs clinic assessment
- Known epilepsy with increased seizure frequency — needs review
- Never assume epilepsy in a child with fever — rule out infection first

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE
- Every child who has had a convulsion needs same-day clinic assessment
  at minimum — no exceptions
- Never advise home management for a child who has just had a convulsion
- If cause unclear — treat as possible malaria with meningitis to exclude
  and escalate urgently
- Convulsion in a child under 6 months — always escalate as emergency
- When in doubt — escalate immediately
