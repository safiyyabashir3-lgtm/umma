## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### Confirming the symptom
A convulsion is a medical emergency. When a caregiver reports a convulsion, the immediate
clinical priority is to establish whether the child is still convulsing now, has just stopped,
or convulsed earlier and has recovered. This determines the urgency of the response before
any further assessment.

Caregivers in northern Nigeria may describe a convulsion in several ways. They may use the
word "farfadiya" (though this sometimes refers to chronic epilepsy specifically — context
clarifies), or describe the child as "ya yi fargaba" (had a fit), shaking uncontrollably,
stiffening, eyes rolling back, not responding, or losing consciousness. All of these
descriptions are a convulsion until proven otherwise.

Confirm three things before anything else:
1. Is the child convulsing right now?
2. Has the child regained consciousness?
3. Is the child breathing?

If the child is convulsing now — this is an active emergency. Escalate immediately. There
is no disease reasoning to perform. The response is: go to hospital now.

If the child has stopped convulsing and is breathing and conscious — proceed through triage
to determine cause and urgency.

Establish age immediately:
- Any convulsion in a child under 3 months — emergency; not a simple febrile convulsion.
- Any convulsion in a child under 6 months — serious; threshold for escalation is lower.

### What is a convulsion vs what may be confused with it
- Febrile rigors — shivering with high fever; child remains conscious, responds to voice,
  does not have rhythmic jerking of limbs; not a convulsion
- Breath-holding spells — child holds breath after crying or fright, may briefly lose
  consciousness or go rigid; always precipitated by crying or upset; brief; not a convulsion
- Jitteriness in newborns — trembling or fine shaking that stops when limb is held still;
  different from true clonic movements which persist when held
- Startle response in infants — reflex response to sudden noise; brief, symmetrical, self-limiting

### Local language and cultural context
- Farfadiya — convulsion / fits / epilepsy (used for both acute episodes and chronic epilepsy
  in Kano — context clarifies which is meant)
- Ya yi fargaba — had a fit / convulsion
- Yana rawar jiki sosai — shaking severely (may describe a convulsion)
- Ya suma — lost consciousness / fainted (may accompany or follow a convulsion)
- Idanunsa sun juya — eyes rolled back (classic convulsion description)
- Bai san komai ba — not knowing anything / unresponsive (may describe postictal state)
- Ba ya motsi — not moving (may describe postictal unresponsiveness)

Convulsions in children in northern Nigeria are frequently attributed to spiritual causes,
evil eye (ido), or teething. These beliefs are deeply held and must not be dismissed. Acknowledge
the caregiver's framing respectfully, and simultaneously make clear that the child needs medical
assessment now, because the physical cause of the convulsion needs to be treated.

Many caregivers in Kano place the child on the floor, hold them, or put objects in the mouth
during a convulsion. Placing objects in the mouth is dangerous and should be addressed.

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

A convulsion itself is a red flag. The question is not whether to escalate — it is how urgently.

Escalate as emergency immediately if any of the following are present:

- Child is convulsing right now — active ongoing convulsion
- Child has not regained consciousness after the convulsion stopped
- Child is not breathing or breathing is very laboured after the convulsion
- Convulsion lasted more than 5 minutes
- More than one convulsion in the past 24 hours
- Convulsion in a child under 3 months — always emergency
- Convulsion with no fever — non-febrile convulsion is always more serious; may indicate
  meningitis, metabolic problem, or epilepsy
- Convulsion with stiff neck — possible meningitis
- Convulsion with bulging fontanelle in an infant
- Convulsion with petechiae or purpura — skin spots that do not fade when pressed
- Convulsion in a child who is known to be severely ill — malaria, pneumonia, meningitis
- Convulsion in a malnourished or very sick-looking child
- First-ever convulsion in any child — always needs hospital assessment to determine cause
- Caregiver unable to rouse the child after the episode

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

### Nigerian and Kano context
Convulsions in children under 5 in northern Nigeria are most commonly caused by febrile illness —
and in Nigeria, febrile illness means malaria until proven otherwise. Cerebral malaria is one
of the most dangerous and fastest-moving medical emergencies in Nigerian paediatrics. It kills
within hours. A child with convulsion and fever in Kano has cerebral malaria until proven
otherwise at hospital.

Bacterial meningitis is the second most dangerous cause and equally time-critical. These two
conditions cannot be reliably distinguished without hospital investigation. Both are emergencies.

Simple febrile convulsions do exist — they are brief (under 5 minutes), generalised, occur
once in a febrile illness, and the child recovers fully — but they cannot be assumed in
Nigeria without ruling out malaria and meningitis. The safety margin for calling something
a "simple febrile convulsion" and managing at home is much narrower in this context than
in high-income settings.

Malnutrition increases the risk of hypoglycaemia (low blood sugar), which can cause convulsions
independently of fever.

---

### DISEASE 1: CEREBRAL MALARIA
Nigerian priority: HIGHEST — the most dangerous and most common cause of convulsion with
fever in Nigerian children under 5

Key differentiating features:
- Fever — present in virtually all cases; may have been present for days before convulsion
- Convulsion — may be focal or generalised
- Impaired or lost consciousness — child cannot be roused or is difficult to rouse after
  the convulsion; this is a defining feature of cerebral malaria
- Repeated convulsions — more than one in 24 hours strongly suggests cerebral malaria
- Pallor — severe anaemia frequently coexists; inner eyelids white or very pale
- Child had fever with vomiting for days before convulsion — typical history
- Rapid deterioration — child worsening over hours
- Jaundice may be present — yellowing of eyes

Differentiating questions:
- Has the child had fever for the past few days?
- Has the child had more than one fit?
- Is the child still unconscious or very difficult to rouse?
- Are the inside of the child's eyelids or palms very pale?
- Has the child been vomiting and refusing to eat?

Confirm towards cerebral malaria if:
- Convulsion + fever + impaired consciousness regardless of other findings
- Convulsion + fever + pallor + repeated episodes
- Any convulsion + fever in a Nigerian child under 5 — cerebral malaria must be excluded

Uncertainty note:
- Cerebral malaria and bacterial meningitis cannot be distinguished clinically —
  both require emergency hospital care and investigation
- Do not attempt to manage cerebral malaria at home under any circumstances
- Repeated convulsions in a febrile child — immediate emergency, every minute matters

---

### DISEASE 2: BACTERIAL MENINGITIS
Nigerian priority: HIGHEST alongside cerebral malaria — equally urgent, equally fatal if delayed

Key differentiating features:
- Fever
- Stiff neck — may be absent in infants under 12 months; presence confirms urgency
- Bulging fontanelle in infants
- Extreme irritability — child cannot be comforted, screams when touched or moved
- High-pitched cry in infants
- Photophobia — turns away from light
- Projectile vomiting
- Petechiae or purpura — small red or purple spots that do not fade when pressed
  (meningococcal disease — immediate emergency)
- Rapid deterioration over hours
- Convulsion may be focal

Differentiating questions:
- Is the child's neck stiff — does the child resist or cry when the head is bent forward?
- In an infant — is the soft spot on the top of the head pushed outward?
- Is the child extremely irritable and impossible to calm even between fits?
- Are there any spots on the skin that don't fade when you press on them?

Confirm towards bacterial meningitis if:
- Convulsion + stiff neck + fever
- Convulsion + bulging fontanelle + fever in an infant
- Convulsion + petechiae — immediate emergency

Uncertainty note:
- Meningitis and cerebral malaria are clinically indistinguishable at the bedside —
  both are emergencies requiring immediate hospital care
- Do not wait for stiff neck — its absence in infants does not exclude meningitis
- If petechiae present — this is among the most time-critical emergencies in paediatrics

---

### DISEASE 3: SIMPLE FEBRILE CONVULSION
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM — exists in Nigeria but must only be considered after cerebral
malaria and meningitis have been excluded at hospital

Key differentiating features:
- Age 6 months to 5 years — peak incidence 18 months to 3 years
- Fever present — convulsion triggered by rapid rise in body temperature
- Convulsion brief — typically less than 5 minutes
- Convulsion generalised — involves whole body, not one limb or side
- Single episode — only one convulsion in this febrile illness
- Full recovery — child returns to normal consciousness and behaviour within 30 to 60 minutes
- Child looks well after recovery — not excessively drowsy or confused beyond normal
  post-seizure tiredness
- No stiff neck, no abnormal posturing, no focal neurological signs

Differentiating questions:
- How long did the convulsion last?
- Was it the first fit this illness or has there been more than one?
- Has the child returned to normal — recognising you, responding to voice?
- Does the child have any stiffness in the neck?

Confirm towards simple febrile convulsion if:
- Age 6 months to 5 years + fever + brief generalised convulsion + single episode
  + full recovery + no neck stiffness + no abnormal neurological signs

Uncertainty note:
- Simple febrile convulsion is a diagnosis of exclusion in Nigeria — cerebral malaria
  and meningitis must be excluded at hospital before this label is applied
- Even a child who appears to have had a simple febrile convulsion must be seen at
  hospital on the same day — the underlying fever cause must be identified and treated
- Never advise home management after a first convulsion in a Nigerian child

---

### DISEASE 4: HYPOGLYCAEMIA
Nigerian priority: HIGH — malnutrition is endemic in Kano; hypoglycaemia is a common
and underrecognised cause of convulsion in malnourished and severely ill children

Key differentiating features:
- Child is visibly malnourished — thin, wasted, or oedematous
- Convulsion may occur with or without fever
- Pallor and sweating
- Child very weak and lethargic before and after convulsion
- Child had been unable to feed or was severely unwell for several days
- May occur as a complication of severe malaria, severe infection, or prolonged fasting

Differentiating questions:
- Is the child visibly thin or wasted?
- Has the child been unable to eat or drink for the past day or more?
- Has the child been very sick for several days before the fit?

Confirm towards hypoglycaemia if:
- Convulsion + visibly malnourished child + prolonged inability to feed
- Convulsion in severe malaria or severe infection — hypoglycaemia frequently coexists

Uncertainty note:
- Hypoglycaemia cannot be confirmed without blood glucose measurement — only at clinic
- Treatment of hypoglycaemia requires medical supervision — glucose given incorrectly
  at home can cause harm
- In any malnourished child with convulsion — hypoglycaemia must be excluded at hospital

---

### DISEASE 5: EPILEPSY — RECURRENT UNPROVOKED SEIZURES
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM — chronic condition; relevant if child has had multiple episodes
without fever over time

Key differentiating features:
- History of multiple previous convulsions — not always with fever
- Episodes occurring at rest or without obvious trigger
- Child may have a known diagnosis of epilepsy already
- Family history of epilepsy may be present
- Convulsions of consistent pattern — same type each time
- Normal or near-normal recovery between episodes
- No fever during all episodes

Differentiating questions:
- Has the child had convulsions before — multiple times?
- Did the fits always happen with fever, or have some happened without fever?
- Does the child have a known diagnosis already?
- Is there a family history of epilepsy or recurrent fits?

Confirm towards epilepsy if:
- Multiple previous convulsions + some without fever + consistent pattern over time

Uncertainty note:
- Epilepsy in a child in Nigeria still needs hospital assessment to exclude treatable
  causes and to consider appropriate management
- During an acute episode — the immediate cause must still be excluded, especially malaria
- Do not apply an epilepsy label to dismiss the urgency of a current convulsion

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE
- Active convulsion — escalate as emergency immediately. No further reasoning.
- Any convulsion in a child under 3 months — emergency regardless of all other findings
- Any convulsion with fever in Nigeria — cerebral malaria and meningitis must be excluded
  at hospital; no exceptions
- Any first-ever convulsion regardless of features — always needs same-day hospital assessment
- Non-febrile convulsion — always more concerning than febrile; escalate urgently
- When in doubt — escalate immediately. Convulsion in a Nigerian child under 5 is an
  emergency until proven otherwise at hospital.
