# SYMPTOM FILE: Postpartum Fever

**File ID:** symptom_postpartum_fever
**Version:** 1.0

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### What counts as postpartum fever?
- Any reported fever — feeling hot, shivering, rigors, sweating — in a woman within 6 weeks of delivery
- Temperature of 38°C or above on two occasions more than 4 hours apart after the first 24 hours postpartum (standard definition), but clinically any fever after delivery should be taken seriously
- Fever in the first 24 hours postpartum is most commonly due to physiological changes or breast engorgement, but infection must not be assumed absent
- Fever after 24 hours is infection until proven otherwise

### Commonly confused symptoms — verify carefully
- **Breast engorgement** — low-grade fever and breast discomfort in days 2–4 postpartum as milk comes in; but mastitis and breast abscess must be excluded if fever persists or breast is red or very painful
- **Normal postpartum sweating** — excessive sweating is common postpartum without true fever; confirm whether she feels genuinely hot or cold and shivering, not just sweating
- **Malaria** — in Nigeria, postpartum fever is malaria until proven otherwise; malaria is the most common cause of fever in any Nigerian woman of reproductive age and must always be the first differential
- **Anaemia-related symptoms** — pallor, weakness, and palpitations can accompany fever; distinguish from fever caused by anaemia alone

### Critical immediate checks
- **Time since delivery** — establish immediately; fever within first 24 hours vs after 24 hours has different differential weighting, though both are urgent
- **Mode of delivery** — vaginal or caesarean section; caesarean section significantly increases risk of wound infection, endometritis, and UTI
- **Any bleeding** — fever with heavy bleeding raises risk of endometritis or retained products
- **Any signs of severe illness** — confusion, inability to sit up, rapid breathing, cold extremities — these indicate possible sepsis and are immediate emergencies
- **Breastfeeding status** — breast symptoms must be assessed if fever is present and woman is breastfeeding

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

Listed from most to least dangerous:

1. **Confusion, disorientation, loss of consciousness, or inability to respond normally** — septic shock or cerebral malaria; immediate emergency
2. **Very rapid breathing or difficulty breathing** — sepsis with respiratory compromise or severe malaria
3. **Cold, clammy, or mottled skin with fever** — shock; immediate emergency
4. **Heavy vaginal bleeding with fever** — endometritis with haemorrhage or retained products with infection; immediate emergency
5. **Fever with severe lower abdominal pain and uterus feels tender** — endometritis or pelvic sepsis
6. **Fever with foul-smelling vaginal discharge or lochia** — endometritis; high risk of progression to sepsis
7. **Fever with reduced or no urine output** — sepsis with renal involvement or severe malaria
8. **Fever with fitting (convulsions)** — cerebral malaria or eclampsia (if within first week postpartum)
9. **Fever with headache and neck stiffness or sensitivity to light** — meningitis must be excluded
10. **Fever with caesarean section wound that is red, swollen, discharging, or opening** — wound infection or dehiscence
11. **Breast that is red, very painful, hot, and hard with fever** — mastitis progressing to abscess
12. **Fever with calf pain, leg swelling, or sudden chest pain or breathlessness** — deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism
13. **Fever not improving after 24–48 hours in a woman being seen at a facility** — treatment failure or undiscovered source
14. **Woman or family member feels she is very unwell or deteriorating** — uncertainty always escalates

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

### 3.1 Malaria
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGHEST**
Malaria is the first differential for any fever in a postpartum Nigerian woman. Pregnancy-related immune suppression and anaemia persist in the early postpartum period, making women highly vulnerable to severe malaria. *Plasmodium falciparum* predominates in Nigeria. Postpartum malaria can deteriorate rapidly to severe malaria and cerebral malaria.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Fever — may be high-grade, cyclical, or continuous
- Rigors and chills
- Headache — often significant
- Generalised body aches and malaise
- Nausea, vomiting, and poor appetite
- No localised source of infection (no uterine tenderness, no wound problems, no breast symptoms)
- May have anaemia (pallor, weakness, dizziness)
- Onset can be within days of delivery or weeks later
- History of inadequate IPTp during pregnancy increases risk

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the fever coming in waves or constant?
- Are there rigors — shaking and feeling very cold before the fever?
- Any headache? How severe?
- Any body aches?
- Did she take her malaria prevention medications (IPTp) during pregnancy?
- Any localised pain — tummy, breast, wound?
- Any confusion or unusual behaviour?

**Confirm towards if:**
Fever + rigors/chills + headache + body aches + no localised infection source = malaria until RDT or blood film proves otherwise

**Uncertainty note:**
A negative RDT does not fully exclude malaria in high-transmission settings if clinical suspicion is high. Malaria and another infection can co-exist. If RDT is negative but clinical picture fits malaria, repeat testing or treat empirically based on clinical guidelines.

### 3.2 Endometritis (Postpartum Uterine Infection)
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGHEST**
Endometritis is infection of the uterine lining and is the commonest cause of non-malarial postpartum sepsis in Nigeria. It is more common after caesarean section but occurs after vaginal delivery too — especially following prolonged labour, multiple vaginal examinations, manual removal of placenta, or retained products. Leading cause of maternal death from infection in Nigeria.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Fever — usually onset day 2–5 postpartum
- Lower abdominal pain and uterine tenderness
- Foul-smelling or purulent lochia (vaginal discharge after delivery)
- Lochia may be excessive or abnormal in appearance
- Uterus may feel large or boggy on examination
- Associated with caesarean delivery, prolonged labour, or PROM

**Differentiating questions:**
- How does her vaginal discharge (lochia) smell — is there a bad odour?
- Is the lochia heavier or more abnormal than expected?
- Is there pain or tenderness in the lower abdomen?
- How was the delivery — vaginal or caesarean? Were there any complications?
- Was there prolonged labour or prolonged ruptured membranes?
- Did she need the placenta removed manually?

**Confirm towards if:**
Fever + uterine/lower abdominal tenderness + offensive lochia + postpartum = endometritis

**Uncertainty note:**
If offensive lochia is present with fever, treat as endometritis regardless of whether abdominal tenderness is confirmed. Do not await full clinical picture to escalate.

### 3.3 Mastitis / Breast Abscess
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGH**
Mastitis affects breastfeeding women, typically in the first 2–6 weeks postpartum. Staphylococcus aureus is the commonest organism. In Nigeria, access to early treatment is often delayed, and mastitis frequently progresses to abscess. Breast abscess requires surgical drainage.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Fever with localised breast symptoms: redness, swelling, warmth, pain in one breast
- A hard, tender area or lump in the breast
- Breast abscess: fluctuant swelling, very tender, may have pus
- Typically unilateral
- Associated with engorgement, missed feeds, cracked nipples, or poor latch
- Woman is breastfeeding

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is one or both breasts red, hot, or painful?
- Is there a specific hard lump or swollen area in the breast?
- Is there any discharge from the nipple that looks like pus?
- Is she breastfeeding, and has she had any difficulty with feeding?
- How long has the breast been sore?
- Has she had any cracked or sore nipples?

**Confirm towards if:**
Fever + unilateral breast redness/warmth/swelling/pain + breastfeeding = mastitis; if fluctuant lump = abscess

**Uncertainty note:**
Breast redness and pain without fever may still be mastitis. Mastitis that does not improve with 24–48 hours of treatment should be reassessed for abscess formation.

### 3.4 Urinary Tract Infection / Pyelonephritis
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGH**
UTI is common postpartum, particularly after catheterisation during labour or caesarean section. Pyelonephritis can develop rapidly and cause sepsis. Often the presenting symptom is fever, with urinary symptoms noted only on direct questioning.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Fever — may be high if pyelonephritis
- Dysuria, urinary frequency, or urgency
- Loin or back pain if upper urinary tract involved (pyelonephritis)
- History of catheterisation or prolonged catheter use
- Suprapubic discomfort

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is there burning or pain when passing urine?
- How often is she urinating?
- Any back or side pain?
- Did she have a catheter during labour or delivery, and for how long?
- Does the urine look dark, cloudy, or smell strongly?

**Confirm towards if:**
Fever + dysuria or frequency + loin pain = pyelonephritis; fever + dysuria + no loin pain = lower UTI

**Uncertainty note:**
Postpartum fever without clear source should include UTI in the differential. Urinary symptoms may be mild or overlooked postpartum.

### 3.5 Wound Infection (Caesarean Section or Perineal)
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGH**
Surgical site infections following caesarean section are common in Nigeria due to high surgical load, limited antiseptic resources, and frequent use of non-standard wound closure materials. Perineal wound infections (episiotomy, lacerations) also occur. Both can progress to necrotising fasciitis in severe cases.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Fever with pain, redness, swelling, warmth at wound site
- Wound may be discharging pus or serosanguinous fluid
- Wound edges may be separating (dehiscence)
- Caesarean section wound: lower abdominal transverse scar
- Perineal wound: pain on sitting, difficulty with perineal hygiene
- Necrotising fasciitis (rare but catastrophic): rapidly spreading redness, skin discolouration, severe pain, skin may feel crunchy (crepitus)

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the caesarean wound or perineal stitches painful, red, or swollen?
- Is there any discharge from the wound — pus, fluid, or blood?
- Are the wound edges holding together or separating?
- How quickly has any redness been spreading?

**Confirm towards if:**
Fever + wound redness/swelling/discharge = wound infection; rapidly spreading redness + severe pain = necrotising fasciitis emergency

**Uncertainty note:**
Rapidly spreading wound redness or crepitus is an emergency. Do not wait for systemic deterioration before escalating.

### 3.6 Postpartum Eclampsia / HELLP Syndrome
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGHEST — within first week postpartum**
Eclampsia can occur up to 4 weeks postpartum, with most postpartum cases occurring in the first week. HELLP syndrome (Haemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets) can present with fever-like symptoms. Both are life-threatening. Hypertension and fever together in the first postpartum week must prompt consideration of these diagnoses.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Severe headache, visual disturbance, or fitting
- Hypertension (if measurable)
- Epigastric or right upper quadrant pain (HELLP)
- Oedema — facial, hand
- Fever may be present but is not the dominant feature
- Typically within first 7 days postpartum

**Differentiating questions:**
- Any severe headache or blurred vision?
- Any fitting or convulsion?
- Any upper abdominal pain, especially on the right side?
- Any swelling of the face or hands?
- Did she have high blood pressure during the pregnancy or delivery?

**Confirm towards if:**
Fever + severe headache + visual changes OR fitting in first postpartum week = postpartum eclampsia/HELLP — EMERGENCY

**Uncertainty note:**
Fever in the first postpartum week with any neurological symptom must prompt consideration of eclampsia even if blood pressure is not confirmed.

### 3.7 Deep Vein Thrombosis / Pulmonary Embolism
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGH**
The postpartum period carries significantly elevated thrombotic risk. DVT and PE are underdiagnosed in Nigeria. PE is a cause of sudden maternal death. Fever can be a feature of DVT and PE.

**Key differentiating features:**
- DVT: unilateral leg swelling, pain, redness, and warmth — typically calf
- PE: sudden breathlessness, chest pain, cough (may be blood-stained), collapse, low oxygen
- Low-grade fever may accompany both
- No other infective source identified
- Immobility (caesarean section, prolonged labour, bed rest) increases risk
- Obesity or prolonged travel also increase risk

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is one leg more swollen or painful than the other?
- Any sudden difficulty breathing or chest pain?
- Any cough, or coughing blood?
- Did she have a caesarean section or was she immobile for a long period?

**Confirm towards if:**
Fever + unilateral leg swelling/pain = DVT; fever + sudden breathlessness/chest pain = PE — EMERGENCY

**Uncertainty note:**
Sudden breathlessness in a postpartum woman must be treated as PE until excluded. Do not attribute to anaemia alone without ruling out PE.

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE
Postpartum fever is never a low-risk symptom. Malaria is always the first differential in Nigeria. Sepsis (from any source — uterine, wound, urinary, breast) must always be considered. When the source of fever is unclear, when the woman looks unwell, when she is deteriorating, or when the clinical picture is mixed — escalate immediately. Postpartum sepsis kills quickly and silently in Nigeria. Uncertainty always escalates.
