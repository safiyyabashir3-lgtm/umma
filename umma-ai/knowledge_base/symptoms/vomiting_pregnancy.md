# UMMA Symptom File: Vomiting in Pregnancy

---

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

**Confirm the symptom:**
- Ask when the vomiting started, how many times per day she is vomiting, and whether she is able to keep any food or fluid down
- Ask gestational age — vomiting has very different significance in the first trimester versus the third trimester
- Ask whether the vomiting is new or a continuation of vomiting that has been present throughout the pregnancy
- Ask whether the vomiting is associated with nausea, abdominal pain, fever, headache, or other symptoms

**Commonly confused with:**
- Nausea without vomiting — confirm whether she is actually vomiting or only feeling sick
- Heartburn or acid reflux described as vomiting — ask whether she is actually bringing up stomach contents
- Coughing so severe it causes vomiting — ask whether vomiting is triggered by coughing

**Critical immediate checks:**
- Is she able to keep any fluid down? — if no fluid for more than 6 hours, escalate for dehydration risk
- Is there blood in the vomit? — immediate escalation
- Is there severe abdominal pain alongside vomiting? — surgical emergency or pre-eclampsia
- Is she in the third trimester with new-onset vomiting? — pre-eclampsia (HELLP) until excluded
- Is she showing signs of dehydration: no urine for many hours, dry mouth, dizziness?

---

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

Escalate immediately if any of the following are present:

1. Blood in vomit — haematemesis at any stage of pregnancy
2. Vomiting with severe abdominal pain — surgical emergency, HELLP syndrome, or abruption
3. Vomiting with severe headache after 20 weeks — HELLP syndrome or pre-eclampsia
4. Vomiting with inability to keep any fluid down for more than 6 hours — dehydration emergency
5. Vomiting with signs of dehydration: no urine output, sunken eyes, dry mouth, dizziness
6. Vomiting with fever — infection, appendicitis, or malaria
7. New onset vomiting in the third trimester (not present throughout pregnancy) — pre-eclampsia, HELLP, or other serious cause
8. Vomiting with jaundice (yellowing of eyes or skin) — acute fatty liver of pregnancy or hepatitis
9. Vomiting with confusion or altered consciousness
10. Vomiting with significant weight loss — unable to maintain nutrition
11. Vomiting causing inability to take essential medications (antihypertensives, antiretrovirals, insulin)
12. Vomiting that is new, projectile, or forceful in the third trimester

---

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

---

### DISEASE 1: HYPEREMESIS GRAVIDARUM
**Nigerian priority level: HIGH**

Hyperemesis gravidarum (HG) is severe, intractable vomiting in pregnancy that causes dehydration, nutritional deficiency, and metabolic disturbances. It is most common in the first trimester and can be life-threatening if untreated. It is distinct from normal morning sickness by its severity and impact on daily functioning and fluid intake.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Severe vomiting, typically more than 3–5 times per day
- Unable to keep any food or fluid down
- Signs of dehydration: no urine output, dark urine, dizziness, weakness
- Significant weight loss
- First trimester, though can persist into second trimester
- More common with multiple pregnancy (twins) and molar pregnancy
- Worse in the morning but can occur throughout the day

**Differentiating questions:**
- How many times a day is she vomiting?
- Is she able to keep any water down at all?
- When did she last pass urine — was it dark or very small in amount?
- Has she lost weight recently?
- Is she carrying twins or has she had a scan?
- Does she feel dizzy or very weak?

**Confirm towards if:**
- More than 3–5 vomiting episodes per day
- Unable to keep fluids down
- Signs of dehydration
- First or early second trimester
- Weight loss

**Uncertainty note:**
Any woman who cannot keep fluids down for 6 hours or more requires intravenous rehydration at a facility. Do not advise home management of dehydration from HG. Escalate for IV fluids and antiemetics.

---

### DISEASE 2: NORMAL NAUSEA AND VOMITING OF PREGNANCY (MORNING SICKNESS)
**Nigerian priority level: MEDIUM**

Normal morning sickness affects the majority of pregnant women, typically from 6 to 14 weeks. It is distinguished from hyperemesis gravidarum by the woman being able to keep some fluids and food down and by the absence of dehydration and weight loss. It is a diagnosis of exclusion.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Vomiting typically in the morning, often relieved by eating small amounts
- Able to keep some fluids down throughout the day
- No significant weight loss
- No signs of dehydration
- Typically resolves by 14–16 weeks
- Does not interfere with basic functioning for the entire day

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is she able to keep some water or food down during the day?
- Is the vomiting mainly in the morning and does it improve later in the day?
- Has she lost weight or is she managing to eat something?
- Has she passed urine normally today?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Vomiting mainly morning, with some improvement
- Able to keep some fluids down
- No dehydration signs
- No weight loss
- Under 16 weeks

**Uncertainty note:**
Normal morning sickness can escalate to hyperemesis gravidarum. Any woman who cannot keep fluids down by evening or who has not passed urine in 8 hours must be escalated, even if symptoms started as typical morning sickness.

---

### DISEASE 3: PRE-ECLAMPSIA / HELLP SYNDROME
**Nigerian priority level: HIGHEST**

New onset vomiting in the second or third trimester — particularly with headache and upper abdominal pain — is a warning sign of HELLP syndrome, a severe complication of pre-eclampsia. This is a maternal emergency.

**Key differentiating features:**
- New vomiting after 20 weeks — was not vomiting throughout the pregnancy
- Associated with severe headache
- Associated with epigastric or right upper quadrant pain
- Associated with visual disturbance
- Hypertension if blood pressure checked
- Swelling of face or hands

**Differentiating questions:**
- Has the vomiting started recently — was she not vomiting before?
- How many weeks pregnant is she?
- Does she have a headache alongside the vomiting?
- Does she have pain in the upper part of her abdomen or under her right ribs?
- Has her blood pressure been checked recently — was it high?
- Is her face or hands swollen?

**Confirm towards if:**
- New onset vomiting after 20 weeks
- Associated headache, visual changes, or epigastric pain
- Hypertension known or suspected
- Face or hand swelling

**Uncertainty note:**
Any new vomiting after 20 weeks must trigger pre-eclampsia screening at a facility before any other cause is assumed. Do not attribute vomiting in the third trimester to a gastrointestinal cause without excluding pre-eclampsia.

---

### DISEASE 4: MALARIA
**Nigerian priority level: HIGHEST**

Malaria is a common cause of vomiting in pregnancy in Nigeria. It must always be considered in any pregnant woman who is vomiting, especially with fever. Severe malaria causes intractable vomiting and inability to take oral antimalarials.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Fever alongside vomiting — cyclical, with chills and sweating
- Headache and body aches accompanying vomiting
- Pallor of conjunctiva or palms suggesting anaemia
- No bednet use or missed IPTp
- Vomiting may prevent oral medication intake

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is there fever alongside the vomiting?
- Is she having chills and sweating?
- Does she have a headache or body aches?
- Is she sleeping under a mosquito net?
- Has she taken her IPTp tablets?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Fever with vomiting
- Cyclical pattern with chills and sweating
- Headache and body aches
- Anaemia signs present

**Uncertainty note:**
Vomiting preventing oral antimalarial intake in a malaria case requires parenteral treatment at a facility. Escalate any woman with suspected malaria who cannot keep oral medication down.

---

### DISEASE 5: ACUTE GASTROENTERITIS
**Nigerian priority level: MEDIUM-HIGH**

Gastroenteritis from contaminated food or water is common in Kano and northern Nigeria. In pregnancy, dehydration from gastroenteritis occurs more rapidly and is more dangerous. Cholera must be considered in areas with outbreaks.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Vomiting with diarrhoea — watery or loose stools
- Abdominal cramping
- May follow eating food from outside the home or street food
- Household contacts may be similarly affected
- Fever may or may not be present
- In cholera: profuse watery diarrhoea (rice-water stool), very rapid dehydration

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is she also having diarrhoea alongside the vomiting?
- Did the vomiting start after eating a particular food or outside food?
- Is anyone else in the household sick in the same way?
- Is the diarrhoea very watery and frequent?
- Is she feeling very dizzy or weak?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Vomiting with diarrhoea
- Onset after food exposure
- Household cluster
- Cramping abdominal pain

**Uncertainty note:**
Gastroenteritis in pregnancy causes rapid dehydration. Any pregnant woman with vomiting and diarrhoea who cannot keep fluids down must be escalated for intravenous fluids. Cholera causes death from dehydration within hours — escalate immediately if suspected.

---

### DISEASE 6: APPENDICITIS
**Nigerian priority level: HIGH**

Appendicitis in pregnancy presents with vomiting, fever, and abdominal pain. The pain location is displaced upward by the uterus and may be confused with other pregnancy-related pain. It is a surgical emergency.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Vomiting with fever and right-sided or right upper abdominal pain
- Pain constant and worsening — not cramping
- Loss of appetite
- Nausea precedes vomiting
- No diarrhoea (distinguishes from gastroenteritis)

**Differentiating questions:**
- Does she have pain in the right side of her abdomen alongside the vomiting?
- Is there fever?
- Is the pain constant or does it come and go?
- Has she lost her appetite completely?
- Is she also having diarrhoea or is it only vomiting?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Vomiting with fever and right-sided abdominal pain
- Constant pain
- No diarrhoea
- Progressive worsening

**Uncertainty note:**
Appendicitis in pregnancy carries high risk of perforation. Vomiting with fever and constant abdominal pain requires urgent surgical evaluation. Do not observe at home.

---

### DISEASE 7: ACUTE FATTY LIVER OF PREGNANCY
**Nigerian priority level: HIGHEST**

Acute fatty liver of pregnancy (AFLP) is a rare but rapidly fatal condition unique to pregnancy, usually occurring in the third trimester. It presents with vomiting, jaundice, abdominal pain, and rapid deterioration to liver failure and coma.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Third trimester — typically after 34 weeks
- Jaundice — yellowing of eyes and skin
- Vomiting with severe malaise and weakness
- Abdominal pain — right upper quadrant or generalised
- Rapidly progressive — woman deteriorates over hours to days
- May be confused with severe pre-eclampsia or HELLP

**Differentiating questions:**
- How many weeks pregnant is she?
- Are her eyes or skin turning yellow?
- Is she very unwell — much weaker than the vomiting alone would explain?
- Does she have pain in the upper right part of her abdomen?
- Is she becoming confused or difficult to rouse?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Third trimester vomiting with jaundice
- Right upper quadrant pain
- Rapid clinical deterioration
- Confusion or altered consciousness

**Uncertainty note:**
AFLP is a rapidly fatal emergency. Any third trimester woman with vomiting and jaundice must be escalated immediately. Do not observe at home.

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE — VOMITING IN PREGNANCY

Vomiting in pregnancy is only normal in the first trimester as mild morning sickness where some fluids can be kept down. Any vomiting with dehydration signs, any vomiting after 20 weeks that is new, any vomiting with fever, abdominal pain, or headache, and any vomiting with blood or jaundice requires facility assessment. When the cause is unclear or the woman cannot keep fluids down, escalate. Dehydration in pregnancy is dangerous to both mother and fetus.
