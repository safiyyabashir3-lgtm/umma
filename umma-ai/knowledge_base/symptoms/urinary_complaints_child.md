## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### What counts as a urinary complaint in a child under 5?
- **Caregiver-reported concern** about the child's urination: frequency, pain on urination, change in urine appearance, change in urinary stream, or lack of urination
- In children under 2: urinary symptoms are almost always reported indirectly — crying during nappy change, unexplained irritability, poor feeding, or fever without obvious source
- In children 2–5: older children may communicate discomfort when urinating, or refuse to urinate; caregiver may notice straining, dribbling, or foul-smelling nappies
- **Haematuria** (blood in urine) — visible blood at any age is always significant
- **Reduced or no urine output** in a sick child is a danger sign

### Commonly confused symptoms — verify carefully
- **Nappy rash / vulvovaginitis** — superficial perineal irritation in girls can cause crying or discomfort associated with urination without true UTI; ask whether discomfort is visible external redness vs internal pain
- **Normal urinary frequency variation** — toddlers urinate frequently; frequency alone without other features is not necessarily pathological
- **Constipation causing urinary symptoms** — severe constipation can cause urinary frequency and incomplete bladder emptying in toddlers; ask about stool pattern
- **Phimosis in boys** — tight foreskin may cause straining or dribbling stream without infection
- **Genital schistosomiasis vs UTI** — haematuria with freshwater exposure may be schistosomiasis, not UTI
- **Severe dehydration causing concentrated or reduced urine** — dark or smelly urine may be from dehydration rather than infection; assess hydration status

### Critical immediate checks
- **Age of child** — any child under 3 months with any fever is an emergency regardless of source
- **Is the child passing urine at all?** — anuria or severe oliguria in a sick child is a danger sign
- **Fever** — fever with urinary symptoms in any child under 5 requires urgent UTI assessment; young children frequently present with fever as the only or dominant feature of UTI
- **General appearance** — does the child look sick, lethargic, or toxic? UTI in young children causes systemic illness
- **Sex of child** — girls have much higher UTI rates; in boys, UTI is less common and may indicate urological abnormality

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

Listed from most to least dangerous:

1. **Child under 3 months with any fever or irritability** — immediate emergency regardless of suspected source
2. **No urine passed for 8 or more hours in a sick child** — possible acute kidney injury, severe dehydration, or obstruction
3. **Fever with child who looks very unwell — limp, unconscious, or cannot be woken** — sepsis; immediate emergency
4. **Visible blood in urine in any child** — haematuria always requires urgent assessment
5. **Fever with abdominal or loin pain in a young child** — pyelonephritis
6. **Child is vomiting all feeds and cannot keep fluids down** — dehydration with systemic infection
7. **Fever not responding to simple measures combined with urinary symptoms** — ascending UTI or systemic infection
8. **Swelling of the face, legs, or whole body with urinary symptoms** — possible nephrotic syndrome or nephritic syndrome
9. **Child with known urological condition (kidney problems, previous UTI, structural abnormality) with new symptoms** — higher risk of severe infection
10. **Foul-smelling urine with fever in a child under 2 who is not feeding well** — UTI presenting atypically
11. **Straining with urination or dribbling stream in a boy** — possible obstruction or phimosis with secondary infection
12. **Caregiver feels child is getting worse, not better** — uncertainty always escalates

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

### 3.1 Urinary Tract Infection (UTI)
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGHEST**
UTI is the most important diagnosis in a child with urinary complaints and fever. It is the commonest serious bacterial infection in children under 5 in Nigeria. In children under 2, it typically presents without classic urinary symptoms — fever, poor feeding, vomiting, and irritability are the dominant features. *E. coli* is the most common organism. Untreated UTI in young children causes renal scarring, reflux nephropathy, and long-term kidney damage. In boys, UTI is less common and may indicate an underlying urological abnormality.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Under 2 years: fever + poor feeding + irritability + no obvious other source (no cough, no diarrhoea, no ear findings) = UTI until excluded
- Over 2 years: dysuria, frequency, urgency, or secondary enuresis (regression to wetting in a toilet-trained child)
- Urine may be foul-smelling, dark, or cloudy
- Abdominal or loin pain in older children
- In boys: UTI is less common and more likely to indicate structural abnormality

**Differentiating questions:**
- Does the child cry or seem in pain when passing urine?
- Has the child had a fever?
- Is the urine smelling bad or looking different?
- Is the child feeding well or has feeding changed?
- Any vomiting?
- For older children: is the child complaining of tummy pain or back pain?
- Has the child had a UTI before?
- Is the child a boy? (Boys with UTI need further investigation)

**Confirm towards if:**
Fever + foul-smelling or cloudy urine + irritability/poor feeding in infant = UTI; fever + dysuria + frequency in toddler/child = UTI

**Uncertainty note:**
In a child under 2 with unexplained fever and no clear alternative source, UTI must be actively excluded. Do not assume viral illness without considering UTI.

### 3.2 Pyelonephritis (Upper UTI in Children)
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGHEST**
Pyelonephritis in children carries risk of acute kidney injury and long-term renal scarring. Young children with UTI involving the upper urinary tract are often not distinguishable clinically from lower UTI without imaging or culture, but severity of systemic illness provides important clues.

**Key differentiating features:**
- High fever — often >39°C
- Child appears systemically unwell — lethargic, not feeding, vomiting
- Loin or abdominal pain if child is old enough to report it
- Rigors or shivering
- May have no localising urinary symptoms in infants
- Slower to improve than lower UTI

**Differentiating questions:**
- How high is the fever?
- Does the child look very unwell — limp, very sleepy, or hard to wake?
- Any pain in the back, side, or abdomen?
- Is the child vomiting?
- Is the fever very high and not coming down?

**Confirm towards if:**
High fever + unwell child + loin or abdominal tenderness + urinary symptoms or foul urine = pyelonephritis

**Uncertainty note:**
Any infant or child under 2 with high fever and UTI suspected should be assessed for pyelonephritis. Cannot be clinically excluded without culture and imaging.

### 3.3 Urinary Schistosomiasis
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGH in endemic areas**
Schistosoma haematobium is endemic in parts of northern Nigeria, particularly near freshwater bodies. Urinary schistosomiasis causes haematuria, urinary frequency, and dysuria in children. Schoolchildren and children who play in rivers, ponds, or irrigation canals are at highest risk, but infection can occur at any age with freshwater exposure.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Haematuria — blood in urine, classically at the end of the urinary stream (terminal haematuria)
- Urinary frequency and dysuria
- No fever or only mild fever (unlike UTI)
- Child plays in or near freshwater — rivers, irrigation canals, ponds, seasonal floodwater
- May have been present for months before presentation
- Painless haematuria is a classic feature

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is there blood in the urine? When does it appear — throughout or at the very end?
- Does the child play in rivers, ponds, streams, or irrigation water?
- Has the blood in the urine been present for a long time?
- Is there any fever?
- Any other symptoms — tummy pain, weight loss?

**Confirm towards if:**
Terminal haematuria + freshwater exposure + no or low fever + no systemic illness = urinary schistosomiasis

**Uncertainty note:**
Haematuria in a child is never normal and always requires clinical assessment. Do not attribute to schistosomiasis alone without excluding UTI, nephrotic syndrome, or trauma.

### 3.4 Nephrotic Syndrome
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGH**
Nephrotic syndrome is a significant cause of childhood morbidity in Nigeria. Minimal change disease and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis are the commonest causes in Nigerian children. Malaria-associated nephrotic syndrome (quartan malaria nephropathy from *P. malariae*) is also seen. Children present with oedema, frothy urine, and reduced urine output — these may be reported by caregivers as urinary complaints.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Generalised oedema — puffy face (especially morning facial puffiness), swollen legs, abdominal distension (ascites)
- Frothy or foamy urine (proteinuria)
- Reduced urine output
- No fever unless secondary infection is present
- Child appears generally unwell, may be lethargic
- Gradual onset over days to weeks

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the child's face swollen or puffy, especially in the morning?
- Are the legs or ankles swollen?
- Is the tummy swollen?
- Does the urine look frothy or bubbly?
- Has the child been passing less urine than usual?
- How long has this been going on?

**Confirm towards if:**
Facial puffiness + leg oedema + frothy urine + reduced urine output = nephrotic syndrome

**Uncertainty note:**
Oedema in a child is always significant. If urinary changes are combined with any swelling — face, legs, or abdomen — escalate for further assessment. Do not attribute facial puffiness to malnutrition without excluding nephrotic syndrome.

### 3.5 Nephritic Syndrome / Acute Glomerulonephritis
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGH**
Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis is the commonest cause of nephritic syndrome in Nigerian children, typically following streptococcal throat or skin infection. Characterised by haematuria, reduced urine output, hypertension, and oedema. Malaria nephritis also occurs.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Dark, brown, or tea-coloured urine (haematuria — blood in urine causing discolouration)
- Reduced urine output (oliguria)
- Facial puffiness and oedema
- Hypertension — child may have headache; seizures in severe cases
- Recent history of throat infection or skin sores (impetigo)
- Usually in children over 2 years

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the urine dark, brown, or like tea or cola?
- Is the child passing less urine than usual?
- Any facial swelling?
- Did the child have a throat infection or skin sores in the weeks before this?
- Any headaches?
- Any fits or convulsions?

**Confirm towards if:**
Dark brown urine + facial oedema + oliguria + recent throat or skin infection = acute glomerulonephritis

**Uncertainty note:**
Dark urine in a child requires urgent assessment — malaria, hepatitis, and haemolysis must also be considered alongside nephritis.

### 3.6 Vulvovaginitis / Perineal Irritation in Girls
**Nigerian Priority Level: MEDIUM**
Vulvovaginitis is common in girls under 5 due to thin, unoestrogeniised vulval tissue and poor perineal hygiene or irritants. Causes crying or discomfort with urination and may be mistaken for UTI. However, UTI must be excluded first.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Redness, soreness, or discharge around the vulva
- Discomfort appears on external contact rather than internal urinary burning
- No fever or systemic illness
- No change in urine appearance
- Associated with harsh soap, tight clothing, poor hygiene, or threadworm (perianal itching)

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the outside skin around the vagina red, sore, or swollen?
- Is there any discharge from the vaginal area?
- Does she scratch herself around the vaginal or bottom area — especially at night?
- Is there fever?
- Does the urine look normal?

**Confirm towards if:**
External vulval redness/soreness + discomfort on contact + no fever + no change in urine + no systemic illness = vulvovaginitis

**Uncertainty note:**
Vulvovaginitis and UTI can co-exist. Do not exclude UTI because vulval irritation is present. If there is any fever or the child appears unwell, UTI takes precedence.

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE
In children under 5, urinary symptoms are frequently atypical or absent even in serious urinary infections. Fever without an obvious source in an infant should always prompt consideration of UTI. Any child with haematuria, significant oedema, reduced urine output, or systemic illness alongside urinary symptoms must be escalated. Uncertainty always escalates — renal disease and urinary sepsis in young children cause long-term harm if missed or delayed.
