# SYMPTOM FILE: Abdominal Pain — Child
**Category:** symptoms | **Subcategory:** child | **Topic:** abdominal_pain

---

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### Confirming the Symptom
- Child reports pain in the stomach, belly, or side
- Caregiver observes child holding abdomen, drawing up knees, or guarding the belly
- Child cries when belly is touched or pressed
- Child refuses to stand upright or walk normally due to abdominal pain
- Infant presenting with persistent crying, drawing up knees, arching back, refusing feeds
- Associated symptoms: vomiting, diarrhoea, constipation, fever, bloating, or loss of appetite

### Commonly Confused Symptoms
- **Chest or lower rib pain** — ask child or caregiver to point to exact location
- **Groin or scrotal pain** — pain radiating down from lower abdomen in boys; may indicate testicular torsion
- **Back or flank pain** — kidney or urinary tract source; may be felt as abdominal by child
- **Hunger or gas (wind)** — crampy, relieved by passing gas or eating; no systemic illness
- **Referred pain from pneumonia** — right lower chest pneumonia commonly presents as right upper abdominal pain in children; always check breathing
- **Sickle cell crisis** — abdominal pain is a common crisis feature; ask about known sickle cell disease

### Critical Immediate Checks
- Location of pain — is it localised (especially right lower quadrant) or diffuse?
- Is the abdomen rigid or board-like?
- Is the child walking, or refusing to move or walk?
- Is there visible abdominal distension?
- Is there blood in stool or vomit?
- Is there fever?
- In boys — is there scrotal pain or swelling?

---

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

*Escalate immediately if any of the following are present:*

1. Rigid or board-like abdomen — indicates peritonitis, EMERGENCY
2. Child unable to walk or refusing to move due to pain — peritonism sign
3. Bile-stained (green) vomiting — intestinal obstruction until proven otherwise, EMERGENCY
4. Bloody stool (frank blood or red-currant jelly stool) — intussusception or surgical emergency
5. Visible abdominal distension with no passage of stool or gas
6. Signs of shock — pale, cold, rapid breathing, lethargic, not responding
7. Severe pain in right lower quadrant with fever — appendicitis
8. Pain in scrotum or groin in a boy with abdominal pain — testicular torsion, EMERGENCY (time-critical)
9. Child with known sickle cell disease in a pain crisis — requires urgent hospital management
10. Abdominal pain after trauma — internal injury must be excluded
11. Infant with episodic severe crying and vomiting, drawing up knees, passing red-currant jelly stool — intussusception, EMERGENCY
12. Altered consciousness alongside abdominal pain — possible severe malaria with hepatosplenomegaly, or surgical emergency
13. Caregiver is very worried or says child is the sickest they have ever seen

---

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

---

### 1. MALARIA
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
Malaria frequently presents with abdominal pain in children, due to hepatosplenomegaly and general visceral discomfort. Severe malaria can cause abdominal pain with altered consciousness.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Fever present — often high, may be cyclical
- Pain is diffuse or in the upper abdomen (from liver or spleen)
- Vomiting common
- Pallor suggesting anaemia
- General malaise, poor appetite, weakness
- Child may have had malaria before — recurrent infections common
- Abdominal pain is rarely the sole symptom — fever and general illness dominate

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Does the child have fever alongside the stomach pain?
- Is the child vomiting?
- Is the child pale or looking washed out?
- Has the child had malaria before?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Fever + upper abdominal pain + vomiting + pallor + malaria-endemic area

**Uncertainty Note:**
When malaria is possible and the child is febrile, do not allow the abdominal pain to redirect attention away from a malaria workup. Both conditions can coexist.

---

### 2. GASTROENTERITIS (Infective Diarrhoea)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
One of the leading causes of childhood morbidity and mortality in Nigeria. Bacterial, viral, and parasitic causes all common.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Crampy, colicky abdominal pain — comes in waves
- Diarrhoea — loose or watery stool, may be frequent
- Vomiting often precedes or accompanies diarrhoea
- Fever may or may not be present
- Signs of dehydration — dry lips, sunken eyes, reduced urine output, skin tenting
- Blood in stool suggests bacterial dysentery (Shigella, E. coli) or amoebic dysentery

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is the child having diarrhoea — loose or watery stools?
- How many times has the child passed stool today?
- Is there blood or mucus in the stool?
- Is the child vomiting as well?
- Is the child drinking and urinating normally?
- Are the child's eyes sunken or lips dry?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Colicky pain + diarrhoea + vomiting + dehydration signs = gastroenteritis

**Uncertainty Note:**
Bloody diarrhoea (dysentery) should be treated as bacterial until proven otherwise and requires clinic assessment. Severe dehydration in a child is a medical emergency.

---

### 3. TYPHOID FEVER
**Nigerian Priority: HIGHEST**
Typhoid is endemic in Nigeria and a common cause of prolonged fever with abdominal symptoms in children. Intestinal perforation from typhoid is a common surgical emergency in Nigerian hospitals.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Prolonged fever — typically stepwise, increasing over days
- Abdominal pain, often diffuse or central
- Constipation more common than diarrhoea in early typhoid (diarrhoea may occur later)
- Headache, malaise, lethargy
- Relative bradycardia for degree of fever (clinical sign)
- Hepatosplenomegaly
- Risk of intestinal perforation — sudden worsening of abdominal pain, rigidity = emergency

**Differentiating Questions:**
- How many days has the child had fever?
- Has the fever been getting worse each day, not getting better?
- Is the child constipated, or alternating constipation and diarrhoea?
- Is the child very weak or lethargic beyond what the fever would explain?
- Has the child recently eaten food or water from outside the home?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Prolonged fever (5+ days) + abdominal pain + constipation or changed bowel habit + lethargy + headache

**Uncertainty Note:**
Typhoid and malaria coexist commonly in Nigeria. A child with prolonged fever and abdominal symptoms may have both. If the abdomen suddenly becomes rigid, refer immediately — perforation is life-threatening.

---

### 4. APPENDICITIS
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH**
A surgical emergency. Often missed in Nigeria due to atypical presentation and delayed presentation to care. Perforation rates are high due to delayed treatment.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Pain typically starts central (periumbilical) then migrates to right lower quadrant
- Fever — low grade initially, higher if perforated
- Nausea and vomiting
- Child refuses to walk or moves very carefully, bent forward
- Anorexia — child not wanting to eat at all
- Onset is progressive — gets worse over hours to days
- Can be missed in young children — presentation more atypical under age 5

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Where exactly is the pain — can the child point to it?
- Did the pain start in the middle of the belly and move to the right lower side?
- Is the child refusing to walk or walking bent over?
- Has the child been eating at all?
- Is the pain constant and getting worse, not coming and going?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Migrating pain to right lower quadrant + fever + anorexia + child guarding abdomen + refuses to walk

**Uncertainty Note:**
If appendicitis cannot be excluded, refer to hospital. Do not observe at home. Perforation in a child can occur within 24–48 hours of symptom onset.

---

### 5. INTESTINAL WORMS (Helminthiasis)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH**
Extremely prevalent in northern Nigeria. Ascaris lumbricoides, hookworm, and Trichuris are the most common. Heavy worm burden can cause significant abdominal symptoms.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Chronic or recurrent abdominal pain — crampy, around the navel
- Child or caregiver may have seen worms in stool
- Abdominal distension
- Poor growth, pallor (hookworm anaemia)
- Intermittent — not constant or worsening
- No or low fever
- Child may report itching around the anus (pinworm)
- Worm bolus can cause intestinal obstruction in heavy infection — this is a surgical emergency

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Has anyone seen worms in the child's stool?
- Has the child had this kind of stomach pain before?
- Does the child complain of itching around the bottom, especially at night?
- Is the child growing and gaining weight normally?
- Does the child defecate in the open or use a latrine?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Chronic periumbilical pain + visible worms in stool + poor growth + anaemia + low hygiene environment

**Uncertainty Note:**
Heavy Ascaris infection can cause complete intestinal obstruction — this presents as bilious vomiting, severe distension, and obstruction, and is a surgical emergency. Refer immediately.

---

### 6. URINARY TRACT INFECTION (UTI)
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH**
UTI is commonly missed in children because abdominal or flank pain is often the presenting complaint rather than urinary symptoms, particularly in young children.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Fever, often without obvious source
- Lower abdominal pain or loin/flank pain
- Dysuria — child reports burning or pain on urination (if old enough to communicate)
- Frequency — urinating more than usual
- Foul-smelling urine
- In infants: unexplained fever, poor feeding, irritability, vomiting — no localising symptoms
- More common in girls but occurs in boys too (especially uncircumcised)

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Does it hurt the child to pass urine?
- Is the child passing urine more often than usual?
- Does the urine smell bad or look different?
- Has the child had a UTI before?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Fever + lower abdominal or flank pain + dysuria or frequency + foul urine

**Uncertainty Note:**
UTI in a young child (under 2 years) always requires formal urine testing. Clinical diagnosis is unreliable in this age group. Refer for urine culture.

---

### 7. SICKLE CELL DISEASE — ABDOMINAL CRISIS
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH**
Nigeria has the highest burden of sickle cell disease in the world. Abdominal crises (vaso-occlusive) are common and can be severe.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Known sickle cell disease in the child (or family history)
- Severe, acute abdominal pain — often very severe
- May be accompanied by bone pain, chest pain
- Acute chest syndrome: chest pain + breathing difficulty + fever in a sickle cell child = EMERGENCY
- Splenic sequestration: sudden severe left upper abdominal pain + pallor + shock in a young child = EMERGENCY
- Previous similar crises — child or caregiver recognises the pattern

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Does the child have sickle cell disease?
- Has the child had pain like this before?
- Is there also chest pain or difficulty breathing?
- Is the child very pale — more pale than usual?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Known sickle cell + severe abdominal pain + no other cause = sickle cell crisis — refer for hospital management

**Uncertainty Note:**
Acute chest syndrome and splenic sequestration in sickle cell disease are life-threatening. Any sickle cell child with abdominal pain and pallor or respiratory symptoms must go to hospital immediately.

---

### 8. INTUSSUSCEPTION
**Nigerian Priority: HIGH — EMERGENCY**
More common in infants and toddlers. Telescoping of bowel causes episodic severe pain and can lead to bowel necrosis if untreated.

**Key Differentiating Features:**
- Classic presentation in infant 3 months to 2 years
- Episodic, severe, colicky pain — child screams then goes quiet between episodes (draws up knees during pain)
- Red-currant jelly stool — blood and mucus mixed — pathognomonic
- Vomiting — may become bile-stained
- Sausage-shaped mass may be palpable in right upper abdomen
- Child becomes progressively more lethargic between episodes as condition worsens

**Differentiating Questions:**
- Is the pain coming in episodes — screaming then going quiet?
- Has the child passed stool that looks like red jam or has blood in it?
- Is the child becoming more limp and sleepy between pain episodes?

**Confirm Towards If:**
Infant + episodic severe pain with quiet intervals + bloody mucousy stool + lethargy = intussusception — EMERGENCY

**Uncertainty Note:**
The triad of colicky pain, bloody stool, and abdominal mass is present in only a minority of cases. Suspect intussusception in any infant with episodic severe pain and refer immediately.

---

## GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE

If the cause of abdominal pain in a child cannot be clearly identified as benign, or if any of the following are present, escalate to clinic or hospital:
- Fever alongside abdominal pain
- Vomiting, especially if bile-stained
- Pain that is constant and worsening (not colicky and self-resolving)
- Child refusing to walk or move
- Any signs of dehydration
- Blood in stool or vomit
- Child appears unwell beyond what the pain alone would explain

**In northern Nigeria, malaria and typhoid are the first considerations for any child with fever and abdominal pain. Surgical emergencies (appendicitis, intussusception, typhoid perforation) are missed or delayed frequently due to their atypical presentation — maintain a high index of suspicion.**
