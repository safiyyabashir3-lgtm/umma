**Version:** 1.0

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### What counts as vaginal discharge in pregnancy?
- Any fluid or substance coming from the vagina that is not urine, blood, or amniotic fluid
- Change in normal vaginal wetness — increased amount, change in colour, change in smell, change in texture
- Associated vulval or vaginal itching, burning, soreness, or swelling

### Commonly confused symptoms — verify carefully
- **Amniotic fluid leakage (PROM/PPROM)** — must be actively excluded in any woman reporting sudden increase in watery discharge. Amniotic fluid is typically clear or pale yellow, watery, continuous or intermittent trickling, may have a faint sweet smell, cannot be controlled like urine
- **Urinary incontinence** — stress incontinence is common in pregnancy; fluid lost with coughing, sneezing, or movement; originates from urethra not vagina
- **Blood-stained discharge vs vaginal bleeding** — small amounts of blood mixed with discharge (show) differ from frank vaginal bleeding; clarify colour, amount, and whether it is mixed with mucus
- **Normal leukorrhoea of pregnancy** — increased clear or white, non-offensive, non-itchy discharge is normal in pregnancy due to oestrogen effect; must be distinguished from pathological discharge
- **Mucus plug expulsion** — thick, jelly-like mucus, sometimes blood-tinged, in late pregnancy; normal but signals approaching labour

### Critical immediate checks
- **Is this possibly amniotic fluid?** — Any watery, uncontrollable, or continuous discharge must be treated as possible rupture of membranes until excluded. This is the single most important check.
- **Gestational age** — watery discharge before 37 weeks raises possibility of preterm prelabour rupture of membranes (PPROM), which is an emergency
- **Is there any vaginal bleeding alongside the discharge?** — blood with discharge requires separate bleeding assessment
- **Any fever with the discharge?** — suggests infection, possibly chorioamnionitis if membranes are ruptured

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

Listed from most to least dangerous:

1. **Watery or clear fluid leaking continuously or in gushes from the vagina** — possible rupture of membranes; if before 37 weeks this is a PPROM emergency
2. **Any watery discharge before 37 weeks of pregnancy** — PPROM must be excluded; preterm labour risk
3. **Discharge with fever (feeling very hot, shivering)** — chorioamnionitis or systemic infection; especially dangerous if membranes may be ruptured
4. **Foul-smelling discharge with fever and abdominal pain** — chorioamnionitis; risk of maternal sepsis and fetal compromise
5. **Discharge with abdominal pain or cramping** — possible infection ascending to uterus or onset of labour
6. **Heavy blood-stained discharge** — must be distinguished from antepartum haemorrhage; escalate
7. **Discharge with reduced fetal movements** — fetal compromise must be excluded
8. **Severe vulval swelling, pain, or inability to walk** — possible Bartholin's abscess or severe vulvovaginitis
9. **Discharge in a woman with known HIV or immunocompromise** — higher risk of opportunistic or severe infection
10. **Woman is very distressed, very worried, or feels something is wrong** — uncertainty always escalates

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

### 3.1 Rupture of Membranes (ROM / PROM / PPROM)
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGHEST — EMERGENCY if preterm**
Premature rupture of membranes (PROM) at term and preterm PPROM are among the most important diagnoses to exclude in any pregnant woman with watery or increased vaginal fluid. PPROM before 37 weeks carries risk of preterm birth, cord prolapse, and chorioamnionitis. In northern Nigeria, access to neonatal intensive care is severely limited, making PPROM a high-stakes diagnosis.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Sudden gush of fluid OR continuous slow trickling that cannot be stopped
- Fluid is watery — not thick or viscous
- Clear, pale yellow, or slightly blood-tinged
- Cannot be controlled (unlike urine)
- May have a faint sweet or odourless quality
- No itching (distinguishes from candidiasis)

**Differentiating questions:**
- Did she feel a sudden gush or pop, or is it a slow continuous wetness?
- Can she control it, or does it keep coming?
- Does it smell like urine, or is it different?
- How many weeks pregnant is she?
- Any contractions, cramping, or back pain?
- Any fever?

**Confirm towards if:**
Watery, uncontrollable fluid + gestational age established + no strong urine odour = PROM/PPROM until excluded by clinical assessment

**Uncertainty note:**
Any uncertainty about whether fluid is amniotic or not must default to treating as PROM. Do not reassure. Escalate for clinical assessment.

### 3.2 Candidiasis (Vulvovaginal Thrush)
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGH**
Candidal infection is the commonest cause of pathological vaginal discharge in pregnant women in Nigeria. Pregnancy-related immune changes and high rates of diabetes and pre-diabetes in northern Nigeria increase risk. *Candida albicans* predominates. Fluconazole is avoided in first trimester; topical treatment is preferred.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Thick, white, curdy or cottage-cheese-like discharge
- No offensive smell (or mild yeasty odour)
- Prominent vulval and vaginal itching — often the dominant symptom
- Vulval redness, soreness, or swelling
- Burning on urination from vulval contact (external dysuria)
- Symptoms often worse at night or after bathing

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the discharge thick and white, like curd or cheese?
- Is there itching — inside the vagina or on the outside skin?
- Is the outside skin red, sore, or swollen?
- Does it smell strongly, or is there little or no smell?
- Is there burning only when urine touches the skin, or also inside?
- Has she had this before? Does it keep coming back?

**Confirm towards if:**
White curdy discharge + prominent itching + vulval redness + no offensive smell = candidiasis

**Uncertainty note:**
If discharge is not clearly curdy, or if smell is offensive, or if there is no itching, do not assume candidiasis. Consider bacterial vaginosis or STI.

### 3.3 Bacterial Vaginosis (BV)
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGH**
BV is highly prevalent in Nigerian pregnant women — prevalence studies show rates of 20–40% in some regions. BV in pregnancy is associated with preterm birth, PPROM, and postpartum endometritis. It is frequently asymptomatic but when symptomatic is an important treatable cause of discharge.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Thin, grey or off-white, homogeneous discharge
- Characteristic fishy or offensive smell — often worse after sex or water contact
- Little or no itching (this distinguishes it from candidiasis)
- No vulval redness or soreness as a primary feature
- May be detected incidentally

**Differentiating questions:**
- What colour is the discharge — white, grey, or yellowish?
- Is the smell strong or fishy, especially after washing or after sex?
- Is there itching, or mainly just the discharge and smell?
- How long has she had the discharge?

**Confirm towards if:**
Thin grey/white discharge + fishy smell + minimal or no itching = bacterial vaginosis

**Uncertainty note:**
BV and candidiasis can co-exist. If both features are present, both must be considered. Offensive smell always requires further assessment regardless of other features.

### 3.4 Trichomoniasis
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGH**
*Trichomonas vaginalis* is prevalent in Nigeria and is a sexually transmitted infection that increases risk of preterm birth and HIV transmission. It is frequently underdiagnosed. Often co-exists with other STIs.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Yellow-green, frothy or bubbly discharge
- Offensive smell
- Vulval itching and irritation — may be significant
- Dysuria (urethral involvement)
- Vulval or vaginal redness; may have strawberry cervix appearance on examination
- Partner may be asymptomatic carrier

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the discharge yellow or green in colour?
- Does it look frothy or bubbly?
- Is there a bad smell?
- Is there itching alongside the discharge?
- Any burning when passing urine?
- Has her partner had any symptoms?

**Confirm towards if:**
Yellow-green frothy discharge + offensive smell + itching + dysuria = trichomoniasis

**Uncertainty note:**
Trichomoniasis, BV, and gonorrhoea can co-exist. Yellow-green discharge with smell should prompt consideration of STI screen for multiple pathogens.

---

### 3.5 Gonorrhoea / Chlamydia (Cervicitis)
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGH**
Gonorrhoea (*Neisseria gonorrhoeae*) and chlamydia (*Chlamydia trachomatis*) are prevalent in Nigeria and frequently co-infect. In pregnancy they cause chorioamnionitis, PROM, preterm birth, and neonatal eye infection (ophthalmia neonatorum). Chlamydia is often asymptomatic. Both are significantly under-tested in northern Nigeria.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Mucopurulent cervical discharge — yellow or green, from the cervix
- May present as increased vaginal discharge that is purulent
- Chlamydia: often minimal or no symptoms
- Gonorrhoea: more likely to cause symptomatic purulent discharge and dysuria
- Lower abdominal pain if ascending infection
- Partner symptoms (urethral discharge, dysuria) are an important clue
- Neonatal eye infection in previous baby may be historical clue

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the discharge yellow or green and coming from inside?
- Any lower tummy pain?
- Has her partner had any urethral discharge or pain when urinating?
- Any history of STIs or similar symptoms before?
- Did her previous baby have eye infection after birth?

**Confirm towards if:**
Purulent yellow/green discharge + partner symptoms + lower abdominal pain = cervicitis from gonorrhoea/chlamydia

**Uncertainty note:**
Chlamydia may present with minimal discharge. If partner has symptoms or there is any clinical suspicion, do not exclude on basis of mild presentation. Syndromic treatment is appropriate in low-resource settings.

### 3.6 Chorioamnionitis
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGHEST — EMERGENCY**
Chorioamnionitis (infection of the amniotic membranes and fluid) is a life-threatening emergency for both mother and baby. It typically develops when membranes have ruptured, but can occur with intact membranes. It is a leading cause of maternal sepsis and neonatal death in Nigeria.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Offensive, foul-smelling vaginal discharge or amniotic fluid
- Fever — often high
- Abdominal or uterine tenderness
- Maternal tachycardia (heart racing)
- Fetal tachycardia if monitored
- May have history of ruptured membranes before labour onset
- Woman looks and feels seriously unwell

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is there a very bad smell from the discharge or the fluid?
- Does she have fever — feeling very hot or cold and shivering?
- Any tummy pain or tenderness?
- Is her heart racing?
- Has her water broken, or has she had any leaking fluid?
- How unwell does she feel overall?

**Confirm towards if:**
Offensive discharge + fever + uterine tenderness + ruptured membranes = chorioamnionitis — EMERGENCY

**Uncertainty note:**
Any combination of offensive discharge + fever in pregnancy must be treated as possible chorioamnionitis. Do not wait for full criteria. Escalate immediately.

### 3.7 Normal Leukorrhoea of Pregnancy
**Nigerian Priority Level: LOW — but must actively exclude pathology**
Increased clear or white non-offensive vaginal discharge is normal in pregnancy due to elevated oestrogen and increased cervical mucus production. However, this is a diagnosis of exclusion — it must only be considered after pathological causes have been reasonably excluded.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Clear to white, thin or slightly mucoid
- No smell or very mild smell
- No itching, burning, or vulval irritation
- No change in colour to yellow, green, or grey
- No associated fever, abdominal pain, or fetal movement change
- Present since early pregnancy with no recent change

**Differentiating questions:**
- Has the discharge always been like this since early pregnancy, or has it changed recently?
- Any smell, itching, or irritation?
- Any pain or fever?

**Confirm towards if:**
White/clear + odourless + no itching + no associated symptoms + present throughout pregnancy unchanged = likely normal leukorrhoea

**Uncertainty note:**
Any change in discharge characteristics — new smell, new colour, new itching, increased amount suddenly — should not be attributed to normal leukorrhoea without re-assessment.

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE
Vaginal discharge in pregnancy carries significant diagnostic weight. The single most dangerous missed diagnosis is rupture of membranes — this must be actively excluded first in any woman with watery or sudden-onset discharge. When the cause of discharge is unclear, or when any red flag is present, or when the woman is worried — escalate. Do not reassure uncertain presentations.
