## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### Confirming the symptom
Caregivers in northern Nigeria may describe a rash in several ways — spots on the skin,
skin changes, "rashin lafiya na fata" (skin problem), or name a specific traditional
illness. Confirm what is being described: location of the rash, whether it appeared
suddenly or gradually, whether it is with or without fever, and whether the child seems
unwell or is acting normally.

The most critical early question with any rash is whether the spots blanch when pressed.
A rash that does NOT fade when a glass is pressed against it (non-blanching) is a medical
emergency — it may indicate meningococcal disease or another life-threatening condition.

Establish age and vaccination history if possible:
- Unvaccinated or incompletely vaccinated children are at higher risk of measles.
- Ask whether the child has been vaccinated — but do not delay escalation to find out.

### Symptoms that may be confused with rash
- Insect bites — clustered, itchy, usually on exposed areas; child otherwise well
- Dry skin or eczema — chronic, not new, not associated with fever
- Nappy rash — limited to nappy area, not associated with fever or systemic illness
- Birthmarks or pigmentation — present since birth, not new

### Local language and cultural context
- Rashin lafiya na fata — skin problem / skin illness
- Yankakken jikin — spots on the body
- Ja ja a jikinsa — redness on the body
- Kanta / kurji — spots or pimples (may describe chickenpox, measles rash, or other)
- Yana jin zafi a jikinsa — skin is itching / painful
- Samari suna fita a jikinsa — spots coming out on the body

Measles is known locally and feared — caregivers who mention measles or a measles-like
rash should be taken seriously. Measles is vaccine-preventable but remains endemic in
Kano due to incomplete vaccination coverage. Traditional treatments for measles rash
include herbal applications — these should be asked about and discouraged for any
serious rash.

The non-blanching rash of meningococcal disease may not be widely recognised by caregivers
as an emergency sign. Educating the caregiver about the glass test is appropriate and
potentially life-saving.

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

Escalate immediately if any of the following are present. Do not proceed to disease reasoning.
Ordered from most dangerous to least dangerous.

- Non-blanching rash — spots that do not fade when pressed firmly with a finger or glass;
  may be petechiae (pinpoint red spots) or purpura (larger purple patches) — emergency;
  possible meningococcal disease or septicaemia
- Rash with child unconscious or very difficult to wake
- Rash with convulsion
- Rash with severe breathing difficulty
- Rash spreading very rapidly — new spots appearing within hours
- Rash with high fever and child looking very unwell and deteriorating quickly
- Rash with stiff neck
- Rash covering large areas of the body with blistering or skin peeling — possible
  Stevens-Johnson syndrome or scalded skin syndrome (rare but life-threatening)
- Measles rash with difficulty breathing — measles pneumonia; escalate urgently
- Measles rash with eye problems — measles keratitis; urgent, can cause blindness
- Measles rash with altered consciousness — measles encephalitis; emergency
- Rash in an infant under 3 months with fever — always escalate

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

### Nigerian and Kano context
Rash in a child under 5 in Kano occurs most commonly in the context of infectious diseases.
Measles is the most important cause of rash with fever in unvaccinated or incompletely
vaccinated children and remains endemic in northern Nigeria due to vaccination gaps.
Measles is a serious disease in malnourished children — it can be fatal and causes
devastating complications including pneumonia, blindness, and encephalitis.

Chickenpox is common and usually self-limiting but can be severe in malnourished or
immunocompromised children. Meningococcal disease with petechial or purpuric rash is
rare but one of the fastest-killing infections in children — it must never be missed.
Malaria does not typically cause rash — a febrile child with a rash is more likely to
have a viral illness, measles, or another cause.

---

### DISEASE 1: MEASLES
Nigerian priority: HIGHEST for rash with fever in an unvaccinated child — endemic
in northern Nigeria; major cause of child death

Key differentiating features:
- Fever — starts 3 to 5 days before the rash; often high
- Cough — prominent, may be severe; "the three Cs": cough, coryza, conjunctivitis
- Runny nose (coryza) — present in pre-rash phase
- Red eyes (conjunctivitis) — eyes red and watering, photophobia
- Koplik spots — tiny white spots on the inside of the cheeks; appear just before the
  rash; pathognomonic for measles but require mouth inspection to see
- Rash appears after 3 to 5 days of fever — starts on face and behind ears, spreads
  downward to neck, trunk, and limbs over 3 to 4 days
- Rash is maculopapular — red flat and raised areas that merge together (confluent)
- Rash blanches when pressed — it is not petechial
- Child looks very unwell — measles is a serious systemic illness
- Unvaccinated or incompletely vaccinated
- Malnutrition significantly worsens measles severity

Differentiating questions:
- Did the rash start on the face or behind the ears before spreading down the body?
- Has the child had high fever for several days before the rash appeared?
- Does the child have red eyes and a cough alongside the rash?
- Has the child received measles vaccination?
- Is the child visibly thin or malnourished?

Confirm towards measles if:
- Fever for 3 to 5 days + cough + red eyes + rash starting on face and spreading downward
  + unvaccinated child

Uncertainty note:
- Measles in a malnourished child is much more severe — escalate earlier
- Measles pneumonia and measles encephalitis are life-threatening complications —
  any breathing difficulty or altered consciousness with measles rash needs emergency care
- Measles is notifiable — clinic or hospital must be informed

### DISEASE 2: CHICKENPOX — VARICELLA
Nigerian priority: HIGH — common in Nigerian children; usually self-limiting but
can be severe in malnourished or immunocompromised children

Key differentiating features:
- Rash is characteristic — begins as flat red spots, progresses to raised bumps, then
  fluid-filled blisters (vesicles), then crusts over; all stages may be present
  simultaneously on the body
- Very itchy — child scratches constantly
- Rash starts on trunk and face, spreads to limbs — spreads from centre outward
  (opposite of measles which spreads head downward)
- Fever low-grade or moderate — usually less severe than measles
- Child may be unwell but often still interactive and alert
- Lesions also appear in mouth and scalp
- Spreads rapidly in households and schools

Differentiating questions:
- Are some spots flat, some blistered, and some crusted over at the same time?
- Is the rash very itchy?
- Did the rash start on the trunk or face before spreading to the arms and legs?
- Are there any spots in the mouth or on the scalp?

Confirm towards chickenpox if:
- Itchy rash + multiple stages simultaneously (spots, blisters, crusts) + starts on trunk
  or face + spreads outward

Uncertainty note:
- Chickenpox in a malnourished or immunocompromised child can be severe — escalate earlier
- Secondary bacterial infection of scratched spots (impetigo) is common — watch for
  spreading redness, warmth, or pus around lesions
- Do not give aspirin for fever in chickenpox — risk of Reye syndrome

### DISEASE 3: MENINGOCOCCAL DISEASE — PETECHIAL OR PURPURIC RASH
Nigerian priority: HIGH — rare but among the most rapidly fatal conditions in children;
must never be missed

Key differentiating features:
- Non-blanching rash — the defining feature; petechiae (pin-point red or purple spots)
  or purpura (larger purple or bruise-like patches) that do NOT fade when pressed
- Glass test: press a clear glass firmly against the rash; if it does not fade, it is
  non-blanching; this is an emergency sign
- Fever — high fever with rapid deterioration
- Child looks very unwell — pale, cold, confused, or collapsed
- Rash spreads rapidly — new spots appearing within an hour
- Stiff neck — may be present if meningitis component
- Shock signs — pale, cold hands and feet, rapid weak pulse, child unable to stand

Differentiating questions:
- Do the spots fade when you press firmly on them with a finger?
- Is the rash spreading and new spots appearing quickly?
- Does the child seem confused, very pale, or cold to touch?

Confirm towards meningococcal disease if:
- Non-blanching rash of any size + fever + child unwell — immediate emergency

Uncertainty note:
- Any non-blanching rash in a febrile child is a medical emergency — do not wait
  for other signs to confirm; escalate immediately
- Meningococcal septicaemia can kill within hours of the first spot appearing
- If unsure whether the rash is blanching — assume it is not and escalate

---

### DISEASE 4: IMPETIGO AND SKIN INFECTIONS
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM-HIGH — extremely common in children in Kano

Key differentiating features:
- Superficial skin infection — usually starts as small red spots or blisters that break
  and form honey-coloured crusts
- Most common around nose, mouth, and limbs
- Spreads easily — multiple lesions appearing over days
- Low or no fever in uncomplicated impetigo
- Child otherwise reasonably well
- Contagious — spreads in households
- Cellulitis — spreading redness, warmth, and swelling around a wound or bite;
  child may have fever if infection spreading deeper

Differentiating questions:
- Are the spots forming crusts that look honey-coloured or yellowish?
- Is the skin around the spots red, warm, or swollen?
- Has the child had a scratch, bite, or wound where the rash started?
- Does the child have fever alongside the skin spots?

Confirm towards impetigo if:
- Honey-coloured crusting spots + low or no fever + child otherwise well

Uncertainty note:
- Spreading cellulitis with fever — needs clinic assessment and antibiotic treatment
- Extensive impetigo in a young child — needs clinic assessment; may need oral antibiotics
- Secondary infection of chickenpox — can look like impetigo; treat the same way

---

### DISEASE 5: HEAT RASH AND MILIARIA
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM — very common in hot climate; important to differentiate
from serious causes

Key differentiating features:
- Small red or clear tiny bumps — especially in skin folds, neck, armpits, groin, back
- Associated with hot weather or excessive clothing / wrapping
- No fever or only mild fever
- Child not systemically unwell
- Rash improves when child is cooled and clothing reduced
- Very common in infants in the hot season in Kano
- Itchy or prickly sensation

Differentiating questions:
- Did the rash appear after the child was in a very hot environment or heavily wrapped?
- Is the rash mainly in skin folds — neck, armpits, or groin?
- Does the child have fever or seem unwell beyond the rash?
- Does the rash improve when the child is cooled?

Confirm towards heat rash if:
- Small papules in skin folds + hot weather or heavy wrapping + no fever + child well

Uncertainty note:
- Heat rash is the only rash in this file where home management is appropriate
- If fever develops alongside a heat rash — reassess for other causes
- A rash that does not improve with cooling needs clinic assessment

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE
- Non-blanching rash of any size in a febrile child — escalate as emergency immediately
- Rash with high fever and a very unwell child — escalate urgently
- Measles rash with breathing difficulty, eye problems, or altered consciousness — emergency
- Rash in an unvaccinated child with fever — consider measles first
- Malnourished child with any rash illness — escalate sooner; complications are more severe
- When in doubt about whether a rash is blanching — assume it is not and escalate.
  It is always safer to be wrong about a heat rash than to miss meningococcal disease.
