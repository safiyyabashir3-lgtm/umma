## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### What counts as newborn jaundice?
- Yellow discolouration of the skin, whites of the eyes (sclerae), or inside the mouth in a newborn
- Most caregivers report: "the baby is yellow", "the skin has turned yellow", "the eyes look yellow"
- In deeply pigmented Nigerian newborns, yellowing of the whites of the eyes and inside of the mouth (mucous membranes) may be the most visible sign before skin colour change is obvious
- Yellowness of the soles of the feet and palms of the hands indicates more advanced jaundice

### Normal vs pathological jaundice — critical distinction
**Physiological (normal) jaundice:**
- Appears on day 2–3 of life
- Peaks around day 3–5
- Resolves by day 10–14 in term babies (may last up to 3 weeks in preterm)
- Baby is feeding well, active, crying normally
- Yellow colour confined mainly to face and upper chest

**Pathological (abnormal) jaundice — consider when:**
- Appears in the first 24 hours of life (always abnormal — emergency)
- Jaundice is severe — spreading to arms, legs, palms, soles
- Jaundice persisting beyond 14 days in a term baby (prolonged jaundice)
- Baby is unwell — not feeding, lethargic, limp, or fitting
- Dark (tea-coloured) urine and pale (white or clay-coloured) stools — suggests liver disease
- Jaundice progressing rapidly or rapidly worsening

### Commonly confused symptoms — verify carefully
- **Carotenaemia** — yellow skin from high beta-carotene intake (orange foods); extremely rare in neonates; sclera remains white (eyes not yellow); distinguishes from true jaundice
- **Normal newborn skin colour variation** — some newborns have olive or golden skin tones; eyes and mucous membranes will not be yellow in the absence of jaundice
- **Post-delivery bruising and caput** — bruising from delivery can contribute to jaundice (haemolysis); ask about difficult delivery, vacuum or forceps use, or large bruise on the head (cephalohaematoma)

### Critical immediate checks
- **Day of life** — jaundice on day 1 (within 24 hours of birth) is always pathological and always an emergency
- **Is the baby feeding?** — a jaundiced baby who is not feeding is an emergency
- **Is the baby lethargic or limp?** — signs of acute bilirubin encephalopathy; emergency
- **How far has the yellow spread?** — assess whether yellow has reached the arms, legs, palms, or soles
- **Colour of urine and stool** — dark urine + pale or white stool = liver/biliary cause; escalate
- **Blood group of mother** — rhesus negative mother or blood group O can cause haemolytic jaundice

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

Listed from most to least dangerous:

1. **Jaundice appearing on day 1 of life (within 24 hours of birth)** — always pathological; haemolytic emergency
2. **Baby is limp, not responding, or cannot be woken** — acute bilirubin encephalopathy (kernicterus); brain damage occurring; immediate emergency
3. **Baby is fitting or having convulsions** — severe kernicterus or sepsis; immediate emergency
4. **Baby has high-pitched or abnormal cry** — early sign of bilirubin encephalopathy
5. **Baby is not feeding at all or has stopped feeding** — encephalopathy or severe illness; immediate emergency in any jaundiced newborn
6. **Yellow colour has spread to the arms, legs, palms, or soles** — severe jaundice; urgent treatment needed
7. **Baby has fever or is cold** — haemolysis with sepsis or neonatal sepsis; emergency
8. **Baby is arching backwards (retrocollis/opisthotonos)** — advanced bilirubin encephalopathy
9. **Jaundice with dark (tea-coloured) urine AND pale or white stool** — cholestatic jaundice / biliary atresia; urgent
10. **Jaundice persisting beyond 14 days in a term baby** — prolonged jaundice; requires assessment for hypothyroidism, biliary atresia, liver disease
11. **Preterm baby (born before 37 weeks) with any jaundice** — lower bilirubin threshold for danger; escalate earlier
12. **Baby with a large bruise or swelling on the head (cephalohaematoma) with worsening jaundice** — haemolysis from bruise breakdown
13. **Jaundice in a baby whose older sibling also had severe jaundice** — likely haemolytic disease; higher recurrence risk
14. **Caregiver feels the yellow is spreading fast or the baby is getting worse** — uncertainty always escalates

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

### 3.1 Physiological Jaundice
**Nigerian Priority Level: MEDIUM — but requires monitoring**
Physiological jaundice is the most common cause of jaundice in newborns and results from normal breakdown of fetal red blood cells combined with the newborn liver's immaturity in processing bilirubin. It is not a disease but requires monitoring because, in Nigeria, limited access to phototherapy and high rates of infection and haemolysis mean physiological jaundice can become severe if inadequately managed.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Onset day 2–3 of life (not day 1)
- Yellow colour begins on face and progresses slowly downward
- Has not reached arms, legs, palms, or soles
- Baby is feeding well
- Baby is active and has normal cry
- Urine is yellow (normal); stool is yellow or green (normal)
- Term baby (born at or after 37 weeks)

**Differentiating questions:**
- When exactly did the yellow colour appear — on the first day or later?
- Has the yellow spread — where is it now? Face only? Chest? Arms and legs?
- Is the baby feeding well from breast or bottle?
- Is the baby active, awake when expected, and crying normally?
- Is the urine normal yellow? Is the stool normal yellow or green?
- Was the baby born full term or early?

**Confirm towards if:**
Day 2–3 onset + yellow confined to face/chest + well baby + normal feeding + term = physiological jaundice

**Uncertainty note:**
Physiological jaundice can become pathological if jaundice is severe, if feeding is poor (leading to dehydration and worsening jaundice), or if an underlying condition (G6PD, infection) is adding haemolysis. Any worsening or any feeding concern tips this toward pathological.

### 3.2 Haemolytic Jaundice — ABO and Rhesus Incompatibility
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGHEST**
Haemolytic disease of the newborn (HDN) from ABO or Rh incompatibility is a common and dangerous cause of severe early-onset jaundice in Nigeria. ABO incompatibility (mother blood group O, baby group A or B) is actually more common than Rh disease in Nigeria due to high frequency of blood group O. Rh incompatibility (mother Rh-negative, baby Rh-positive) causes more severe haemolysis. Both can cause rapidly rising bilirubin requiring urgent phototherapy or exchange transfusion. Exchange transfusion capacity is very limited in most Nigerian hospitals.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Jaundice onset within 24 hours of birth — this is the hallmark
- Rapidly deepening yellow colour
- Baby may have anaemia (pale appearance, poor feeding, tachycardia)
- Mother is blood group O (ABO incompatibility) or Rh-negative (Rh disease)
- Previous baby with jaundice or haemolytic disease
- Rh disease: often more severe; may cause hydrops fetalis in severe cases
- Positive direct Coombs test (if available)

**Differentiating questions:**
- When did the jaundice first appear — was it on the first day of life or before 24 hours?
- Does the caregiver know the mother's blood group?
- Did any previous baby have severe jaundice or need treatment?
- Is the baby looking pale alongside the yellow?
- Was the baby born early or is there any swelling on the body?

**Confirm towards if:**
Jaundice day 1 + mother blood group O or Rh-negative + rapidly worsening = haemolytic jaundice — EMERGENCY

**Uncertainty note:**
Any jaundice appearing before 24 hours of age must be treated as haemolytic until proven otherwise. Do not observe and wait — escalate immediately.

### 3.3 G6PD Deficiency
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGHEST**
Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency is extremely common in Nigeria — prevalence is among the highest in the world (up to 20–25% in some Nigerian populations). G6PD-deficient newborns can develop sudden, severe, life-threatening haemolytic jaundice, often triggered by exposure to certain substances (naphthalene in mothballs, menthol, certain herbal medicines). This is one of the most important causes of severe neonatal jaundice and kernicterus in Nigeria.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Jaundice can appear at any time in the newborn period — sometimes day 1, sometimes later
- Sudden rapid worsening of jaundice
- Anaemia — baby becomes pale
- Trigger: exposure to naphthalene (mothballs — very commonly used in Nigerian homes), herbal medicines, henna, certain antimalarials (given to mother)
- More common in male babies (X-linked) but also affects females
- Family history of G6PD or jaundice in siblings

**Differentiating questions:**
- Are there mothballs (naphthalene balls) used in the home or in the baby's clothing, bedding, or cot?
- Have any herbal medicines, oils, or traditional preparations been given to the baby or applied to the skin?
- Has the jaundice appeared or worsened suddenly?
- Is the baby becoming paler alongside the jaundice?
- Did any older brother or sister have severe jaundice?
- Has the mother taken any antimalarial tablets recently?

**Confirm towards if:**
Severe or sudden-worsening jaundice + naphthalene exposure + male baby + pallor = G6PD-triggered haemolysis — EMERGENCY

**Uncertainty note:**
Naphthalene exposure in any jaundiced Nigerian newborn must always be asked about. G6PD is so prevalent that it must be considered in all cases of severe or rapidly worsening neonatal jaundice regardless of family history.

### 3.4 Neonatal Sepsis-Related Jaundice
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGHEST**
Infection is a major cause and exacerbator of neonatal jaundice in Nigeria. Sepsis causes haemolysis and liver dysfunction, both worsening bilirubin levels. Jaundice and sepsis frequently co-exist and amplify each other. A sick-looking jaundiced baby must be assessed for sepsis as a primary diagnosis.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Baby looks unwell: poor feeding, lethargic, temperature instability
- Jaundice appearing or worsening alongside signs of illness
- Possible cord infection, skin infection, or maternal infection history
- Fever or hypothermia
- Rapid deterioration
- May follow home delivery with non-sterile cord care

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the baby feeding normally?
- Is the baby active and responsive?
- Does the baby feel hot or cold?
- Is there any problem with the cord?
- Did the mother have fever or illness around the time of delivery?
- Where was the baby delivered and how was the cord managed?

**Confirm towards if:**
Jaundice + poor feeding + lethargy + fever or cold + any possible infection source = sepsis with jaundice — EMERGENCY

**Uncertainty note:**
A jaundiced baby who looks sick must be treated as sepsis until excluded. Do not attribute the clinical deterioration to jaundice alone.

### 3.5 Prolonged Jaundice — Biliary Atresia / Liver Disease / Hypothyroidism
**Nigerian Priority Level: HIGH**
Jaundice persisting beyond 14 days in a term newborn (or 21 days in a preterm) is prolonged jaundice and requires investigation. Biliary atresia — absent or blocked bile ducts — is a surgical emergency that must be identified before 60 days of life for Kasai surgery to be effective. Congenital hypothyroidism also causes prolonged jaundice. Both are treatable if identified early; missed cases lead to death or severe permanent disability.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Jaundice present for more than 2 weeks in a term baby
- Dark urine (tea-coloured, deep yellow-brown) — from conjugated bilirubin passing into urine
- Pale, white, or clay-coloured stool — bile not reaching the gut
- Progressive liver enlargement — abdomen appears swollen
- Baby may be feeding and growing normally initially
- Biliary atresia: no fever, no acute illness — an insidiously progressing condition
- Hypothyroidism: may have poor feeding, constipation, prolonged newborn sleeping, hoarse cry, large tongue, or puffy face

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the yellow colour still present after 2 weeks?
- Is the urine darker than normal — tea or deep brown colour?
- What colour are the stools — yellow and normal, or pale, white, or chalky?
- Is the abdomen getting bigger?
- Is the baby feeding and gaining weight normally?
- Does the baby seem unusually sleepy or constipated?

**Confirm towards if:**
Jaundice beyond 14 days + dark urine + pale/white stool = biliary atresia until excluded — URGENT surgical assessment needed

**Uncertainty note:**
Pale or white stools in a jaundiced newborn beyond 14 days is a surgical emergency. Do not wait to see if it resolves. Biliary atresia treated after 60 days of life has significantly worse outcomes.

### 3.6 Breastfeeding Jaundice / Breast Milk Jaundice
**Nigerian Priority Level: MEDIUM**
Two related but distinct entities: **breastfeeding jaundice** results from inadequate milk intake causing dehydration and worsening bilirubin clearance (more dangerous, associated with insufficient early feeding); **breast milk jaundice** is a benign prolonged jaundice in well, well-fed breastfed babies caused by a substance in breast milk that inhibits bilirubin conjugation. Both are diagnoses that require active exclusion of serious causes first.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Breastfeeding jaundice: early onset (day 2–5), associated with poor latch, insufficient milk, poor feeding, weight loss, infrequent stooling
- Breast milk jaundice: onset after day 4, baby is well-fed, thriving, gaining weight, no dark urine, no pale stool
- Both occur exclusively in breastfed babies
- Breast milk jaundice may persist for weeks but baby remains well

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the mother breastfeeding?
- How is feeding going — is the baby latching well and feeding frequently?
- Is the baby gaining weight?
- Is the baby producing normal amounts of wet nappies and stools?
- Is the stool normal yellow or pale?
- Is the baby well and active?

**Confirm towards if:**
Prolonged jaundice in thriving, well-fed breastfed baby + normal urine + normal stool + well appearance = breast milk jaundice (after excluding serious causes)

**Uncertainty note:**
Breastfeeding jaundice with poor intake can cause severe hyperbilirubinaemia. Do not reassure about jaundice in a breastfed baby who is not gaining weight or not feeding well. Breast milk jaundice must only be diagnosed after biliary atresia, sepsis, and haemolytic causes have been excluded.

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE
Neonatal jaundice is common but must never be assumed benign without active assessment. In Nigeria, the combination of high G6PD prevalence, widespread naphthalene use, limited phototherapy access, and high rates of neonatal infection makes severe jaundice and kernicterus a major cause of childhood death and disability. Any jaundice in the first 24 hours, any jaundice in a sick-looking baby, any jaundice with spread to the limbs, palms, or soles, and any prolonged jaundice with dark urine or pale stools must be escalated without delay. When uncertain about the severity or cause of jaundice — escalate. Kernicterus is permanent and preventable.
