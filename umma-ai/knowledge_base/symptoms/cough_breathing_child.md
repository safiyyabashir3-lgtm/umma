# SYMPTOM FILE: COUGH AND BREATHING DIFFICULTY IN A CHILD UNDER 5
## For UMMA AI Clinical Reasoning — Nigerian Context (Kano / Northwest Priority)
### Awaiting Clinician Verification

---

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### Confirming the symptom
Before proceeding, confirm the nature of the symptom.

If not clearly stated, ask ONE of:
- "Is the child coughing, or is the breathing fast or difficult?"
- "Are you worried about the child's breathing?"

Establish age immediately if not stated:
- "How old is the child?"
- Child under 2 months with any fast breathing or cough — always escalate urgently.
  Do not attempt disease reasoning. Go directly to triage.

### Symptoms that may be confused with breathing difficulty
- Nasal congestion from common cold — noisy breathing through nose, child feeds
  normally, no chest indrawing, no fast breathing
- Noisy breathing during sleep only — may be normal in infants, no distress when awake
- Crying — fast breathing during or just after crying is normal, resolves quickly

---

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE

### QUESTIONING RULES FOR TRIAGE
- Ask ONE question at a time only
- Maximum 3 triage questions before moving to disease reasoning
- If caregiver's opening message already contains red flag information —
  escalate immediately without asking further
- Acknowledge what caregiver said warmly before each question
- Never repeat information the caregiver has already given

### RED FLAGS — Escalate immediately if any present
- Child unable to drink, feed, or breastfeed at all due to breathing difficulty
- Chest indrawing — skin pulling in between ribs or below breastbone with each breath
- Nostril flaring with each breath
- Grunting sound with each breath — especially in infants
- Stridor — harsh crowing noise when breathing in
- Child's lips, tongue, or fingertips appearing blue or grey
- Child looks severely unwell — limp, exhausted, not responsive to surroundings
- Breathing so fast the child cannot complete feeding
- Any child under 2 months with fast or difficult breathing
- Cough with high fever and child looking very unwell
- Sudden choking — consider foreign body inhalation, especially in child 6 months
  to 3 years

### Triage question priority order
Ask only what is not already known. Stop as soon as a red flag is confirmed.

Priority 1: "Can you see the skin between the child's ribs or below the neck
             pulling in with each breath?"
Priority 2: "Is the child able to feed or drink — or is the breathing making
             it impossible to feed?"
Priority 3: "Are the child's lips or the area around the mouth looking dark
             or bluish at all?"

### IF ANY RED FLAG CONFIRMED — Escalate immediately. Do not proceed.
### IF NO RED FLAGS AFTER MAXIMUM 3 QUESTIONS — Proceed to Section 3.

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

### QUESTIONING RULES FOR DISEASE REASONING
- Ask ONE question at a time only
- Maximum 4 questions total across all disease reasoning
- Use answers already given by caregiver — do not re-ask
- If pattern is already clear from caregiver's story — reason confidently
  without asking all 4 questions
- If pattern remains unclear after 4 questions — apply uncertainty rule
- Always acknowledge caregiver's answer warmly before next question

### NIGERIAN / KANO CONTEXT NOTE
Pneumonia is the second leading cause of death in Nigerian children under 5 after
malaria. In Kano, acute respiratory infection affects approximately 38% of under-5
children. Malnutrition affects over 60% of under-5 children in Kano and significantly
worsens the severity of any respiratory illness — a malnourished child with
pneumonia deteriorates faster and needs earlier escalation. Malaria and pneumonia
frequently coexist. Indoor air pollution from cooking fires is a significant
risk factor for respiratory illness in northern Nigerian households.
Never treat cough with breathing difficulty in a child under 5 as minor.

---

### DISEASE 1: PNEUMONIA — BACTERIAL OR VIRAL
Nigerian priority: HIGHEST for cough with breathing difficulty

Key differentiating features:
- Fast breathing — persistent, not only during crying or activity
- Chest indrawing — most reliable sign of serious pneumonia
- Cough — may be wet, dry, or barking
- Fever present in most cases but may be absent in severe or malnourished children
- Poor feeding due to breathing effort
- Child looks tired and unwell
- In infants — grunting, poor feeding, fast breathing may be the only signs

Fast breathing thresholds by age (WHO IMCI):
- Child under 2 months — 60 breaths per minute or more
- Child 2 to 11 months — 50 breaths per minute or more
- Child 1 to 5 years — 40 breaths per minute or more

Differentiating questions:
- "Does the child have a cough — and how long has it been going on?"
- "Is the breathing faster than normal — even when the child is calm and not crying?"
- "Is the child feeding or drinking much less than usual because of the breathing?"
- "Does the child have a fever along with the cough?"

Confirm towards pneumonia if:
- Cough + fast breathing + fever + poor feeding
- Cough + chest indrawing — regardless of fever
- Fast breathing persistent at rest in any child under 5

Uncertainty note:
- If fast breathing is present at rest — treat as pneumonia and escalate urgently
  regardless of other findings
- In malnourished children — fever may be absent even in severe pneumonia
- Malaria and pneumonia coexist frequently — fever with fast breathing may be both
- Do not wait for certainty — fast breathing with cough in a child is always urgent

---

### DISEASE 2: MALARIA WITH RESPIRATORY INVOLVEMENT
Nigerian priority: HIGH — malaria commonly coexists with or mimics pneumonia

Key differentiating features:
- Fever prominent — often with chills and sweating episodes
- Fast breathing present — driven by severe anaemia from malaria, not chest infection
- Pallor of inner eyelids and palms — from malaria-related anaemia
- Cough may be present but is not the dominant feature
- No chest indrawing in uncomplicated malaria
- No grunting
- Child looks pale and weak rather than showing active respiratory distress

Differentiating questions:
- "Is the child shivering and shaking at times, then getting very hot and sweaty?"
- "Are the inside of the child's eyelids or the palms looking very pale?"

Confirm towards malaria with anaemia if:
- Fever + chills + pallor + fast breathing with no chest indrawing
- Known poor mosquito net use + fast breathing + pallor

Uncertainty note:
- Fast breathing can be caused by severe malaria anaemia alone — not only chest
  infection
- If both malaria pattern AND chest signs present — treat as both and escalate
- Pallor with fast breathing in a febrile child — serious regardless of cause

---

### DISEASE 3: BRONCHIOLITIS — IN INFANTS UNDER 12 MONTHS
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM-HIGH in infants

Key differentiating features:
- Age under 12 months — most common in this age group
- Starts as runny nose and mild cough, then breathing worsens over 2 to 3 days
- Wheezing — a whistling sound when the child breathes out
- Fast breathing with or without chest indrawing
- Low-grade fever or no fever
- Poor feeding due to breathing difficulty
- No high fever — distinguishes from bacterial pneumonia in many cases

Differentiating questions:
- "How old is the child and how did this start — did it begin with a runny nose?"
- "Is there a whistling sound when the child breathes out?"
- "Has the breathing been getting gradually worse over the past two to three days?"

Confirm towards bronchiolitis if:
- Infant under 12 months + started with runny nose + progressive worsening breathing
  + wheeze + low or no fever

Uncertainty note:
- Bronchiolitis can become severe quickly in infants — poor feeding is a warning sign
- If child under 3 months with any respiratory distress — escalate immediately
  regardless of suspected cause
- Bronchiolitis and bacterial pneumonia can coexist

---

### DISEASE 4: FOREIGN BODY INHALATION
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM — must not be missed; most common in children 6 months
to 3 years

Key differentiating features:
- Sudden onset — child was well, then suddenly began coughing or choking
- No fever at onset
- Persistent cough that does not improve
- Localised wheeze — one side of chest sounds different
- History of playing with small objects, seeds, or eating nuts
- May follow an episode of choking that seemed to resolve

Differentiating questions:
- "Did the coughing start suddenly while the child was eating or playing with
  something small?"
- "Was the child perfectly well just before the coughing started?"

Confirm towards foreign body if:
- Sudden onset cough with no fever + history of possible inhalation
- Child eating or playing with small objects just before coughing began

Uncertainty note:
- If foreign body is suspected — escalate immediately. Do not wait.
- A child who choked and seemed to recover but continues to cough still needs
  urgent assessment — the object may be lodged in the airway
- Do not attempt to remove object at home

---

### DISEASE 5: SEVERE CROUP — VIRAL LARYNGOTRACHEOBRONCHITIS
Nigerian priority: MEDIUM

Key differentiating features:
- Barking cough — sounds like a seal or dog bark
- Stridor — harsh noise when breathing in, not out
- Hoarse voice or cry
- Worse at night
- Usually preceded by cold symptoms
- Fever low-grade or absent
- Most common in children 6 months to 3 years

Differentiating questions:
- "Does the cough have a barking or seal-like sound?"
- "Is there a harsh or crowing noise when the child breathes in?"

Confirm towards croup if:
- Barking cough + stridor on breathing in + hoarse voice + worse at night

Uncertainty note:
- Mild croup — barking cough, no stridor at rest, child drinking normally —
  can be monitored at home with clear safety net
- Stridor at rest or child unable to drink — escalate urgently
- Any stridor in a child under 6 months — escalate immediately

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE
- Any cough with fast breathing in a child under 5 — treat as serious and
  escalate urgently
- Any cough with poor feeding in an infant — escalate same day
- If pattern unclear after maximum questions — default to possible chest infection
  and advise urgent clinic visit
- Never advise home management for cough with fast breathing in a child
- Malnourished child with any respiratory symptoms — escalate sooner, illness
  is always more severe
- When in doubt — escalate. Uncertainty is never a reason to reassure.
