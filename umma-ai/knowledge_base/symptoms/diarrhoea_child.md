## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

### Confirming the symptom
Caregivers in northern Nigeria may describe diarrhoea as "zawo" or "gudawa" — both are
widely understood Hausa terms. Confirm what is meant: loose or watery stools, more frequent
than the child's normal pattern. In exclusively breastfed infants, frequent loose seedy stools
are normal — clarify whether this is a change from the child's usual pattern.

Establish age immediately:
- Any diarrhoea in a child under 2 months — urgent escalation. Do not proceed to disease reasoning.
- Any diarrhoea in a child under 6 months — treat as urgent throughout.

Clarify key features if not already stated:
- How many loose stools in the past 24 hours?
- Is there blood in the stool?
- Is the child still drinking and able to keep fluids down?

### Symptoms that may be confused with diarrhoea
- Normal frequent loose stools in an exclusively breastfed newborn — normal pattern, no change
  in behaviour or feeding, no blood
- Constipation with overflow — small amounts of liquid stool around a hard blockage;
  ask about the last normal stool
- Mucus in stool without true diarrhoea — may be normal in infants

### Local language and cultural context
- Zawo — diarrhoea (most commonly used Hausa term in Kano)
- Gudawa — diarrhoea (also widely used)
- Zawo mai jini — bloody diarrhoea (red flag — escalate urgently)
- Gudawa mai yawa — frequent diarrhoea
- Amai da zawo — vomiting and diarrhoea together (dehydration risk escalates rapidly)
- Bai sha ruwa ba — not drinking at all (red flag — emergency dehydration)
- Idanunsa sun ja ciki — sunken eyes (red flag — significant dehydration)

Caregivers in Kano commonly withhold food and fluids during diarrhoea based on the belief
that feeding worsens it. This is incorrect and dangerous — it accelerates dehydration.
Address this belief directly and firmly but respectfully if it comes up.

Traditional remedies including agbo and herbal preparations are commonly given to children
with diarrhoea in northern Nigeria before presentation. Always ask what has already been given.
Some traditional remedies can worsen diarrhoea or cause harm in young children.

Open defecation, contaminated water sources, and poor hand hygiene are significant contributing
factors to diarrhoeal disease in Kano. Rotavirus, enterotoxigenic E. coli, and Shigella are
important causative organisms. Cholera must be considered during outbreak periods.

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

Escalate immediately if any of the following are present. Do not proceed to disease reasoning.
Ordered from most dangerous to least dangerous.

- Any diarrhoea in a child under 2 months — emergency
- Child unconscious or very difficult to wake
- Child limp and unresponsive — unable to sit, stand, or hold head
- Child unable to drink at all — refuses all fluids
- Signs of severe dehydration — sunken eyes, very dry mouth and tongue, no tears when crying,
  skin pinch returns very slowly (more than 2 seconds), no urine for 8 or more hours
- Sunken fontanelle in an infant — the soft spot on the head is depressed inward
- Blood in stool with high fever and child looking very unwell — possible dysentery or
  haemorrhagic colitis
- Diarrhoea with convulsion
- Severe vomiting alongside diarrhoea — unable to keep any fluid down for more than 4 hours
- Very watery rice-water stools with rapid onset — possible cholera, especially during
  outbreak periods in Kano
- Diarrhoea in a malnourished child who is visibly wasted — dehydration is more dangerous
  and develops faster
- Diarrhoea persisting more than 14 days — persistent diarrhoea; always needs clinic assessment

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

### Nigerian and Kano context
Diarrhoeal disease is among the three leading killers of children under 5 in Nigeria. In Kano
and northwest Nigeria, diarrhoea kills children primarily through dehydration. Dehydration
in a young child can progress from mild to fatal within hours — particularly in children who
are already malnourished.

Malnutrition, which affects over 60% of children under 5 in Kano, creates a lethal cycle with
diarrhoea — each episode worsens nutritional status, and malnutrition worsens diarrhoea severity.
Rotavirus is the leading cause of severe diarrhoea in young children in Nigeria. Cholera causes
outbreaks in Kano. Bloody diarrhoea (dysentery) is primarily caused by Shigella in this context.
ORS is available free or cheaply at all primary health centres and is the cornerstone of treatment.

---

### DISEASE 1: ACUTE WATERY DIARRHOEA — VIRAL GASTROENTERITIS INCLUDING ROTAVIRUS
Nigerian priority: HIGHEST — most common cause of acute diarrhoea in children under 5

Key differentiating features:
- Profuse watery diarrhoea — large volume, frequent
- Vomiting common especially in early disease
- Fever — low-grade or moderate; high fever less common
- No blood in stool
- Rapid dehydration — sunken eyes, dry mouth, reduced urine output
- Rotavirus most common in children under 2 years
- Spreads rapidly in households and communities

Differentiating questions:
- How many watery stools has the child had in the past 24 hours?
- Is there any blood or mucus in the stool?
- Has the child been vomiting?
- Is the child still able to drink and keep fluids down?
- How does the child's mouth look — wet or dry?

Confirm towards viral gastroenteritis if:
- Profuse watery diarrhoea + vomiting + no blood + age under 2 years

Uncertainty note:
- The cause of watery diarrhoea matters less than the degree of dehydration
- Assessment of hydration status is the clinical priority regardless of cause
- Rotavirus and bacterial gastroenteritis cannot be reliably distinguished clinically

---

### DISEASE 2: DYSENTERY — SHIGELLA OR OTHER INVASIVE BACTERIAL INFECTION
Nigerian priority: HIGH — Shigella is the most common cause of bloody diarrhoea in children
in Nigeria

Key differentiating features:
- Blood in stool — may be streaks or frank blood mixed with stool
- Mucus in stool
- High fever — more prominent than in viral gastroenteritis
- Abdominal pain and cramping — child may cry before each stool
- Tenesmus — straining and feeling of incomplete emptying in older children
- Child looks more unwell than expected for the degree of diarrhoea
- Seizures can occur in Shigella dysentery — Shigella toxin is neurotoxic

Differentiating questions:
- Is there blood in the stool — even a small amount or streaks?
- Does the child have abdominal pain, especially before passing a stool?
- Does the child have a high fever?
- Is the child straining to pass stool even after finishing?

Confirm towards dysentery if:
- Blood in stool + fever + abdominal pain regardless of volume of diarrhoea

Uncertainty note:
- Any blood in the stool of a febrile child — treat as dysentery and escalate urgently
- Shigella dysentery can cause seizures — any convulsion in a child with bloody diarrhoea
  is an emergency
- Do not attempt to manage bloody diarrhoea at home

---

### DISEASE 3: CHOLERA
Nigerian priority: HIGH during outbreak periods — Kano has experienced cholera outbreaks

Key differentiating features:
- Profuse watery diarrhoea — very large volumes; described as rice-water stools
  (grey, watery, no faecal smell) in classical presentation
- Rapid onset dehydration — severe dehydration can develop within hours
- Vomiting common early in the illness
- Usually no fever or low-grade fever only — high fever suggests another cause
- Muscle cramps in older children and adults — less prominent in young children
- Outbreak context — other family members or community members affected
- Source of unsafe water or food identified

Differentiating questions:
- Is the diarrhoea extremely watery and coming in very large amounts?
- Has anyone else in the family or neighbourhood had similar sudden severe diarrhoea?
- Is there a known outbreak of diarrhoea in the area?

Confirm towards cholera if:
- Profuse rice-water diarrhoea + rapid severe dehydration + outbreak context

Uncertainty note:
- During a known cholera outbreak — any severe acute watery diarrhoea should be managed
  as possible cholera and escalated to hospital urgently
- Cholera dehydration is faster and more severe than other diarrhoeal diseases

---

### DISEASE 4: MALARIA WITH DIARRHOEA
Nigerian priority: HIGH — malaria commonly presents with diarrhoea in young children

Key differentiating features:
- Fever prominent — with or without chills and sweating
- Diarrhoea present but not always the dominant symptom
- Vomiting very common in malaria in young children
- Pallor — inner eyelids pale
- Child looks generally unwell beyond just the diarrhoea
- No blood in stool
- Diarrhoea may be watery

Differentiating questions:
- Does the child have a fever alongside the diarrhoea?
- Has the child been shivering or sweating?
- Are the inside of the child's lower eyelids looking pale?
- Has the child been sleeping under a mosquito net?

Confirm towards malaria with diarrhoea if:
- Fever + diarrhoea + pallor + vomiting in a child under 5 with no mosquito net use

Uncertainty note:
- Malaria can cause diarrhoea directly, especially in young children
- Malaria and gastroenteritis can coexist — one does not exclude the other
- Never rule out malaria in a febrile Nigerian child with diarrhoea

---

### DISEASE 5: PERSISTENT DIARRHOEA AND MALNUTRITION CYCLE
Nigerian priority: HIGH — affects enormous numbers of children in Kano

Key differentiating features:
- Diarrhoea lasting more than 14 days — persistent diarrhoea by definition
- Child visibly thin or wasted — signs of malnutrition
- Hair changes — reddish or sparse hair in kwashiorkor
- Swelling of feet and legs — oedematous malnutrition (kwashiorkor)
- Poor weight gain or weight loss over weeks to months
- Multiple previous episodes of diarrhoea
- Frequent infections — child has been sick repeatedly

Differentiating questions:
- How long has the diarrhoea been going on?
- Has the child been losing weight or not growing well over the past few months?
- Has the child had multiple episodes of illness over the past few months?
- Does the child have swelling in the feet or legs?

Confirm towards persistent diarrhoea with malnutrition if:
- Diarrhoea more than 14 days + weight loss or poor growth + multiple prior episodes

Uncertainty note:
- Persistent diarrhoea in a malnourished child always needs clinic assessment
- The malnutrition-diarrhoea cycle cannot be broken at home — nutritional rehabilitation
  requires clinic or hospital-based care
- Do not advise home management for persistent diarrhoea regardless of other findings

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE
- Any diarrhoea in a child under 2 months — escalate immediately
- Any blood in the stool of a febrile child — escalate urgently
- Severe dehydration signs — escalate as emergency
- Diarrhoea in a malnourished child — escalate sooner; dehydration progresses faster
- Persistent diarrhoea more than 14 days — always needs clinic assessment
- When in doubt about hydration status — escalate; dehydration in a young child
  in Kano is a life-threatening emergency that moves faster than it looks.
