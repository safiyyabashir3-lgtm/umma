# UMMA Symptom File: Headache in Pregnancy

---

## SECTION 1: SYMPTOM VERIFICATION

**Confirm the symptom:**
- Ask where the pain is located (front, back, sides, whole head), how severe it is, and how long it has been present
- Confirm gestational age — headache after 20 weeks requires immediate pre-eclampsia screening
- Distinguish from neck pain or jaw pain which may be reported as headache

**Commonly confused with:**
- Neck stiffness or pain reported as headache — clarify location
- Eye pain or sinus pain reported as headache — ask about eye changes and nasal symptoms
- Dizziness or lightheadedness described as head pain — clarify whether the head actually hurts or whether the woman feels unsteady

**Critical immediate checks:**
- Gestational age: if 20 weeks or beyond, pre-eclampsia is the first differential until excluded
- Ask immediately about visual changes, swelling of face or hands, and epigastric pain — these together with headache constitute a pre-eclampsia emergency
- Ask about fever — headache with fever may indicate malaria or meningitis

---

## SECTION 2: TRIAGE — RED FLAGS

Escalate immediately if any of the following are present:

1. Severe headache after 20 weeks with visual disturbance (blurring, flashing lights, seeing spots) — eclampsia or severe pre-eclampsia
2. Headache with sudden onset, described as the worst headache of her life — intracranial haemorrhage
3. Headache with convulsions or loss of consciousness
4. Headache with neck stiffness and fever — meningitis
5. Headache with confusion, disorientation, or inability to recognise family
6. Headache with face, hand, or whole body swelling after 20 weeks
7. Headache with severe epigastric or right upper quadrant abdominal pain
8. Headache with inability to see clearly or sudden vision loss
9. Headache with vomiting that is new, severe, or projectile in the third trimester
10. Headache not relieved by paracetamol in a woman with known or suspected hypertension
11. Headache present for more than 24 hours without any relief
12. Any headache in a woman with a known history of hypertension or pre-eclampsia in a previous pregnancy
13. Headache with reduced or absent fetal movement
14. Woman or family member reports this feels different or more severe than any previous headache

## SECTION 3: CLINICAL REASONING GUIDE

---

### DISEASE 1: PRE-ECLAMPSIA / ECLAMPSIA
**Nigerian priority level: HIGHEST**

Pre-eclampsia is the most dangerous cause of headache after 20 weeks of pregnancy and must always be the first differential considered. It is a leading cause of maternal death in Nigeria. Eclampsia (seizures from pre-eclampsia) can occur without warning.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Headache after 20 weeks gestation
- Swelling of face, hands, or legs — may be noticed by the woman or family
- Visual changes: blurring, flashing lights, temporary vision loss
- Epigastric or right upper quadrant pain (HELLP syndrome component)
- Hypertension if blood pressure has been checked
- Proteinuria if urine has been tested
- Previous history of pre-eclampsia increases risk significantly

**Differentiating questions:**
- How many weeks pregnant is she?
- Has her blood pressure been checked recently — was it high?
- Has she noticed swelling in her face, hands, or feet that was not there before?
- Is her vision blurry, or does she see flashing lights or spots?
- Does she have pain under her ribs on the right side or in the upper part of her stomach?
- Has she had pre-eclampsia or high blood pressure in a previous pregnancy?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Headache at or after 20 weeks
- Plus any one of: visual changes, face or hand swelling, epigastric pain, known hypertension
- Previous pre-eclampsia history
- Headache unrelieved by paracetamol

**Uncertainty note:**
If pre-eclampsia cannot be excluded in any woman with headache after 20 weeks, escalate immediately. Blood pressure measurement is required to exclude it — this cannot be done remotely. Never reassure a woman with headache after 20 weeks without confirmed normal blood pressure.

---

### DISEASE 2: MALARIA
**Nigerian priority level: HIGHEST**

Malaria is a major cause of headache in pregnancy across Nigeria and must be considered alongside pre-eclampsia in any woman with headache, especially if fever or chills are also present.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Headache accompanied by fever, chills, or sweating
- Cyclical pattern — comes in waves
- Body aches and joint pain
- Pallor of conjunctiva or palms suggesting anaemia
- History of poor bednet use or missed IPTp doses

**Differentiating questions:**
- Does she have fever or has she been feeling hot and cold?
- Has she been sweating heavily?
- Does she have pain in her body or joints alongside the headache?
- Is she sleeping under a mosquito net?
- Has she taken her IPTp (malaria prevention) tablets this pregnancy?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Headache with fever and cyclical pattern
- Body aches and chills present
- Anaemia signs
- No swelling, no visual changes, no hypertension history

**Uncertainty note:**
Malaria and pre-eclampsia can coexist. If headache is present after 20 weeks with fever, both must be evaluated. Escalate for both to be assessed at facility. Do not allow a malaria diagnosis to delay pre-eclampsia screening.

---

### DISEASE 3: TENSION HEADACHE / DEHYDRATION HEADACHE
**Nigerian priority level: MEDIUM**

Tension-type and dehydration headaches are common in pregnancy due to hormonal changes, poor fluid intake in hot climates, and fatigue. However, these are diagnoses of exclusion — dangerous causes must be ruled out first.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Mild to moderate headache, typically bilateral, described as pressure or tightness
- No fever, no visual changes, no swelling
- Associated with tiredness, poor sleep, or inadequate fluid intake
- Relieved by rest, fluids, or paracetamol
- Occurs in first or second trimester more commonly than third
- No history of hypertension

**Differentiating questions:**
- Is the headache mild or moderate rather than severe?
- Does rest or drinking water make it better?
- Has she been drinking enough water in this heat?
- Has she been sleeping poorly or feeling very tired?
- Has paracetamol helped when she has taken it?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Mild to moderate headache, bilateral
- No fever, no swelling, no visual symptoms
- Relieved by fluids or paracetamol
- Under 20 weeks gestation
- No hypertension history

**Uncertainty note:**
Even if tension headache seems likely, if the woman is over 20 weeks, blood pressure must still be checked at a facility before this diagnosis is accepted. Do not reassure a woman in the third trimester based on headache character alone.

---

### DISEASE 4: MENINGITIS
**Nigerian priority level: HIGH**

Bacterial meningitis occurs in pregnancy and carries high maternal and fetal mortality. Nigeria sits within the meningitis belt and epidemic seasons affect northern Nigeria including Kano state. It must be considered in any severe headache with fever and neck stiffness.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Severe headache with fever — both present together
- Neck stiffness — woman cannot bend neck forward comfortably
- Photophobia — pain when looking at bright light
- Vomiting alongside headache and fever
- Rash (meningococcal — non-blanching, may be difficult to see on dark skin)
- Confusion or altered consciousness

**Differentiating questions:**
- Does she have a fever alongside the headache?
- Is her neck stiff — can she bring her chin to her chest?
- Does bright light hurt her eyes?
- Is she vomiting alongside the headache?
- Is she confused or not acting normally?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Severe headache plus fever plus neck stiffness — any combination of two or more
- Photophobia present
- Confusion or altered mental state
- During meningitis season (dry season, harmattan months in northern Nigeria)

**Uncertainty note:**
Neck stiffness with headache and fever is meningitis until proven otherwise. Escalate as emergency. Do not delay for observation.

---

### DISEASE 5: MIGRAINE
**Nigerian priority level: MEDIUM**

Migraine can occur in pregnancy, though it often improves in the second trimester. It is a diagnosis of exclusion and must never be assumed without ruling out pre-eclampsia and other dangerous causes.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Unilateral, throbbing headache
- Previous history of migraines before or during this pregnancy
- Nausea and vomiting accompanying headache
- Sensitivity to light and sound
- Aura (visual disturbance before headache) — this must be carefully distinguished from pre-eclamptic visual symptoms
- No fever, no swelling, no hypertension

**Differentiating questions:**
- Has she had migraines before this pregnancy?
- Is the pain on one side of the head and throbbing?
- Did she see zigzag lines, flashing lights, or blind spots before the headache started?
- Is she sensitive to light or sound?
- Has her blood pressure been checked recently and was it normal?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Clear prior history of migraine with the same pattern
- Unilateral throbbing headache with nausea
- Confirmed normal blood pressure
- No swelling, no fever, no new visual changes beyond typical aura

**Uncertainty note:**
Migraine aura (visual symptoms before headache) must be distinguished from pre-eclamptic visual disturbance. If there is any doubt, or if the woman is over 20 weeks with visual symptoms, escalate for blood pressure check. Migraine cannot be diagnosed remotely in pregnancy.

---

### DISEASE 6: ANAEMIA-RELATED HEADACHE
**Nigerian priority level: MEDIUM**

Severe anaemia is common in pregnancy in northern Nigeria, particularly from malaria, nutritional deficiency, and sickle cell disease. Headache is a common symptom of significant anaemia.

**Key differentiating features:**
- Headache with pallor of conjunctiva, lips, or palms
- Dizziness, weakness, or easy fatigue alongside headache
- Breathlessness on mild exertion
- History of sickle cell disease, poor diet, or repeated malaria
- No fever necessarily present

**Differentiating questions:**
- Have people told her she looks pale or does she notice paleness in her own eyes or palms?
- Does she feel very weak or dizzy alongside the headache?
- Does she get short of breath easily?
- Does she have sickle cell disease?
- Has she had malaria frequently during this pregnancy?

**Confirm towards if:**
- Headache with visible pallor signs
- Weakness and dizziness accompanying headache
- History of sickle cell or repeated malaria
- Breathlessness on exertion

**Uncertainty note:**
Severe anaemia in pregnancy requires facility assessment. Do not manage at home. Escalate any woman with headache and pallor signs.

---

### GENERAL UNCERTAINTY RULE — HEADACHE IN PREGNANCY

Any headache in pregnancy after 20 weeks must be treated as pre-eclampsia until blood pressure is measured and found to be normal at a facility. Any headache with fever must be evaluated for malaria and meningitis. Any severe, sudden, or unrelieved headache in pregnancy requires emergency escalation. When the cause cannot be clearly identified and safely excluded, escalate.
