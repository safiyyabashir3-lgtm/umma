# Abdominal Pain — Symptom Care File (Child)
**UMMA AI | Maternal & Child Health | Kano/Northern Nigeria Context**
**Target: Children 0–5 years**

---

## ACTION TIERS

### EMERGENCY — Go to hospital immediately
**Triggered by:**
- Abdomen is hard, swollen, or board-like to touch
- Child has bile-green or bloody vomit
- Blood visible in stool (red or black/tarry stool)
- Child is in severe, constant pain — not coming and going in waves
- Child has not passed urine in 8+ hours (in infant) or 12+ hours (in older child)
- Child is unconscious, floppy, or cannot be roused
- Child has a visible lump or swelling in the groin or scrotum (possible hernia)
- Pain follows a fall, injury, or blunt blow to the abdomen
- Child under 3 months with any abdominal pain or distension

**Plain language:**
"This is very serious. Your child needs a hospital right now — not a chemist, not a clinic. If you can, go to the nearest government hospital or teaching hospital. Do not give any medicine or food before you go. If you cannot get transport, ask a neighbor, call someone, or go to the nearest health post and tell them your child needs emergency help."

---

### URGENT CLINIC — Go today, same day
**Triggered by:**
- Pain has lasted more than 6 hours without improving
- Child has vomited 3 or more times and cannot keep water down
- Child has diarrhea with signs of dehydration (sunken eyes, dry mouth, no tears, skin that stays up when pinched)
- Child has fever above 38.5°C alongside abdominal pain
- Child is under 1 year with pain lasting more than 1–2 hours
- Pain is located only on the right lower side of the belly
- Child is crying and pulling up their legs repeatedly (possible intussusception in infants)

**Plain language:**
"Your child needs to be seen by a nurse or doctor today — not tomorrow. Go to your nearest primary health care centre (PHC) or clinic this morning or this afternoon. Tell them your child has stomach pain and has been vomiting [or has fever, or cannot keep water down]. Do not wait to see if it gets better on its own."

---

### HOME CARE — Only if ALL of these are true
**Conditions for home care:**
- Child is older than 6 months
- Pain is mild and comes and goes (not constant)
- No fever, or fever is low (under 38°C)
- No vomiting, or vomited only once
- No blood in stool
- Child is still drinking fluids and passing urine normally
- Child is alert, responding, and not in severe distress
- No hard or distended abdomen

**Home care instructions:**
1. Let the child rest — lying down quietly often helps
2. Offer small sips of clean water or ORS frequently — do not force large amounts
3. Do not give solid food until pain passes
4. Do not give adult pain medicines — paracetamol syrup (child's dose on the packet) can be given if child also has fever
5. Do not give local herbal remedies — some can be harmful to a child's stomach and liver
6. Gently rub the child's belly in a circular motion if they find it soothing
7. Check the child every 30 minutes

**Plain language:**
"For now, it is safe to watch your child at home, but you must watch carefully. Give small sips of water or ORS. Let them rest. Do not give herbal medicines or 'agbo.' Check on them every 30 minutes. If the pain gets worse, does not go away in a few hours, or any of the danger signs appear — go to the clinic right away."

---

### MONITOR — With clear safety net
**Conditions:**
- Pain has just started and is very mild
- Child is alert, playful between pain episodes
- No danger signs present
- Child is feeding or drinking normally

**What to watch:**
- Is the pain getting stronger or more frequent?
- Is the belly becoming swollen or hard?
- Is the child vomiting?
- Is there any blood in the stool?
- Is the child becoming weak, drowsy, or unresponsive?
- Is the child passing urine normally?

**Plain language:**
"Watch your child closely for the next 1–2 hours. If the pain goes away and they are playing and drinking normally, that is a good sign. But keep watching for any of the danger signs below. If anything changes or gets worse — do not wait, go to the clinic."

---

### UNCERTAIN — When UMMA cannot determine severity
**Plain language:**
"I am not sure how serious your child's stomach pain is based on what you have told me. When I am not sure, I always recommend going to a clinic or health post so a nurse or doctor can check your child properly. It is always better to go and find out it was nothing serious than to wait at home. Please go today."

---

## SAFETY NET
**Go to hospital immediately if any of these happen:**
- Your child's belly becomes hard, swollen, or tight like a drum
- You see blood in the stool — red blood or black/tar-coloured stool
- Your child vomits green or yellow-green fluid
- Your child stops passing urine
- Your child becomes very weak, floppy, or difficult to wake up
- The pain becomes constant and very severe — not coming and going
- You see a lump in the groin or between the legs
- Your child is under 3 months and has stomach pain

---

## GENERAL SAFE ADVICE
- ORS (oral rehydration salts) is the safest drink when a child has vomiting or diarrhea alongside pain — it replaces lost salts and water. It is available at most government health posts and pharmacies.
- Never give aspirin or ibuprofen to children with abdominal pain — these can harm the stomach lining
- Paracetamol syrup is safe for pain relief if the child also has fever — use the correct child dose on the packet
- If a child has not had a bowel movement in 3+ days and complains of abdominal pain, they may be constipated — increase fluids and see a nurse
- A child who eats something unusual, dirty water, or spoiled food can develop pain quickly — mention this to the nurse or doctor

---

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC CARE ADVICE
- **Herbal remedies:** Many families use local herbs ("agbo," "ororo," bark teas) for stomach pain. UMMA understands this is a common practice and part of many families' traditions. However, these preparations can irritate or injure a child's stomach, liver, and kidneys, and some have caused serious harm in young children. Please do not give herbal remedies to children under 5 for abdominal pain — it is the safest choice for your child.
- **Patent medicine vendors (PMVs):** If you visit a PMV, tell them the child's age and symptoms clearly. Do not accept any injection or adult tablet. Paracetamol syrup is the only safe over-the-counter option for a young child.
- **Transport barriers:** If you are far from a clinic and cannot get transport, go to your nearest PHC even if it is small — they can assess the child and arrange referral if needed. Do not wait for the pain to "pass on its own" for more than a few hours.
- **Heat:** In Kano's hot climate, children dehydrate quickly. Abdominal pain combined with heat exposure can escalate fast — push fluids early.
- **Family support:** Ask a family member or neighbor to come with you. Having another adult present helps you focus on the child and navigate the health facility.
