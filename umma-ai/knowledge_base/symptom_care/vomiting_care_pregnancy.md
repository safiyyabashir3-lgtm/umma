# Vomiting — Symptom Care File (Pregnancy)
**UMMA AI | Maternal & Child Health | Kano/Northern Nigeria Context**
**Target: Pregnant women, all trimesters**
**Covers: Nausea and vomiting of pregnancy, hyperemesis gravidarum, vomiting with other causes**

---

## CRITICAL NOTE FOR UMMA
Nausea and vomiting are extremely common in the first trimester. Mild to moderate nausea and vomiting of pregnancy (NVP) is benign and managed at home. However, hyperemesis gravidarum — severe, persistent vomiting causing dehydration, weight loss, and electrolyte imbalance — is a medical emergency that requires hospital management. Vomiting in the second or third trimester is not typical of NVP and must prompt investigation for other causes: malaria, pre-eclampsia, appendicitis, urinary infection, or liver disease. UMMA must stratify vomiting carefully by trimester, severity, and associated symptoms.

## ACTION TIERS

### EMERGENCY — Go to hospital immediately
**Triggered by:**
- Vomiting with complete inability to keep any fluid down for 24 hours or more
- Signs of severe dehydration — no urine for 8 hours, sunken eyes, very dry cracked mouth, confusion or extreme weakness
- Vomiting blood — either bright red or dark brown ("coffee grounds") appearance
- Vomiting with severe headache and visual disturbance — possible pre-eclampsia
- Vomiting with severe abdominal pain — possible appendicitis, placental abruption, or other surgical emergency
- Vomiting with high fever and rigors — possible severe malaria or sepsis
- Vomiting with yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice) — possible liver disease
- Vomiting in the third trimester that is new, severe, or worsening — always needs investigation
- Vomiting with seizure — possible eclampsia
- Woman is so weak she cannot stand or walk
- Vomiting following a fall, injury, or trauma
- Persistent vomiting that has caused significant weight loss — visibly thinner, clothes much looser

**Plain language:**
"This level of vomiting is dangerous — your body is losing too much fluid and you cannot replace it at home. You need fluids through a drip at the hospital. Please go now. If you are too weak to travel alone, ask someone to help you. Bring your antenatal card. Do not take any medicine or herbal preparation before going."

---

### URGENT CLINIC — Go today, same day
**Triggered by:**
- Vomiting more than 5 times in 24 hours and keeping very little fluid down
- Vomiting for more than 3 days without any improvement
- Vomiting with fever at or above 37.5°C at any stage of pregnancy
- Vomiting with back or loin pain (possible kidney infection)
- Vomiting with burning when passing urine (possible UTI worsening)
- Vomiting in the second trimester (after 14 weeks) — less expected and needs a cause identified
- Vomiting with reduced fetal movements after 28 weeks
- Vomiting that is worsening rather than improving after 12 weeks of pregnancy
- Woman has not passed urine in 6–8 hours
- Vomiting with dark or very reduced urine
- Vomiting with any swelling of face or hands
- Woman cannot take prescribed medications (iron, folic acid, malaria prophylaxis) due to vomiting

**Plain language:**
"This vomiting needs to be seen by a nurse today. You may need fluids and medicine to stop it safely. Go to the PHC or ANC clinic today. Tell the nurse how many times you have vomited in the last 24 hours, whether you have been able to drink anything, and when you last passed urine. Take your antenatal card."

---

### HOME CARE — Only if ALL of these are true
**Conditions for home care:**
- Vomiting is in the first trimester (up to 14 weeks) with a known pattern of morning sickness
- Vomiting is fewer than 5 times per day and woman can keep some fluid down
- No signs of dehydration — she is passing urine at least every 6–8 hours; urine is pale, not dark
- No fever, no abdominal pain, no headache
- No blood in vomit
- Baby is moving normally if past 28 weeks
- Woman is maintaining some nutrition even if reduced
- No weight loss that is visible or alarming

**Home care instructions:**
1. Eat small amounts frequently — do not eat large meals; an empty stomach worsens nausea
2. Eat dry, plain foods first thing in the morning before getting out of bed — dry toast, plain biscuits, plain pap, crackers
3. Avoid foods with strong smells — cooking smells are a major trigger; ask family members to cook outside or open windows when cooking
4. Ginger is safe in pregnancy and can help with nausea — small amounts of fresh ginger in warm water or plain food; ginger biscuits; ginger tea without other additives
5. Cold or room-temperature foods cause less nausea than hot, steaming dishes
6. Sip fluids slowly and frequently throughout the day — small sips of water, diluted juice, or ORS; do not try to drink large amounts at once
7. Rest as much as possible — tiredness worsens nausea significantly
8. Avoid triggers — strong smells, heat, and being in a moving vehicle on an empty stomach
9. Do NOT take any anti-nausea medicine bought from a patent medicine vendor without a nurse's prescription — some are not safe in pregnancy
10. Do NOT use ginger in very large amounts — as a spice and mild remedy it is safe; as a concentrated extract or capsule, it has not been adequately studied in pregnancy
11. If taking iron tablets and they are worsening nausea: take them with food, take them at bedtime, or ask the nurse about splitting the dose — do not stop taking them without the nurse's advice

**Plain language:**
"Eat small amounts often. Start the morning with plain dry food before getting up. Sip water throughout the day — small sips, not large amounts. Rest. Ask family to cook outside if smells are triggering vomiting. Ginger in small amounts is safe and can help. If you cannot keep any fluid down or you feel very weak — go to the clinic that day."

### MONITOR — With clear safety net
**Conditions:**
- First trimester, mild nausea and occasional vomiting (1–2 times per day)
- No dehydration — urine is normal colour and frequency
- Woman is able to keep most fluids and some food down
- No fever or other symptoms
- Vomiting follows a predictable pattern — worse in the morning or with specific triggers

**What to watch:**
- Is the vomiting getting more frequent rather than less?
- Is the woman unable to keep fluids down at all?
- Has urine output reduced — dark urine, or not passing urine?
- Has fever, abdominal pain, or headache developed?
- Is vomiting persisting beyond 14 weeks without improvement?
- Is the woman losing weight visibly or becoming very weak?

**Plain language:**
"Watch yourself carefully. As long as you are keeping some fluid down and passing urine regularly, you can manage at home with the advice above. If the vomiting gets worse, you cannot keep anything down, or you feel very weak — go to the clinic that day. Do not wait more than 24 hours if you cannot keep any fluid down."

---

### UNCERTAIN — When UMMA cannot determine severity
**Plain language:**
"I am not completely sure whether your vomiting is the normal sickness of early pregnancy or something that needs treatment at the clinic. If you are unsure whether you are keeping enough fluid down — the safest test is whether you are passing urine every 6–8 hours and whether it is pale. If not, go to the clinic today. When in doubt, I always recommend being seen by a nurse."

## SAFETY NET
**Go to hospital immediately if any of these happen:**
- You have not passed urine for 8 hours
- Your mouth is very dry and you cannot produce any saliva or tears
- You vomit blood — red or dark brown material
- You feel very confused, extremely weak, or cannot stand
- You develop a severe headache or your vision changes alongside the vomiting
- You develop fever with shaking chills alongside vomiting
- Vomiting starts or worsens after 14 weeks — especially in the second or third trimester
- Your baby is moving less than normal after 28 weeks alongside vomiting

## GENERAL SAFE ADVICE
- Nausea and vomiting of pregnancy (NVP) affects up to 80% of pregnant women; it typically begins between 4–6 weeks and improves by 12–14 weeks in most women; it is caused by rising human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG) and oestrogen and is a sign of a hormonally active pregnancy
- Hyperemesis gravidarum — severe, persistent vomiting causing dehydration, weight loss greater than 5% of body weight, and electrolyte imbalance — occurs in 1–2% of pregnancies and requires IV fluids and antiemetic medication in hospital
- Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) in the correct dose has been shown to reduce nausea safely in pregnancy — ask the nurse at ANC if this is available; it may be provided
- Iron tablets are a very common cause of worsened nausea in pregnancy — taking them with food, at bedtime, or switching to a different iron formulation can help; do not stop iron without the nurse's advice as anaemia in pregnancy is dangerous
- Malaria is a common cause of vomiting with fever in pregnant Nigerian women and must be investigated whenever vomiting is accompanied by fever in any trimester
- Severe vomiting in the second or third trimester can be a sign of pre-eclampsia, liver disease of pregnancy (acute fatty liver, cholestasis, HELLP syndrome), or appendicitis — these require urgent hospital investigation

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC CARE ADVICE
- **Herbal remedies for vomiting:** Many families in Kano use local herbal teas, bitter preparations, or traditional drinks to settle the stomach in pregnancy. UMMA understands this is a familiar and caring response to a distressing symptom. However, many herbal preparations contain compounds that are toxic in early pregnancy — some have been associated with miscarriage and liver damage. Ginger in small culinary amounts (fresh ginger root, ginger tea without additives) is the one traditional plant remedy with reasonable safety evidence. Other herbal preparations should be avoided.
- **Food aversions and cultural context:** Pregnancy food aversions are medically recognised and often culturally reinforced. In northern Nigeria, a woman who cannot eat may be advised by family to try local comfort foods — fura da nono, kunu, tuwon shinkafa. These are acceptable options if tolerated and are nutritious. The priority is keeping something down, not eating any specific food.
- **Stigma around hospital attendance for vomiting:** Some women and families feel that vomiting is "normal" in pregnancy and that going to hospital for it is unnecessary or embarrassing. UMMA wants to be clear: going to the hospital for severe vomiting in pregnancy is appropriate and correct — doctors and nurses take hyperemesis gravidarum seriously and treat it actively. It can kill.
- **Antiemetic medications:** Medicines to stop vomiting — such as promethazine (Phenergan), metoclopramide, or ondansetron — can be prescribed by a nurse or doctor in pregnancy. They are not all freely available over the counter and some require careful dosing in pregnancy. Do not buy anti-nausea medicine from a PMV without a prescription — ask for a prescription at the PHC.
- **Oral rehydration salts (ORS):** If a woman with vomiting can keep small sips down, ORS — available free at PHCs and cheaply at pharmacies — is better than plain water because it replaces lost salt and potassium alongside fluid. Sip slowly, as little as a teaspoon every few minutes if necessary.
- **Iron tablets and compliance:** Non-compliance with iron and folic acid tablets is very common in northern Nigeria, and vomiting is one of the most frequently reported reasons. This leads to severe anaemia by the third trimester. Ask the nurse about taking iron at night with food if nausea is a problem. Missing iron in the first trimester — a critical period for fetal development — has lasting consequences. Seek a solution rather than stopping the tablets.
- **Multiple pregnancy:** Women carrying twins or more produce higher levels of hCG and have significantly worse nausea and vomiting. If you have been told you are carrying more than one baby and the vomiting is severe — go to the clinic earlier than you might otherwise.
