# Symptom: CONVULSION (SEIZURE) IN A CHILD (under 5 years)
# Context: Nigeria / Northern Nigeria (Kano)

---

## IMPORTANT NOTE FOR UMMA
A convulsion is always a serious symptom. There is NO home care tier for active convulsions. Every child who has had a convulsion must be seen at a health facility, even if the seizure has stopped. The tiers below address what to do during and after a convulsion.

---

## ACTION TIERS

### EMERGENCY — Go to hospital immediately
**Triggered by:**
- Child is having a convulsion right now — any shaking, jerking, stiffening, eyes rolling, or unresponsiveness
- Child has had a convulsion today, even if it has stopped and the child seems better
- Convulsion lasted more than 5 minutes
- Child has had more than one convulsion today
- Child does not return to normal consciousness after the convulsion stops — remains very drowsy, confused, or limp for more than 30 minutes
- Convulsion in a child under 6 months
- Convulsion without fever (could be epilepsy or a serious brain condition)
- Convulsion with stiff neck, severe headache, or sensitivity to light
- Child has difficulty breathing after the convulsion
- Child swallowed something before the convulsion

**Plain language:**
"Every child who has a seizure must go to hospital immediately — even if the seizure has stopped. Please go now. Do not wait to see if the child improves at home. A health worker must find out why this happened and check that your child is safe."

---

### URGENT CLINIC — Go today, same day
**Triggered by:**
- This tier does NOT apply to convulsions. Every child who has had a convulsion goes to hospital today, same day, even if recovered — no exceptions.

**Plain language:**
"Even if your child seems fine now, please take them to hospital today. A convulsion is always a warning sign that needs proper assessment."

---

### HOME CARE — Only if ALL of these are true
**This tier does NOT apply to convulsions.**
Every child who has had a convulsion must be seen by a health worker. There is no safe home management for a child who has had a seizure.

**Plain language:**
"There is no safe home treatment for a child who has had a convulsion. Please go to hospital."

---

### WHAT TO DO DURING A CONVULSION (First aid while awaiting transport)

1. **Stay calm.** Your child needs you to act clearly.
2. **Lay the child on their side** (recovery position) — on a flat, safe surface. This prevents choking if the child vomits.
3. **Do not restrain the child** — do not hold the arms and legs down. You cannot stop the seizure by force and restraint can cause injury.
4. **Do not put anything in the child's mouth** — do not put a spoon, cloth, finger, or any object in the mouth. A child cannot swallow their tongue. Putting objects in the mouth causes injury and may block the airway.
5. **Move dangerous objects away** — clear the area around the child of anything that could cause injury — sharp objects, hot items, hard surfaces.
6. **Time the seizure** — if possible, note what time it started. Tell the health worker how long it lasted.
7. **Watch the child's breathing** — after the seizure stops, make sure the airway is clear.
8. **Do not give water, food, or medicine by mouth during the seizure** — the child cannot swallow safely.
9. **After the seizure stops, go to hospital immediately** — even if the child wakes up and seems normal.

**Plain language:**
"During the seizure: lay your child on their side, move dangerous things away, and do not put anything in their mouth. Do not try to hold their limbs still. Once the shaking stops, go to hospital immediately."

## SAFETY NET
**Go to hospital immediately if any of these happen:**

- Your child has another seizure
- Your child cannot be woken after the seizure stops
- Your child is breathing with difficulty after the seizure
- Your child's lips or face look blue
- Your child's neck is stiff
- The seizure lasts more than 5 minutes without stopping
- Your child has a very high fever with repeated seizures
- You are frightened something is wrong — go. Do not wait.

## GENERAL SAFE ADVICE

**Why do children have convulsions?**
The most common cause in young children in Nigeria is fever (febrile convulsion). This happens when a fever rises quickly, causing the brain to react with a seizure. Febrile convulsions are frightening but are usually short (under 5 minutes) and stop on their own. However, they must always be assessed at hospital because:
- Malaria is a major cause of fever-related convulsions in Nigeria and needs immediate treatment
- Meningitis (infection of the brain) can also cause fever and seizures and is very dangerous
- A health worker must determine the cause before the child goes home

Other causes of convulsions in children include meningitis, low blood sugar, severe anaemia, and — less commonly — epilepsy.

**Febrile convulsions and long-term effects:**
Most simple febrile convulsions in otherwise healthy children do not cause brain damage. However, this does not mean the child should not be seen at hospital. The cause of the fever must be treated.

**Preventing febrile convulsions:**
Giving paracetamol early when a fever starts may help reduce the risk of a rapid temperature rise. However, if a child has already had a febrile convulsion, they should be assessed by a doctor who will advise on longer-term management.

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC CARE ADVICE

**Malaria and convulsions:**
Malaria is the most important cause of febrile convulsions in children in northern Nigeria. Cerebral malaria (malaria affecting the brain) is life-threatening and causes seizures. Any child with fever and a convulsion must be tested for malaria and treated if positive. Do not delay going to hospital.

**Meningitis:**
Meningitis (infection of the lining of the brain) is also common in northern Nigeria, especially during the dry season (harmattan). Signs include fever, stiff neck, sensitivity to light, and seizures. This is a medical emergency. Vaccines for meningitis (meningococcal vaccine) are available through the national immunisation programme — make sure your child is up to date.

**Traditional responses to convulsions:**
In many communities in northern Nigeria, a child having a seizure may be taken to a traditional healer or religious leader first. UMMA understands this is rooted in deep cultural belief. However, a child with a seizure needs emergency medical care immediately — delay can be fatal, especially if the cause is malaria or meningitis. Religious care and medical care are not mutually exclusive — please go to hospital first.

**Harmful practices to avoid during a seizure:**
- Do not pour cold water on the child
- Do not put the child over smoke or fire
- Do not give any oral preparations (herbs, water, medicine) during the seizure
- Do not restrain the child forcefully
- Do not place objects in the mouth

All of these practices are common but can cause harm — burns, choking, aspiration, or injury.

**Immunisation:**
Ensure your child has received all scheduled vaccines, including the meningococcal (Menveo/MenAfriVac) vaccine. Vaccines are available free at PHCs through Nigeria's National Programme on Immunisation.

**Nearest facility:**
Children with convulsions require a facility that can administer IV medication (diazepam or phenobarbitone) if the seizure does not stop. Most General Hospitals can do this. If the nearest PHC cannot manage an ongoing seizure, ask them to help you get to a General Hospital or teaching hospital quickly.
