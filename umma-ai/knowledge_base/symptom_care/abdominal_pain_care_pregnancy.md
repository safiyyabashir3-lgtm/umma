# Abdominal Pain — Symptom Care File (Pregnancy)
**UMMA AI | Maternal & Child Health | Kano/Northern Nigeria Context**
**Target: Pregnant women, all trimesters**

---

## CRITICAL NOTE FOR UMMA
Abdominal pain in pregnancy carries a different and higher risk profile than in non-pregnant individuals. Several causes of abdominal pain in pregnancy are obstetric emergencies with significant risk of maternal death. The threshold for escalation must always be low. UMMA must consider the trimester when reasoning about cause and urgency.

---

## ACTION TIERS

### EMERGENCY — Go to hospital immediately
**Triggered by:**
- Severe, sudden abdominal pain at any stage of pregnancy
- Pain with vaginal bleeding at any stage of pregnancy
- Constant severe lower abdominal pain with rigidity (hard abdomen) — possible placental abruption
- Severe pain in early pregnancy (first 12 weeks), especially on one side, with or without vaginal bleeding — possible ectopic pregnancy (life-threatening)
- Shoulder-tip pain (pain at the top of the shoulder) alongside abdominal pain in early pregnancy — sign of internal bleeding from ectopic pregnancy
- Signs of shock alongside pain: pale face, cold sweat, rapid weak pulse, confusion, near-fainting
- Pain accompanied by heavy vaginal bleeding at any gestation
- Regular, painful contractions before 37 weeks (possible preterm labour)
- Severe upper abdominal pain — especially in the right upper side or centre, in the second or third trimester — possible pre-eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, or liver problem
- Abdominal pain with severe headache and visual disturbance in the third trimester — possible severe pre-eclampsia
- Pain with fever above 38°C and rigors (shaking chills) — possible sepsis
- Complete inability to feel baby moving alongside abdominal pain after 28 weeks
- Trauma to the abdomen — fall, blow, road traffic accident

**Plain language:**
"This type of pain in pregnancy is a serious warning sign. Please go to the hospital immediately — not a chemist, not a patent medicine vendor. Do not take any medicine before you go. If you have pain on one side in early pregnancy and feel faint or dizzy, this could be a burst tube — this is life-threatening and you must go now. Ask someone to take you. If you cannot get transport, call out for help, go to the nearest health post, or find anyone who can help you reach the hospital."

---

### URGENT CLINIC — Go today, same day
**Triggered by:**
- Abdominal pain lasting more than 2 hours that is not improving
- Pain with burning or pain when passing urine (possible UTI or kidney infection, which is dangerous in pregnancy)
- Lower abdominal cramping in the first trimester without bleeding (possible threatened miscarriage — needs assessment)
- Pain with fever above 37.5°C (fever threshold is lower in pregnancy)
- Abdominal pain with vomiting that prevents keeping fluids down
- Persistent right-sided pain under the ribs (possible gallbladder or liver problem)
- Any new abdominal pain after 28 weeks that is different from previous Braxton Hicks
- Pain with reduced or absent fetal movement (after 28 weeks)
- Abdominal pain in a woman who has not confirmed the location of her pregnancy (rule out ectopic)

**Plain language:**
"This pain needs to be assessed by a nurse or doctor today. Please do not wait to see if it passes. Go to the nearest PHC, maternity clinic, or antenatal clinic. Bring your antenatal card with you. Tell them exactly where the pain is, how long it has lasted, and whether there is any bleeding or fever."

---

### HOME CARE — Only if ALL of these are true
**Conditions for home care:**
- Pain is mild and clearly associated with a benign cause (round ligament pain, gas/constipation, Braxton Hicks contractions)
- Pain is brief, intermittent, and not worsening
- No vaginal bleeding
- No fever
- Baby is moving normally (after 28 weeks)
- No headache, visual disturbance, or face/hand swelling
- Pain is not in the upper right abdomen
- Pregnancy is confirmed intrauterine (not suspected ectopic)
- Symptoms have been discussed with a nurse at a previous ANC visit and she identified the likely cause as benign

**Home care instructions:**

*Round ligament pain (sharp, brief pain on the sides of the lower abdomen, common in second trimester):*
1. Rest in the position that gives most relief — lying on the side with a pillow between the knees often helps
2. Change position slowly — avoid sudden movements that stretch the ligament
3. Warm (not hot) compress on the side where pain is felt
4. Paracetamol at the recommended adult dose if pain is moderate — this is the only safe pain medicine in pregnancy; do not use ibuprofen, aspirin, or diclofenac at any stage of pregnancy

*Gas and constipation:*
1. Increase fluid intake — drink more clean water
2. Eat more fruits and vegetables if available
3. Gentle walking can help move gas
4. Do not strain during bowel movements
5. Do not take laxatives without a nurse's advice

*Braxton Hicks contractions (irregular, non-painful tightenings, common after 20 weeks):*
1. Rest and drink water — dehydration worsens Braxton Hicks
2. Change position — walking or lying down can settle them
3. If they become regular, more painful, or more frequent — go to the clinic

**Plain language:**
"If your midwife has already told you this type of pain is normal for your pregnancy, you can rest and use paracetamol if needed. Drink plenty of water and rest on your side. But if the pain gets worse, does not go away, you see any bleeding, the baby stops moving, or any of the warning signs below appear — go to the hospital immediately."

---

### MONITOR — With clear safety net
**Conditions:**
- Known and previously assessed benign cause (e.g. constipation, gas, round ligament pain)
- Pain has just started and is very mild
- Baby is moving well (after 28 weeks)
- No bleeding, no fever, no headache

**What to watch:**
- Is the pain getting stronger or more frequent?
- Is the abdomen becoming rigid or board-like?
- Is any vaginal bleeding appearing?
- Is the baby moving less than normal?
- Are there any new symptoms — headache, visual changes, swelling, dizziness?
- Is the pain becoming constant rather than coming and going?

**Plain language:**
"Watch carefully over the next 1–2 hours. Drink water and rest on your side. Count the baby's movements if you are past 28 weeks. If the pain gets worse, you bleed, or the baby seems less active — do not wait, go to the hospital."

---

### UNCERTAIN — When UMMA cannot determine severity
**Plain language:**
"I am not sure from what you have described whether this abdominal pain is normal in pregnancy or a warning sign. Abdominal pain in pregnancy can sometimes be serious even when it feels mild at first. Please go to your antenatal clinic or nearest PHC today to be checked. Bring your antenatal card. Do not take any pain medicine other than paracetamol before you are seen."

## SAFETY NET
**Go to hospital immediately if any of these happen:**
- Pain becomes severe, sudden, or constant
- You see any vaginal bleeding
- Pain is on one side in early pregnancy and you feel faint or dizzy
- Your abdomen becomes hard and rigid
- You have a fever with shaking chills
- You develop a severe headache or cannot see clearly alongside pain
- You have pain at the top of your shoulder
- Your baby is not moving normally after 28 weeks
- You feel something is very wrong — trust this feeling and go

---

## GENERAL SAFE ADVICE
- Paracetamol is the only pain medicine that is considered safe to use in pregnancy — do not use ibuprofen, aspirin, diclofenac, or any NSAID at any stage of pregnancy; these can harm the baby and cause complications
- Ectopic pregnancy (pregnancy in the fallopian tube) is life-threatening and the symptoms — one-sided pain, early pregnancy, shoulder pain, fainting — can be mistaken for less serious problems; it must always be considered in early pregnancy with pain
- Urinary tract infections are common in pregnancy and can cause abdominal and back pain; they are more dangerous in pregnancy than outside it and must be treated with the correct antibiotic prescribed by a nurse
- All pregnant women should attend antenatal care regularly — a minimum of 8 ANC visits is recommended; problems found early are much more treatable
- Any abdominal trauma in pregnancy — even apparently minor — must be assessed at a hospital on the same day

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC CARE ADVICE
- **Herbal remedies in pregnancy:** The use of local herbs, roots, and preparations during pregnancy is common across Nigeria, including in Kano. Some of these are taken to "strengthen the womb," ease pain, or facilitate delivery. UMMA understands these practices are deeply rooted in tradition. However, many herbal preparations in pregnancy have caused miscarriage, preterm labour, liver damage, and maternal death. Please do not take any herbal preparation — by mouth, as steam, or applied to the abdomen — without discussing it with your doctor or nurse. This includes 'agbo,' local bitter leaf preparations, and bark teas.
- **Transport planning:** In Kano and surrounding areas, transport to hospital can be slow, expensive, or unavailable at night. Every pregnant woman should plan before an emergency: identify which hospital you will go to, how you will get there, who will go with you, and how you will pay for transport and care. The Kano State Emergency Obstetric Care facilities are available at AKTH and Murtala Muhammad Specialist Hospital.
- **ANC card:** Always carry your antenatal card with you. It contains information — your blood group, HIV status, blood pressure readings — that allows the hospital to treat you faster in an emergency.
- **Believing women's pain:** In some settings, women's pain in pregnancy is dismissed as "normal." UMMA takes your pain seriously. If a health worker dismisses your pain and you are still concerned — ask again, seek a second opinion, or go to a higher-level facility.
- **Distance from facility:** Women who live far from a health facility should plan to stay closer to the facility as their due date approaches. Ask your PHC nurse about available waiting homes (maternity waiting homes) near the hospital.
