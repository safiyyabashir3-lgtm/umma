# UMMA AI — Symptom Care File
# Symptom: BREATHING DIFFICULTY IN A CHILD (under 5 years)
# Context: Nigeria / Northern Nigeria (Kano)

---

## ACTION TIERS

### EMERGENCY — Go to hospital immediately
**Triggered by:**
- Child is breathing very fast and you can count the breaths easily (more than 60 per minute in infants, more than 50 in children 1–5)
- Child's chest is pulling inward between or below the ribs with every breath (chest indrawing)
- Nostrils are flaring (opening wide) with each breath
- Child is making a grunting or wheezing noise when breathing
- Child's lips, tongue, or fingernails look blue or grey
- Child is too breathless to cry, speak, feed, or swallow
- Child is very drowsy or difficult to wake
- Child has stopped breathing briefly or is gasping
- Breathing difficulty after choking, swallowing something, or inhaling smoke or fumes
- Any child under 2 months with breathing difficulty of any kind

**Plain language:**
"This is a medical emergency. Your child needs hospital care right now — do not wait. Call someone to help you get there. While travelling, keep your child upright or sitting forward slightly if they are older — do not lay them flat if they are struggling to breathe. Do not give food or drink if the child is in severe distress."

---

### URGENT CLINIC — Go today, same day
**Triggered by:**
- Child is breathing faster than normal but not extremely fast, with mild to moderate effort
- Child has had a cough with fast breathing for more than 2 days
- Child has a fever with breathing difficulty — could be pneumonia
- Child is feeding poorly and seems tired from the effort of breathing
- Child has a known history of asthma and current episode is not improving with usual treatment
- Wheezing or high-pitched noise when breathing in (stridor) that is new or worsening
- Any breathing problem in a child under 6 months

**Plain language:**
"Your child's breathing needs to be checked by a health worker today. Please go to your nearest clinic or Primary Health Centre now. Do not wait until tomorrow. Pneumonia can become very serious very quickly in young children."

---

### HOME CARE — Only if ALL of these are true
**Conditions for home care:**
- Child has a mild cold or blocked nose only — no fast breathing, no chest indrawing, no wheezing
- Child is feeding and drinking normally
- Child is alert and playful between episodes of coughing
- No fever, or mild fever only
- Child is over 6 months old
- Symptoms have been present less than 48 hours and are not getting worse

**Home care instructions:**
1. Keep the child's head slightly elevated — prop up the head of the sleeping surface slightly. For an infant, hold them more upright during feeds.
2. Saline (salt water) nose drops or a clean wet cloth can help clear a blocked nose before feeds. Do not use medicated nasal drops in young children.
3. Offer fluids often — breastmilk, water, or ORS. A congested child may feed in shorter bursts.
4. Keep the air in the room clean — do not cook with firewood or charcoal indoors near the child. Smoke worsens breathing problems.
5. If there is a mild fever, give paracetamol in the correct dose for weight.
6. Do not cover the child's face or wrap tightly.
7. Do not give cough syrups or cold medicines — they are not safe or helpful for young children.

**Plain language:**
"Your child has a blocked or runny nose but is breathing normally and feeding well. You can care for them at home. Keep the air clean and fresh, offer plenty of fluids, and check on them often. If their breathing changes, go to the clinic immediately."

---

### MONITOR — With clear safety net
**Conditions:**
- Mild upper respiratory symptoms only (runny nose, mild cough)
- Child is breathing normally at rest
- Feeding, drinking, and urinating normally
- Alert and responsive

**What to watch:**
- Breathing becoming faster or harder
- Chest pulling in with each breath
- Nostrils flaring
- Blue or grey colour around lips or fingernails
- Child too tired to feed or cry
- Cough worsening or lasting more than 3 days
- Fever developing or rising

**Plain language:**
"Watch your child's breathing carefully. Count their breaths for one minute when they are calm and resting — not while crying. If you notice anything from the safety net signs below, go to hospital immediately."

---

### UNCERTAIN — When UMMA cannot determine severity
**Plain language:**
"I cannot tell from what you have described if your child's breathing is safe or not. Breathing problems in young children can become serious very quickly. Please take your child to the nearest health facility today so a health worker can listen to their chest and assess them properly."

## SAFETY NET
**Go to hospital immediately if any of these happen:**

- Your child's chest pulls inward with every breath
- Your child's nostrils open wide with every breath
- Your child is breathing very fast — you can count many breaths just by watching the chest
- Your child's lips, tongue, or nails look blue or grey
- Your child is too breathless to cry, feed, or speak
- Your child makes a grunting sound with each breath
- Your child becomes very drowsy or hard to wake
- Your child stops breathing, even briefly
- Breathing difficulty comes on suddenly after the child was eating or playing with small objects

---

## GENERAL SAFE ADVICE

- **Count breaths** — the most important thing you can do is count how many times your child breathes in one minute when they are calm. In a baby under 2 months: more than 60 breaths per minute is fast. In a child 2–12 months: more than 50 is fast. In a child 1–5 years: more than 40 is fast. If breathing is fast, go to clinic.
- **Chest indrawing is a danger sign** — if the skin between or below the ribs sucks in with every breath, this is called chest indrawing and means the child is working very hard to breathe. Go to hospital immediately.
- **No cough syrups** — over-the-counter cough and cold syrups are not recommended for children under 5. They do not help and some contain ingredients that are harmful.
- **Pneumonia is common and serious** — many cases of fast breathing with fever in young children are pneumonia. It needs antibiotic treatment from a clinic — it cannot be treated at home.
- **Smoke is a major cause** — cooking smoke, cigarette smoke, incense, and burning rubbish are major causes of respiratory illness in children. Keep the child away from all sources of smoke.

---

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC CARE ADVICE

**Indoor cooking smoke:**
This is one of the most important causes of breathing problems in children in northern Nigeria. Cooking over firewood or charcoal inside the home fills the air with smoke particles that damage young lungs. If possible, cook outside or in a well-ventilated area, and keep the child away from the kitchen during cooking. Harmattan dust in the dry season also worsens breathing for vulnerable children.

**Patent medicine vendors (PMVs):**
PMVs may offer cough syrups or antihistamines for children with breathing difficulties. These are not appropriate treatment and can mask danger signs. If your child has fast breathing with fever, they need amoxicillin (an antibiotic) prescribed by a trained health worker — not cough medicine. Go to a PHC or clinic for assessment.

**Pneumonia and malaria together:**
In Nigeria, young children can have both pneumonia and malaria at the same time. A child with fever AND fast breathing should always be seen at a health facility — both conditions must be assessed and treated.

**Herbal remedies:**
Steaming with herbs or putting herbal preparations in the nose or mouth of a child with breathing difficulty can be dangerous. Steam burns are a real risk, and some herbal ingredients can worsen breathing or cause other harm. UMMA advises against this respectfully. If breathing is difficult, the safest action is to go to a health facility.

**Transport:**
If transport is difficult and your child has chest indrawing or very fast breathing, send for help immediately. A child with severe breathing difficulty should not be left alone. Ask a trusted person to accompany you and the child.

**Positioning:**
A child struggling to breathe is often more comfortable slightly upright. Hold a young baby against your chest or over your shoulder. Do not lay a struggling child flat on their back.

**Nearest PHC:**
Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI) training at PHCs includes assessment of breathing. Most PHCs in Kano State can assess and treat uncomplicated pneumonia with oral amoxicillin. Severe cases will be referred to secondary care.
