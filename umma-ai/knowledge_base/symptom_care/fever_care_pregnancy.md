# Fever — Symptom Care File (Pregnancy)
**UMMA AI | Maternal & Child Health | Kano/Northern Nigeria Context**
**Target: Pregnant women, all trimesters**

---

## CRITICAL NOTE FOR UMMA
Fever in pregnancy is never trivial. The threshold for concern is lower than in non-pregnant women — a temperature of 37.5°C or above in a pregnant woman requires assessment. Fever in pregnancy is associated with miscarriage, preterm labour, fetal distress, and maternal sepsis. Malaria is the most common cause of fever in pregnant Nigerian women and is particularly dangerous in the second and third trimester. UMMA must always assess for malaria, urinary tract infection, and signs of sepsis when a pregnant woman reports fever.

---

## ACTION TIERS

### EMERGENCY — Go to hospital immediately
**Triggered by:**
- Fever with shaking chills (rigors) — uncontrolled shaking with high temperature
- Fever with severe headache and stiff neck — possible meningitis
- Fever with confusion, agitation, or loss of consciousness
- Fever with seizure (convulsion)
- Fever with difficulty breathing or very fast breathing
- Fever with vaginal bleeding at any gestation
- Fever with severe abdominal pain
- Fever with reduced or absent fetal movements (after 28 weeks)
- Very high fever above 39.5°C that does not come down with paracetamol
- Fever with signs of shock — pale, cold, sweating, faint, rapid weak pulse
- Fever in a woman who is less than 6 weeks postpartum (puerperal sepsis is life-threatening)
- Fever with foul-smelling vaginal discharge (possible uterine infection)
- Fever in a woman who has recently had a miscarriage, termination, or any uterine procedure

**Plain language:**
"Fever in pregnancy with shaking, confusion, fits, or very fast breathing is a serious emergency. Malaria and womb infections can kill a pregnant woman and her baby quickly. Please go to the hospital immediately — not the chemist, not a patent medicine vendor. Go now. Take your antenatal card with you."

---

### URGENT CLINIC — Go today, same day
**Triggered by:**
- Any fever at or above 37.5°C in a pregnant woman — even if she feels only mildly unwell
- Fever with pain or burning when passing urine (UTI or kidney infection)
- Fever with back or loin pain (kidney infection)
- Fever that has lasted more than 24 hours
- Fever with vomiting that prevents keeping fluids down
- Fever in the first trimester — associated with risk of miscarriage and fetal harm
- Fever with rash
- Fever in a woman who has not taken malaria prevention medicine (IPTp) at ANC
- Fever in a woman returning from an area with known malaria transmission
- Any fever during or immediately after labour, or in the first 6 weeks postpartum

**Plain language:**
"Any fever in pregnancy needs to be checked by a nurse or doctor today — even if you feel it is mild. Malaria is the most common cause and it is dangerous for your baby even when you do not feel very sick. Go to the nearest PHC or clinic and ask for a malaria test. Take your antenatal card. Do not take any herbal or traditional fever remedy before going."

---

### HOME CARE — Only if ALL of these are true
**Conditions for home care:**
- Temperature is between 37.5°C and 38°C and woman feels only mildly unwell
- No rigors, no confusion, no headache, no stiff neck
- No abdominal pain, no vaginal bleeding, no foul discharge
- Baby is moving normally (after 28 weeks)
- No signs of dehydration
- She is able to keep fluids down
- She has already been assessed at clinic in the past 24 hours and sent home with a plan
- She can reach a clinic within 1–2 hours if symptoms worsen

**Home care instructions:**
1. Take paracetamol at the recommended adult dose — this is the ONLY safe fever medicine in pregnancy; take it every 4–6 hours as needed but do not exceed the maximum daily dose on the packet
2. Do NOT take aspirin, ibuprofen, diclofenac, or any NSAID — these are harmful to the baby at any stage of pregnancy and can cause serious complications including kidney problems and premature closure of a fetal blood vessel
3. Do NOT take quinine, chloroquine, or any malaria medicine at home without a prescription — dose and regimen must be directed by a nurse or doctor in pregnancy
4. Drink plenty of clean fluids — water, clean soups, or diluted juice; aim to drink more than usual to replace fluid lost through sweating
5. Remove heavy clothing and blankets — keep covered with a single light cloth
6. Rest in a cool, ventilated room; avoid direct sun
7. Sponge the face, neck, and arms with cool (not cold) clean water if temperature is high — this helps bring it down alongside paracetamol
8. Monitor temperature every 2–3 hours if a thermometer is available
9. Check fetal movements regularly — if baby is moving less than normal, go to hospital immediately
10. If fever does not reduce within 2 hours of taking paracetamol, or returns very quickly — go to the clinic immediately

**Plain language:**
"Take paracetamol for the fever — it is safe in pregnancy. Remove heavy clothing, drink lots of clean fluids, and rest. Use a cool damp cloth on your face and neck. But you must go to the clinic today or tomorrow for a malaria test even if the fever comes down. Fever in pregnancy always needs to be investigated. Watch the baby's movements."

### MONITOR — With clear safety net
**Conditions:**
- Woman has been seen at clinic today, malaria test was negative, no UTI found, and she was sent home to monitor
- Temperature was low (37.5°C–38°C) and came down with paracetamol
- Baby is moving normally
- No other symptoms present

**What to watch — every 2 hours:**
- Has the fever come down with paracetamol and stayed down?
- Has any shaking, chills, or rigors developed?
- Has a headache, stiff neck, or confusion developed?
- Has any vaginal bleeding or foul-smelling discharge appeared?
- Is the baby still moving normally?
- Is the fever returning quickly after paracetamol?
- Has any new symptom appeared — rash, difficulty breathing, severe pain?

**Plain language:**
"Watch yourself closely every few hours. Take paracetamol as needed. Drink fluids. Check the baby's movements. If the fever comes back quickly, gets worse, or any of the danger signs below appear — go to the hospital immediately, even at night."

### UNCERTAIN — When UMMA cannot determine severity
**Plain language:**
"I am not sure from what you have described whether this fever is mild or a warning sign of something serious. Any fever in pregnancy needs a malaria test and proper assessment. Please go to the nearest PHC or clinic today. Do not wait to see if the fever passes on its own. Take paracetamol for now — do not take any other medicine before being seen."

## SAFETY NET
**Go to hospital immediately if any of these happen:**
- You develop shaking chills or your body shakes uncontrollably
- Your fever rises above 39°C or does not come down with paracetamol
- You develop a severe headache or a stiff neck
- You feel confused, very agitated, or unlike yourself
- You have a seizure
- You have difficulty breathing
- You notice any vaginal bleeding or foul-smelling discharge
- Your baby is moving less than normal or has stopped moving
- You feel faint, very weak, or like you might collapse
- Your fever returns again and again despite paracetamol

## GENERAL SAFE ADVICE
- Paracetamol is the only safe fever medicine in pregnancy — it is effective and does not harm the baby when taken at the correct dose
- Aspirin and ibuprofen are dangerous in pregnancy — aspirin increases the risk of bleeding; ibuprofen and other NSAIDs can cause premature closure of the ductus arteriosus (a blood vessel in the baby's heart) and kidney problems; they must never be used
- Malaria in pregnancy causes severe anaemia, miscarriage, stillbirth, low birth weight, and maternal death — it is the most important cause of fever to identify and treat urgently in Nigerian pregnant women
- Intermittent preventive treatment for malaria (IPTp) with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine is given at ANC visits from 13 weeks — every pregnant woman should receive this; it does not treat active malaria but reduces the risk
- Fever in the first trimester above 38.9°C has been associated with neural tube defects and miscarriage — this is an additional reason to treat fever promptly and attend ANC early
- Typhoid, urinary tract infection, and respiratory infection are other common causes of fever in pregnancy — all require treatment with medicines appropriate for pregnancy

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC CARE ADVICE
- **Malaria and pregnancy:** Malaria is the leading infectious cause of maternal and perinatal mortality in Nigeria. Pregnant women are more susceptible to malaria, and the parasites concentrate in the placenta even when the mother does not feel severely ill. A woman with malaria may not feel very sick but her baby may be in danger. Every fever in pregnancy must be tested for malaria — rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) are available at most PHCs.
- **Self-treatment with antimalarials:** It is common in Nigeria for families to buy antimalarial medicines — chloroquine, artemether-lumefantrine (Coartem), or quinine — from PMVs without a prescription when someone has fever. In pregnancy, antimalarial regimens are specific to the trimester and must be prescribed correctly. Incorrect treatment can harm the baby. Please go to the PHC for a test and prescription rather than buying antimalarials at the chemist.
- **Herbal fever remedies:** The use of fever-reducing herbal preparations — including bitter leaf soup, neem bark tea, and various 'agbo' combinations — is common in northern Nigeria. While some traditional plants have mild antipyretic properties, the doses and preparations are not standardised, some contain hepatotoxic (liver-damaging) compounds, and none have been studied for safety in pregnancy. Please use paracetamol and go to the clinic.
- **Blanket wrapping:** In some communities, a feverish person is covered with heavy blankets to "sweat out" the fever. In pregnancy, this can cause the body temperature to rise further, which is harmful to the baby. Remove heavy coverings and allow the body to cool.
- **Postpartum fever:** Any fever in the 6 weeks after delivery must be assessed at hospital the same day — it can indicate a life-threatening uterine infection (puerperal sepsis). This is true even if the delivery went well and the woman feels "only a little warm."
- **ANC malaria prevention:** Every pregnant woman attending ANC should receive IPTp, insecticide-treated bed nets (ITNs), and counselling on malaria prevention. If you have not received these — ask for them at your next visit or go to the PHC to request them.
