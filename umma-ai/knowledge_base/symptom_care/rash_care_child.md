# rash_care_child.md
# UMMA AI — Symptom Care File
# Symptom: RASH IN A CHILD (under 5 years)
# Context: Nigeria / Northern Nigeria (Kano)

---

## ACTION TIERS

### EMERGENCY — Go to hospital immediately
**Triggered by:**
- Rash of small dark red, purple, or brown spots that do NOT fade or turn white when pressed firmly with a finger or clear glass (non-blanching purpuric rash — possible meningococcal disease)
- Rash with fever, stiff neck, or extreme sensitivity to light
- Rash with the child becoming very drowsy, limp, or difficult to wake
- Rash spreading very rapidly across the body within hours
- Rash with severe swelling of the lips, tongue, or face — or difficulty breathing or swallowing (possible severe allergic reaction / anaphylaxis)
- Rash with seizures
- Rash with bleeding from other parts of the body (nose, gums)
- Rash in a very unwell-looking child — pale, grey, or blue

**Plain language:**
"This rash could be a sign of a very serious infection. Go to hospital immediately. Do not wait. On the way, keep your child calm and as comfortable as possible."

---

### URGENT CLINIC — Go today, same day
**Triggered by:**
- Rash that blanches (turns white when pressed) with high fever — possible measles, rubella, or other viral illness
- Rash with fever lasting more than 2 days
- Rash spreading rapidly or covering large parts of the body
- Rash with swollen glands in the neck, armpits, or groin
- Rash that looks like blisters or open sores — especially around the mouth, palms, or soles (possible hand, foot and mouth disease or chickenpox)
- Rash with vomiting, diarrhoea, or refusing fluids
- Rash around the eyes, or eyes that are red and sticky with rash elsewhere
- Rash with swelling of the face, hands, or feet
- Child appears moderately unwell
- You are not sure what the rash is

**Plain language:**
"Your child's rash needs to be seen by a health worker today. Some rashes are signs of infections that need treatment. Please go to your nearest clinic or Primary Health Centre before evening."

---

### HOME CARE — Only if ALL of these are true
**Conditions for home care:**
- Child is well — alert, feeding, playing normally
- Rash is mild — small area, not spreading rapidly
- No fever, or very mild fever only
- Rash blanches when pressed (turns white, then returns to pink)
- No swelling of face, lips, or tongue
- No difficulty breathing
- Rash looks like heat rash, mild insect bites, or a known nappy rash

**Home care instructions for heat rash (miliaria):**
1. Move the child to a cooler, shaded, well-ventilated area.
2. Remove extra clothing layers.
3. Gently clean the skin with clean lukewarm water and pat dry.
4. Do not apply talcum powder, heavy creams, or oils to the rash — these block sweat glands and make heat rash worse.
5. Dress the child in loose, light cotton clothing.
6. Ensure adequate fluid intake — breastmilk or clean water.

**Home care for mild nappy rash:**
1. Change nappies or wrapping cloths frequently — keep the area dry.
2. Clean gently with clean water and soft cloth.
3. Allow nappy-free time in a safe, clean space to let the skin air.
4. Apply a thin layer of petroleum jelly (Vaseline) or zinc cream if available.
5. Do not use powder or perfumed products.

**Home care for mild insect bites:**
1. Clean the area with clean water.
2. Apply a cold clean cloth to reduce swelling.
3. Do not scratch — keep the child's nails short to prevent scratching and infection.
4. Watch for spreading redness, warmth, or pus — this means infection and needs clinic assessment.

**Plain language:**
"Your child's rash looks mild and is likely related to heat or a minor irritation. Keep the skin clean and cool, dress them lightly, and watch the rash closely. If it spreads, worsens, or the child develops fever, go to the clinic."

---

### MONITOR — With clear safety net
**Conditions:**
- Known heat rash improving with cooling
- Mild insect bites without signs of infection
- Known nappy rash, improving with skin care
- Child is well, no fever, feeding normally

**What to watch:**
- Rash spreading or changing in appearance
- New spots appearing, especially dark non-blanching spots
- Fever developing
- Child becoming unwell — drowsy, refusing feeds, crying inconsolably
- Swelling of face, lips, or tongue
- Difficulty breathing

**Plain language:**
"Watch the rash and your child closely. Check the rash every few hours. Press gently on a spot with your finger — if it turns white and then comes back, that is reassuring. If spots do NOT turn white when pressed, or if your child develops fever and seems unwell, go to hospital immediately."

---

### UNCERTAIN — When UMMA cannot determine severity
**Plain language:**
"I cannot tell from your description what kind of rash this is or how serious it might be. Rashes can be caused by many different things — some are harmless, others are signs of serious infections. Please take your child to a health worker today so they can examine the rash properly."

## SAFETY NET
**Go to hospital immediately if any of these happen:**

- Spots appear that do NOT turn white when you press on them firmly — dark red, purple, or brown pinprick spots
- The rash spreads very quickly across the child's body
- Your child's face, lips, or tongue swell
- Your child has difficulty breathing or swallowing
- Your child develops a stiff neck with the rash
- Your child becomes very drowsy or cannot be woken
- Your child develops bleeding from the nose, gums, or other areas
- Your child has a seizure
- The rash starts to blister, weep, or become infected (warm, red, swollen, pus)

---

## GENERAL SAFE ADVICE

**The glass test:**
Press a clear glass or your clean finger firmly against the rash. If the spots disappear or turn pale under pressure, the rash is "blanching" — this is usually less concerning. If the spots stay the same colour and do not disappear under pressure (non-blanching), this is a danger sign — go to hospital immediately.

**Measles:**
Measles is still common in parts of northern Nigeria and causes a widespread blotchy rash that starts on the face and spreads downward, along with fever, red eyes, and runny nose. Measles is highly contagious and can be severe, especially in malnourished children. It is preventable by vaccination. If measles is suspected, the child needs clinic assessment — complications include pneumonia, ear infections, and blindness.

**Chickenpox:**
Chickenpox causes itchy blisters starting on the chest and spreading. It is common and usually mild, but can be severe in young babies and children with malnutrition. Keep the child's nails short to prevent scratching and skin infection. Do not give aspirin for the fever — give paracetamol only.

**Meningococcal disease:**
This is a serious bacterial infection common in the "meningitis belt" of sub-Saharan Africa, which includes northern Nigeria. It can cause fever, stiff neck, and a characteristic rash of dark non-blanching spots. It kills very quickly. The glass test is essential — any non-blanching rash in a sick child is an emergency.

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC CARE ADVICE

**Heat rash:**
Prickly heat (heat rash) is extremely common in infants and young children in northern Nigeria due to the hot climate. It appears as tiny red bumps or blisters, usually on the neck, chest, and skin folds. It is not dangerous but can be uncomfortable. The most important treatment is keeping the child cool, clean, and lightly dressed. Do not apply thick creams or oils.

**Harmattan dust:**
Harmattan (dry season) causes dry, irritated skin in many children, which can look like a rash. Mild moisturising with a gentle oil or lotion can help. Ensure the child is drinking enough fluids.

**Meningitis vaccination:**
Meningococcal vaccine (MenAfriVac) is given free through Nigeria's national immunisation programme. Ensure your child has received all their scheduled vaccines. This is especially important in northern Nigeria, which lies in the meningitis belt.

**Measles vaccination:**
Measles vaccine is given at 9 months through the national immunisation schedule. A second dose is recommended. If you are not sure whether your child has been vaccinated, visit your nearest PHC.

**Herbal remedies for rash:**
Some families apply herbal pastes, leaves, or oils to skin rashes in children. Some of these can cause allergic reactions, skin irritation, or worsen the rash. UMMA respectfully advises against applying any untested preparation to a child's skin, especially if the rash is associated with fever or looks serious. Tell the health worker at the clinic if you have applied anything to the rash.

**Patent medicine vendors (PMVs):**
PMVs may offer antihistamines or steroid creams for rashes. Topical steroids should not be used on young children's skin without medical advice. Antihistamines may be appropriate for itching in some cases but are not appropriate for serious rashes associated with fever. When in doubt, go to the PHC for assessment.

**Nearest PHC:**
PHC workers can assess rashes and identify danger signs. Measles, chickenpox, and meningococcal disease are all notifiable conditions — health workers are trained to recognise and report them. Bring your child's immunisation card to the visit.
