# DISEASE FILE: INTESTINAL WORMS / SOIL-TRANSMITTED HELMINTHS (CHILD)
**Category:** Child (0–5 years)
**Subcategory:** Parasitic / Nutrition
**Version:** 1.0

---

## OVERVIEW

Intestinal worm infections (soil-transmitted helminths, STH) are among the most prevalent infections in children in Kano and northwest Nigeria. The major species are:
- **Ascaris lumbricoides** (roundworm): most common, large worm, causes respiratory and GI disease
- **Trichuris trichiura** (whipworm): causes bloody diarrhoea and rectal prolapse in heavy infections
- **Hookworm** (Ancylostoma duodenale, Necator americanus): causes iron deficiency anaemia
- **Enterobius vermicularis** (pinworm/threadworm): causes perianal itching, predominantly a household and nuisance infection
- **Strongyloides stercoralis**: less commonly recognised but important in immunocompromised children

STH are transmitted via faecal-oral route (Ascaris, Trichuris, Enterobius) and via skin penetration of larvae in contaminated soil (hookworm, Strongyloides). In Kano, open defaecation, playing in contaminated soil, consumption of unwashed produce, and poor handwashing create near-universal transmission conditions.

**Epidemiology in Kano/northwest Nigeria:**
- STH prevalence in school-age children in northern Nigeria exceeds 50% in some studies; in under-5s, Ascaris and Trichuris predominate
- Heavy worm burdens (polyparasitism — multiple species) are common and cause more severe disease than single infections
- Worm infections directly contribute to malnutrition, anaemia, stunting, and impaired cognitive development — major public health consequences in this population
- Mass drug administration (MDA) programmes with albendazole exist in Nigeria but coverage in Kano has been inconsistent; many children miss scheduled deworming
- Seasonal: transmission is highest in the rainy season when soil contamination is greatest

**Why it matters for UMMA:**
Intestinal worms are so prevalent they may not seem urgent to mothers. However, heavy infections cause significant harm — malnutrition, anaemia, intestinal obstruction (Ascaris), rectal prolapse (Trichuris), and growth failure. UMMA can play a role in recognising symptomatic infections, advising appropriate treatment (albendazole/mebendazole), and promoting prevention through hygiene counselling. UMMA must also recognise the rare but life-threatening complications of heavy Ascaris infection (obstruction, biliary/liver migration).

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
- Mild or intermittent abdominal discomfort — crampy, periumbilical, not constant
- Slightly reduced appetite
- Child appears well and active
- Occasional passage of worms in stool — mother may bring worm sample, often the most alarming and diagnostic feature for her
- Perianal itching especially at night (Enterobius): child scratches bottom, restless at night, disturbed sleep
- Mild loose stools or intermittent diarrhoea
- Mother may describe child as "pot-bellied" (abdominal distension from worm burden and associated malnutrition/gas)
- Mild pallor in hookworm but not obvious anaemia

### Moderate Presentation
- Significant abdominal pain and distension
- Diarrhoea — may be watery or mucousy (Trichuris)
- Reduced appetite with visible weight loss or failure to gain weight
- Visible pallor — moderate anaemia from hookworm infection
- Fatigue — child less active, tires quickly
- Growth faltering: child appears smaller than expected for age
- Passage of multiple visible worms in stool (Ascaris) or visible larvae/worms at anus (Enterobius)
- Rectal mucosa prolapse visible at anus after defaecation — especially Trichuris-associated
- Nutritional oedema in setting of severe worm burden combined with protein-energy malnutrition
- Cough during Ascaris larval migration through lungs (Löffler syndrome — acute phase): dry cough, wheeze, mild fever 2–4 weeks after heavy Ascaris infection; blood eosinophilia if tested

### Severe Presentation (Complications — requires urgent referral)
- **Ascaris intestinal obstruction**: sudden onset severe colicky abdominal pain, vomiting (may vomit worms), palpable mass of worms in right iliac fossa or across abdomen, inability to pass stool or gas, high-pitched bowel sounds — surgical emergency
- **Biliary ascariasis**: worm migration into bile duct — right upper quadrant pain, jaundice, fever, vomiting — may mimic cholecystitis
- **Ascaris hepatic abscess or lung abscess**: rare but serious complication of larval or adult migration
- **Severe Trichuris dysentery syndrome**: profuse bloody mucous diarrhoea, tenesmus, rectal prolapse with irreducible prolapse, severe anaemia — children appear toxic and very ill
- **Severe anaemia (Hb < 7 g/dL)** from hookworm: pallor, cardiac failure features, tachycardia, oedema, feeding difficulty in young children — medical emergency requiring blood transfusion
- **Strongyloides hyperinfection** in immunocompromised child (HIV, steroid use, severe malnutrition): diarrhoea, respiratory failure, bacteraemia from larvae carrying gut bacteria through bowel wall — life-threatening

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Intestinal worms vs Gastroenteritis:**
- Gastroenteritis: acute onset, often with vomiting, profuse watery diarrhoea, fever; worm infections are chronic/subacute with intermittent symptoms
- Worm infection: longer duration (weeks to months), milder daily symptoms, visible worm passage is diagnostic
- Ascaris obstruction can mimic severe gastroenteritis — important distinction: abdominal mass, visible worms in vomit/stool

**Intestinal worms vs Malnutrition:**
- They coexist and cause each other — worm infection causes malnutrition (malabsorption, anorexia); malnutrition reduces immunity to worms
- Both cause abdominal distension and growth failure
- A child with moderate malnutrition should always have STH as a consideration — deworming is part of nutritional rehabilitation

**Intestinal worms vs Anaemia from other causes:**
- Hookworm anaemia: iron deficiency pattern, history of soil contact/playing in mud, positive stool O&P
- Sickle cell anaemia: family history, episodic crises, haemolytic anaemia pattern, splenomegaly
- Malaria anaemia: febrile episodes, positive RDT, splenomegaly
- These often coexist in Nigerian children — multiple causes of anaemia common

**Intestinal worms vs Appendicitis:**
- Ascaris migration into appendix can cause appendicitis — clinically indistinguishable from conventional appendicitis; diagnosis often made at surgery

**Perianal itching — pinworm vs other causes:**
- Pinworm (Enterobius): nocturnal, worse at night, visible white worms near anus at night, affects multiple household members, no fever, no systemic illness
- Threadworm vs vulvovaginitis in girls: Enterobius can enter vagina and cause vaginal discharge and dysuria — can be confused with UTI or sexual abuse (important safeguarding consideration)
- Perianal itching also from skin conditions: eczema, contact dermatitis, fungal infection

**Common coexistences:**
- Worms + malnutrition (universal in heavily burdened populations)
- Worms + malaria anaemia (both cause anaemia — additive)
- Worms + typhoid (worm burden facilitates Salmonella translocation across gut wall)
- Worms + gastroenteritis (worm burden increases diarrhoeal frequency and duration)
- Worms + UTI in girls (Enterobius carries perianal bacteria into urethra)

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Visible worms in stool or around anus as only symptom
- Mild abdominal discomfort only
- Good appetite and normal growth
- No anaemia visible
- No systemic symptoms
- Child otherwise well
- Perianal itching without systemic features

### Moderate criteria
- Recurrent abdominal pain affecting daily activity
- Reduced appetite with growth faltering or visible weight loss
- Mild-to-moderate pallor (moderate anaemia)
- Visible abdominal distension disproportionate to age
- Loose stools or mild diarrhoea (not watery or profuse) persisting for more than 2 weeks
- Rectal mucosa visible but reducible after defaecation (partial prolapse)
- Growth faltering — weight below expected, or no weight gain over 4 weeks in under-2
- Cough with wheeze in setting of known or possible worm infection (Löffler syndrome — Ascaris larval migration)

### Severe criteria — ANY ONE = severe/emergency
- Intestinal obstruction features: severe colicky pain, vomiting of worms, palpable abdominal mass, obstipation
- Irreducible rectal prolapse
- Jaundice with abdominal pain (biliary ascariasis)
- Severe anaemia: Hb < 7 g/dL (estimated clinically: very pale conjunctivae/palms, tachycardia, cardiac failure signs)
- Strongyloides hyperinfection: diarrhoea with respiratory distress in immunocompromised child
- Signs of peritonitis (worm-related perforation)
- Child with SAM + worm infection — needs integrated nutritional and deworming management

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild — UMMA advice
**Clinical approach:** Single anthelmintic dose (albendazole or mebendazole) is curative for most mild STH infections. Can be initiated at PHC level. Hygiene counselling is essential to prevent rapid reinfection.

- Child needs deworming medicine — albendazole (single dose) or mebendazole — available at PHC or can be purchased at pharmacies
- Take the child to the PHC for proper deworming — the health worker will give the right dose based on the child's weight
- All household members under 15 years should be dewormed at the same time — worms spread within families
- Treat 6 months later and then every 6 months if worm reinfection is likely (most children in this environment)
- For Enterobius: all household contacts must be treated simultaneously or the child will be reinfected within weeks
- Hygiene: wash hands before eating and after toilet use; wash fruits and vegetables; encourage child not to put hands in mouth or eat soil
- Avoid open defaecation near play areas
- Shoes: wearing sandals reduces hookworm transmission significantly
- Ensure iron-rich foods in diet: beans, green leaves, eggs, meat where available

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child has worms — this is very common in young children and can be treated easily. Take your child to the clinic for deworming medicine. Also deworm all other children in the house at the same time or the worms will come back. The medicine works best when everyone is treated together. After treatment, wash hands carefully before eating and after using the toilet. Worms come from dirty soil and contaminated food — good handwashing and washing fruits helps prevent them coming back."

---

### Moderate — UMMA advice
**Clinical approach:** Child needs PHC or clinic assessment for deworming, anaemia evaluation, nutritional assessment, and follow-up. If anaemia is confirmed, iron supplementation is needed alongside deworming. Nutritional rehabilitation if growth faltering.

- Child needs to be seen at a clinic — not just given deworming medicine. There may be anaemia or malnutrition that also needs treatment.
- At clinic: the health worker will assess for anaemia (check eyes and tongue), measure weight and height, and examine the abdomen.
- Deworming medicine will be given. Iron supplementation may be prescribed if anaemia is found.
- Nutritional counselling and support — the clinic may enrol the child in a nutritional programme if weight is low.
- If the child is also malnourished: the combination of worms and malnutrition needs integrated management — deworming alone will not be sufficient.
- Follow up in 4–6 weeks to confirm weight gain and improvement.
- Repeat deworming in 6 months.

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child has worms and may also have anaemia from the infection. This needs to be checked at a clinic so the health worker can examine your child properly, check for anaemia, and measure their growth. The clinic will give deworming medicine and may add iron medicine if your child's blood is low. Please do not just buy deworming medicine from the chemist without getting your child properly checked — there may be more going on."

---

### Severe — UMMA advice
**Clinical approach:** Suspected intestinal obstruction, biliary ascariasis, irreducible rectal prolapse, or severe anaemia are emergencies requiring immediate hospital referral.

- This is a medical emergency and the child must go to a hospital with surgical capability immediately.
- Intestinal obstruction from worms can be fatal if not treated urgently — do not give deworming medicine at home when obstruction is suspected (this can worsen the obstruction by stimulating worm movement)
- Severe anaemia may require a blood transfusion — this can only be done at a hospital
- In Kano: AKTH or the nearest general hospital with surgical and paediatric services
- Do not attempt home treatment. Go now.

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child is very seriously ill and needs to go to a hospital right now. The worm infection has caused a serious problem inside the body that needs emergency treatment. Do not give any medicines at home. Go to [AKTH / general hospital] immediately."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous local practices:**
- Herbal deworming preparations: widely used — various plant-based preparations including pawpaw seeds, bitter leaf, mango bark extracts, and garbo preparations. Some have mild anthelmintic activity but dosage is uncontrolled, and others are hepatotoxic or cause severe vomiting and diarrhoea. Reliance on these delays access to effective treatment.
- Overuse of deworming without hygiene change: many families deworm every 3 months but do not change hygiene practices — reinfection is immediate. UMMA must pair deworming advice with hygiene counselling.
- Giving piperazine to a child with intestinal obstruction: piperazine paralyses worms but the bolus remains obstructing — can worsen obstruction. Standard deworming drugs should NOT be given if obstruction is suspected.
- Dose guessing: mothers buying albendazole from PMVs without knowing the correct dose for the child's age/weight may under- or over-dose.
- Ignoring household contacts: treating only the symptomatic child without treating siblings leads to rapid reinfection (especially Enterobius which spreads through shared bedding, toilet, and handshaking).

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Stool microscopy (ova and parasite examination — O&P) for definitive diagnosis is available at general hospital and AKTH labs but rarely done at PHC; clinical diagnosis and empirical deworming are the norm
- Albendazole and mebendazole are on the essential medicines list and are low cost but stock-outs occur at PHC; PMV supply is generally available
- Nutritional rehabilitation centres (NRC) for children with SAM and worm burden are present in Kano but capacity is limited and waiting times exist
- Blood transfusion for severe hookworm anaemia requires grouping and cross-matching — only reliably available at general hospital level and above

**Cultural factors:**
- Geophagia (eating soil): common in young Nigerian children and in pregnant women — dramatically increases Ascaris and Toxocara exposure. UMMA should ask about soil eating in a non-judgemental way and counsel clearly against it.
- "Pot belly is normal in children": cultural acceptance of abdominal distension as normal in young children delays recognition of significant worm burden as a pathological condition
- Food taboos during deworming: some families restrict food during deworming treatment unnecessarily — this reduces medication absorption (albendazole is better absorbed with fatty food) and weakens the child
- Reluctance to discuss defaecation openly: limits history-taking about stool consistency, frequency, and passage of worms — UMMA should use normalising language
- Household decision-making: deworming the whole family requires cooperation from the head of household — UMMA should explain the importance of treating everyone

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Children under 2 with heavy Ascaris burden: highest risk of intestinal obstruction and larval migration complications
- Malnourished children (SAM/MAM): immunocompromised, worse outcomes from both worm infection and treatment
- HIV-positive children: risk of Strongyloides hyperinfection with any systemic illness or steroid use
- Children with geophagia: highest exposure
- Children without latrines or toilet access (open defaecation households): highest transmission risk
- Girls with pinworm and recurrent UTI: Enterobius is an underrecognised cause of recurrent UTI in girls — treat household simultaneously
