# CONJUNCTIVITIS — CHILD (Under 5)

## OVERVIEW
Conjunctivitis (red eye / eye infection) is inflammation of the conjunctiva caused by bacterial, viral, or allergic triggers. In Kano and northwest Nigeria, bacterial conjunctivitis is the dominant form in young children, driven by poor handwashing, shared towels, dust, overcrowding, and fly contact. Neonatal conjunctivitis (ophthalmia neonatorum) is a separate high-risk entity occurring in the first 28 days of life, often caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae or Chlamydia trachomatis acquired during delivery and requires urgent escalation.

Conjunctivitis is extremely common in children under 5 in northern Nigeria, particularly during the harmattan season (November–March) when dust, dry air, and vitamin A deficiency create conditions for frequent eye infections. During dry season, epidemic bacterial conjunctivitis ("Apollo") spreads rapidly in households and schools.

Trachoma — caused by Chlamydia trachomatis — is endemic in northwest Nigeria and must always be considered in children with recurrent or chronic conjunctivitis. Repeated trachoma infections in childhood lead to adult blindness and are a major public health concern in this region.

Why it matters for UMMA: Mothers frequently manage eye infections at home with breast milk, kohl (tiro/kwalli), or traditional eye drops — practices that can worsen or mask serious infections. Corneal ulceration from untreated bacterial conjunctivitis or mismanaged trachoma can cause permanent vision loss. UMMA must identify red-flag features early and distinguish simple conjunctivitis from gonococcal ophthalmia, corneal ulceration, and periorbital cellulitis.

Common delays and barriers:
- Use of tiro (kohl) containing lead compounds applied to eye margins — routine practice that introduces pathogens and toxins
- Breast milk instillation into infected eyes — widely practiced, occasionally delays care-seeking
- Patent medicine vendors selling chloramphenicol eye drops without assessment — appropriate drug but wrong diagnosis common
- Transport to ophthalmology available only at tertiary level (AKTH, Murtala Muhammad Specialist Hospital) — most PHCs lack eye assessment capacity
- Mothers may not recognise vision threat in young infants who cannot verbalise visual symptoms

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
- Unilateral or bilateral conjunctival redness (injection)
- Watery or mildly mucoid discharge
- Sticky eyelids in the morning ("eyelids glued shut" on waking) — common presenting complaint from mothers
- No pain, no photophobia, no vision change
- Child is playful, feeding normally
- In allergic conjunctivitis: bilateral itch, watery discharge, seasonal pattern, no fever — often associated with dust and harmattan exposure
- Mild chemosis (conjunctival swelling) may be present

### Moderate Presentation
- Bilateral mucopurulent (thick yellow-green) discharge
- Significant lid swelling, crusting
- Conjunctival injection prominent, visible from a distance
- Child rubbing eyes frequently
- Preauricular lymph node tender (suggests viral or chlamydial cause)
- May have concurrent upper respiratory symptoms (viral conjunctivitis)
- In trachoma: follicles on upper tarsal conjunctiva, papillary hypertrophy — requires eversion of upper lid to see
- Mild periorbital oedema (lid swelling only, not beyond orbital rim)
- Child may be irritable but feeding and alert

### Severe Presentation
- Profuse purulent discharge — "pus pouring from eye" — especially in neonates (ophthalmia neonatorum): EMERGENCY — assume gonococcal until proven otherwise
- Severe lid oedema with chemosis — eye cannot be opened by child
- Corneal haziness, opacity, or ulceration visible — white/grey area on normally clear cornea: VISION EMERGENCY
- Photophobia, child turning away from light, covering eyes
- Periorbital cellulitis: erythema, warmth, and induration extending BEYOND the eyelids onto the cheek or brow — indicates spread of infection to orbital soft tissue
- Proptosis (eye appearing pushed forward)
- Restricted eye movement (suggests orbital involvement)
- Fever with systemic upset
- Any conjunctivitis in a neonate (0–28 days) is severe by default

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Bacterial conjunctivitis vs viral conjunctivitis:**
- Bacterial: mucopurulent thick discharge, often unilateral initially, no preauricular node, responds to antibiotics
- Viral: watery discharge, preauricular lymphadenopathy, often follows URTI, bilateral from start, self-limiting

**Conjunctivitis vs periorbital/orbital cellulitis:**
- Conjunctivitis: redness confined to eye, no skin erythema beyond lids, no proptosis, eye movement normal
- Periorbital cellulitis: skin redness extends to cheek/brow, warm and tender skin, systemic fever — REQUIRES ADMISSION

**Conjunctivitis vs corneal ulcer:**
- Corneal ulcer: white/grey opacity on cornea, severe pain, photophobia, vision reduced — these features are ABSENT in simple conjunctivitis
- Corneal ulcer is a vision emergency

**Ophthalmia neonatorum vs simple sticky eye in newborn:**
- Simple sticky eye (lacrimal duct obstruction): mucoid but NOT purulent, appears after day 3, no conjunctival injection, responds to massage
- Ophthalmia neonatorum: purulent, conjunctival injection present, within first 28 days — especially days 1–5 (chemical), days 2–5 (gonococcal), days 5–14 (chlamydial)

**Trachoma vs bacterial conjunctivitis:**
- Trachoma: follicles on UPPER tarsal conjunctiva (not visible without lid eversion), chronic/recurrent course, scarring on upper lid margin in older children, endemic area
- Bacterial: acute onset, mucopurulent discharge, follicles NOT dominant feature

**Conjunctivitis vs measles-associated conjunctivitis:**
- Measles eye: conjunctivitis is part of prodrome — always check for Koplik's spots, rash, fever pattern, vaccination status

**Allergic conjunctivitis vs bacterial:**
- Allergic: BILATERAL, itching dominant symptom, watery not purulent discharge, no fever, seasonal (harmattan), no morning crusting as prominent feature

Common coexistences in Nigerian context:
- Conjunctivitis + URTI (viral)
- Conjunctivitis + otitis media (same pathogen spread)
- Trachoma + vitamin A deficiency (dramatically worsens corneal risk)
- Neonatal conjunctivitis + neonatal sepsis (same organism, same route)
- Conjunctivitis during measles prodrome

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Conjunctival redness with watery or minimal mucoid discharge
- Eyelids openable by child
- No corneal involvement
- No periorbital skin changes
- Child over 28 days old
- Feeding and activity normal
- No fever

### Moderate criteria
- Mucopurulent discharge with significant crusting
- Lid oedema confined to eyelids only
- Child irritable, rubbing eyes
- Concurrent systemic symptoms (fever, URTI) present
- Possible trachoma features
- No corneal haziness
- Child over 28 days old

### Severe criteria — any ONE = severe/emergency
- Any conjunctivitis in a neonate (age 0–28 days)
- Profuse purulent discharge (suspected gonococcal)
- Corneal haziness, opacity, or ulceration
- Periorbital cellulitis (skin redness/swelling beyond eyelid margin)
- Proptosis or restricted eye movement
- Photophobia with pain
- Child unable to open eye due to swelling
- Associated high fever with systemic deterioration

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- Clean eyes gently with clean cotton wool or cloth soaked in clean cooled boiled water, wiping from inner corner outward, using a fresh piece for each wipe
- Wash hands thoroughly before and after touching child's eyes
- Do not share towels or face cloths with the child
- Stop use of tiro/kohl around or in the eye immediately
- Do not instill breast milk into the eye
- Allergic conjunctivitis: reduce dust exposure, avoid harmattan winds on child's face
- Most mild bacterial conjunctivitis resolves with hygiene alone in 5–7 days
- If not improving in 2–3 days or worsening, visit PHC for assessment — clinician may prescribe antibiotic eye drops
- Return immediately if discharge becomes thick/purulent, eye swells shut, or any white spot appears on eye

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child's eye looks mildly infected. The most important thing is to keep the eye clean. Use a clean piece of cotton wool dipped in cooled boiled water to wipe the eye from the inside corner to the outside, and use a fresh piece each time. Wash your hands before and after touching the eye. Please stop putting tiro or breast milk in the eye — these can make the infection worse. Most mild eye infections get better on their own in about a week with good cleaning. If the eye is not improving after 2–3 days, or if pus starts coming out, or if the eye swells shut, or if you see any white cloudiness on the eye, please go to the clinic right away."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Visit PHC or clinic today — child needs eye examination and likely antibiotic eye drops
- Continue eye cleaning as above during journey to clinic
- Do not apply tiro or any traditional eye preparations
- If trachoma is suspected (recurrent infections, community with known trachoma), advise provider awareness — family members may also need assessment
- If fever present, child needs full assessment at clinic
- Do not patch or cover the infected eye — increases bacterial growth

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child's eye infection looks moderate — the discharge is thick and the eye is quite swollen. You should take your child to the clinic today so a health worker can check the eye properly and give the right treatment. Keep cleaning the eye gently with clean water on the way. Please do not put tiro or any traditional drops in the eye before the clinic visit — this can make it harder for the health worker to see what is happening. If your child also has a fever, mention this at the clinic."

### Severe
UMMA should advise — EMERGENCY RESPONSE:
- Go to the nearest hospital with emergency capacity NOW — do not wait
- Neonatal conjunctivitis (baby under 28 days): this is an emergency — go immediately to a hospital, not a PHC — baby may need IV antibiotics and urgent eye washout
- Corneal haziness or white spot on eye: go to a hospital with ophthalmology — vision is at risk
- Periorbital cellulitis: child needs IV antibiotics in hospital — do not treat at home or at patent medicine vendor
- While travelling: keep eye clean, do not cover with patch, do not apply anything to the eye
- If child has known vitamin A deficiency or has had measles recently — tell the doctor immediately, corneal risk is very high

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"This is a serious eye emergency. Your child needs to go to hospital right now — not a patent medicine shop, not a PHC — a proper hospital. [For neonate:] A new baby with a very red eye and pus coming out can go blind very quickly if not treated fast — this is an emergency. [For corneal involvement:] The white spot you can see on your child's eye means the eye itself is in danger. If this is not treated urgently, your child could lose sight in that eye. Please go to the hospital now and tell them the child's eye has a white spot on it. Do not put anything in the eye until the doctor has seen it."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Tiro (kohl/kwalli): widely applied to infant and child eyes for cultural/religious reasons and believed to strengthen eyesight. Contains lead compounds, introduced by contaminated finger, introduces pathogens directly. Associated with neonatal lead poisoning and worsening of eye infections. UMMA must address this sensitively but clearly.
- Breast milk in the eye: common practice, not sterile, associated with introducing normal oral flora into already infected eye
- Urine in the eye: occasionally practiced, introduces bacterial contamination
- Patent medicine vendor chloramphenicol eye drops: sometimes appropriate but used for all red eyes including gonococcal neonatal conjunctivitis (needs IV ceftriaxone, not drops alone) and corneal ulcers (inadequate)
- Delay seeking care because "eye infections always clear on their own"

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Ophthalmology specialist only at tertiary hospitals (AKTH Kano, Murtala Muhammad Specialist Hospital) — significant transport barrier from rural LGAs
- PHC staff rarely trained to evert eyelids for trachoma assessment
- Corneal ulcer requires specialist slit-lamp examination — unavailable at PHC
- Neonatal eye swabs for culture rarely available outside tertiary level — empirical treatment is the norm

**Cultural factors:**
- Tiro application is deeply embedded in Hausa/Fulani practice for newborns and young children — mothers may feel judged when advised against it. UMMA should frame this as "something that can harm the eye when already infected" rather than attacking the practice outright
- Eye problems may be attributed to "evil eye" (ido) — families may seek spiritual care first, delaying medical attention
- Female children may receive less urgent care-seeking compared to male children

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Neonates (ophthalmia neonatorum risk — gonococcal and chlamydial)
- Children with vitamin A deficiency — corneal ulceration risk dramatically increased with any eye infection or measles
- Children recently recovered from measles — corneal scarring risk
- Children in households with active trachoma cases
- Children in crowded compounds with shared water/towels
- Children with sickle cell disease — increased infection susceptibility
