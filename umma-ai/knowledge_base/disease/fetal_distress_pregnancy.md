# UMMA Disease File — Fetal Distress / Reduced Fetal Movements / Fetal Compromise
# Category: Pregnancy illness | Subcategory: Fetal wellbeing — EMERGENCY
# Last reviewed: 2025

---

## OVERVIEW

Fetal distress refers to signs indicating that the fetus is not receiving adequate oxygen or is otherwise compromised in utero. It encompasses a clinical spectrum from reduced fetal movements (an early warning that the mother can self-detect) through to acute intrapartum fetal compromise requiring emergency delivery. This file covers antepartum and intrapartum fetal distress assessable by UMMA from maternal report.

In Kano and northwest Nigeria, fetal distress and stillbirth are major contributors to perinatal mortality — Nigeria has one of the highest stillbirth rates globally. Many of these deaths are potentially avoidable with earlier recognition of warning signs and faster access to emergency obstetric care. The key risk factors in this population include severe anaemia, malaria, pre-eclampsia, prolonged or obstructed labour, post-term pregnancy, and cord complications.

**The most important UMMA-assessable early warning sign is reduced fetal movements.** A mother who perceives that her baby is moving less than usual has identified a potential fetal problem that requires same-day assessment. UMMA must take any report of reduced fetal movements seriously, at any gestational age from 24 weeks onward.

**Why it matters for UMMA:** Stillbirth is catastrophic and in many cases preventable. In northern Nigeria, women frequently do not know that reduced fetal movements are a warning sign, or they wait to see if movements return before seeking care. UMMA can close this gap by teaching fetal movement awareness, responding immediately to reduced movement reports, and generating urgent referrals without delay. UMMA must also recognise the signs of acute intrapartum fetal distress reported by women in labour.

**Common delays and barriers:**
- Reduced fetal movements not known to be a warning sign — "the baby is sleeping"
- Waiting 24–48 hours before reporting reduced movements
- Travel time and cost to hospital with CTG monitoring capability
- Labour managed at home until advanced — acute fetal distress not assessed until delivery
- Fear that hospital will perform caesarean section — reluctance to attend
- Previous poor birth experience or bereavement at hospital
- Cultural belief that expressing concern about the baby invites bad luck — some women reluctant to "complain" about their pregnancy

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation — Early warning / reduced fetal movements

**Reduced fetal movements (RFM):**
- Mother perceives the baby is moving less than her usual baseline
- Movements feel weaker than before
- Baby that was previously very active seems quieter
- Any period of 2 hours or more in the third trimester (>28 weeks) where the mother cannot count 10 movements is clinically significant
- May be accompanied by maternal anxiety without other symptoms
- Mother may be unsure whether this is normal — any doubt warrants assessment
- No pain, no bleeding, no fluid loss at this stage

**Nigerian-specific note:** Formal kick counting (counting 10 movements in 2 hours) is not widely taught in ANC in Kano, but mothers do perceive changes in their baby's movement pattern intuitively. UMMA should validate this perception and take it seriously.

**Obstetric causes to consider with RFM:**
- Uteroplacental insufficiency (IUGR, pre-eclampsia, post-term)
- Maternal illness (malaria, severe anaemia, sepsis)
- Fetal anaemia (from Rh incompatibility — rare but present in Nigerian context)
- Oligohydramnios (reduced amniotic fluid)
- Fetal sleep cycle (normal — but mother should feel movements resume within 2 hours)

### Moderate Presentation — Subacute fetal compromise

Signs that may indicate more significant compromise, in addition to RFM:
- Maternal fever + RFM — maternal infection (malaria, chorioamnionitis) compromising fetal oxygenation
- Severe anaemia + RFM — reduced oxygen delivery to placenta
- Pre-eclampsia features + RFM (headache, visual disturbance, swelling, high BP) — placental insufficiency
- Trauma to abdomen + RFM — abruption must be excluded
- Prolonged rupture of membranes + RFM — cord compression or infection risk
- Vaginal bleeding + RFM — placenta praevia, abruption
- Maternal perception of baby becoming completely still for several hours

### Severe / Acute Presentation — Intrapartum fetal distress or acute emergency

**Signs reported by a woman in labour or late pregnancy:**
- Cord prolapse — woman in labour reports seeing or feeling something protruding from the vagina; or cord visible at vulva; ABSOLUTE EMERGENCY
- Heavy vaginal bleeding (antepartum haemorrhage) — sudden, painless (placenta praevia) or painful with board-hard abdomen (abruption)
- Cessation of fetal movements for more than 12 hours — fetal death risk
- Woman in labour reports labour has stopped but baby has not been born — obstructed labour
- Meconium-stained liquor — green or black amniotic fluid reported by woman or birth attendant — sign of fetal stress
- Prolonged labour at home — woman reporting contractions for >12–18 hours without delivery

**At facility — features of acute intrapartum fetal distress (for UMMA context if woman is calling from facility):**
- CTG showing late decelerations or prolonged bradycardia (woman may be told this by nurses)
- Fetal heart rate <100 or >180 beats per minute on auscultation
- Meconium in liquor with abnormal fetal heart rate

**Cord prolapse — maximum emergency:**
- Cord prolapse is immediately life-threatening for the fetus
- If cord is felt or seen at the vaginal opening: do not push it back; position mother on hands and knees (knee-chest position) to reduce cord compression; go to hospital immediately; call ahead

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Reduced fetal movements — normal vs abnormal:**
- Normal: movements felt, variable pattern; can feel 10 movements in 2 hours; baby sometimes more active after eating or in the evening
- Abnormal: genuine sustained reduction from the woman's normal baseline; <10 movements in 2 hours despite concentrated counting; complete cessation

**Fetal distress vs Braxton Hicks contractions:**
- Braxton Hicks: irregular tightening without progressive pain; no effect on fetal movements; no bleeding or fluid loss
- Fetal distress: RFM; associated with maternal pathology; progressive

**Placenta praevia vs Placental abruption (both cause antepartum haemorrhage):**
- Praevia: painless bright red bleeding; soft uterus; no abdominal pain; fetus may be in abnormal lie
- Abruption: painful, uterus hard/tense/board-like; dark or concealed bleeding; fetal movements may cease; maternal shock
- Both are emergencies requiring immediate hospital attendance — UMMA need not distinguish; refer immediately for any significant vaginal bleeding in pregnancy

**Chorioamnionitis vs UTI (both cause fever in pregnancy):**
- Chorioamnionitis: fever + uterine tenderness + offensive vaginal discharge + RFM after ROM; obstetric emergency
- UTI: fever + dysuria + frequency + loin pain; fetal movements usually maintained
- Any fever + RFM = treat as fetal compromise until proven otherwise

**Fetal distress vs Maternal illness causing fetal concern:**
- Maternal severe malaria, severe anaemia, sepsis, eclampsia — all can compromise fetal oxygenation; fetal distress in these cases is secondary
- Treat maternal illness and concurrently assess fetal wellbeing

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria — warrants same-day assessment
- Reduced fetal movements for 2 hours or more without return
- No other associated symptoms
- No pain, bleeding, or fluid loss
- Maternal perception that baby is quieter than usual
- Gestational age ≥28 weeks

### Moderate criteria — urgent same-day hospital attendance
- RFM persisting for several hours
- RFM with maternal fever or malaria symptoms
- RFM with pre-eclampsia features
- RFM with history of abdominal trauma
- Complete absence of fetal movements for 4–6 hours
- Reduced or stopped liquor noted by woman or birth attendant
- RFM in woman with known IUGR, placenta praevia, or high-risk pregnancy

### Severe criteria — emergency, go to hospital immediately
- Complete absence of fetal movements for >12 hours
- Any antepartum haemorrhage (vaginal bleeding in pregnancy after 24 weeks)
- Cord prolapse — any cord visible or felt at vagina
- Signs of placental abruption (painful, hard uterus + bleeding)
- Meconium-stained liquor reported (green/black fluid)
- Prolonged labour at home (>12–18 hours of contractions without delivery)
- Rupture of membranes with no fetal movement felt after
- Any intrapartum emergency reported from home birth setting
- Eclamptic fit in third trimester (fits + pregnancy = obstetric emergency)
- Maternal collapse with fetal movements absent

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- Same-day ANC or hospital attendance — fetal heart rate auscultation and clinical assessment needed
- While waiting to go: eat or drink something cold or sweet (juice, biscuit) and lie on the left side for 30–60 minutes; count movements; this can stimulate fetal movement and helps assessment
- If after 2 hours of concentrated counting fewer than 10 movements are felt: go to hospital same day without further waiting
- Do not wait until tomorrow — even if this happens at night
- At hospital: request fetal heart rate check; mention when movements reduced and for how long

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"If your baby has been moving less than usual, do not wait to see if it gets better. Go to the antenatal clinic today. Before you go, try eating or drinking something sweet and lie on your left side for an hour — count how many times you feel the baby move. If you count less than 10 movements in that time, go to the hospital now, even if it is nighttime. The nurses need to listen to the baby's heartbeat. This is too important to leave until tomorrow."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Go to the hospital today — not the PHC; needs fetal heart rate monitoring and clinical assessment of maternal cause
- If fetal movements have been absent for several hours: go now; do not wait to try the counting exercise
- Bring any ANC card and list of current medications
- Report all associated symptoms to clinician: fever, headache, bleeding, fluid loss, pain
- If malaria or severe anaemia suspected concurrently: treat as dual emergency

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your baby has not been moving properly for some hours now. You need to go to the general hospital today — they need to check your baby's heartbeat properly and find out why the baby is quiet. Bring your antenatal card. Tell the doctor or nurse everything: when you last felt the baby move, whether you have a fever, a headache, or have had any bleeding. Do not go to the health centre for this — you need the general hospital."

### Severe
UMMA should advise:
- Emergency hospital immediately — AKTH or nearest hospital with emergency obstetric care
- Do not stop at PHC unless actively delivering and cannot move
- For cord prolapse: get into knee-chest position now (on hands and knees with bottom in the air); do not push the cord back in; get to hospital as fast as possible; call ahead — the baby must be delivered by emergency caesarean immediately
- For heavy bleeding: lie flat; apply clean cloth or pad to the vagina; go immediately
- For prolonged labour at home: go immediately — obstructed labour can rupture the uterus within hours
- For complete absence of fetal movement >12 hours: emergency assessment for fetal death — hospital will confirm and provide support

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt — general:**
"What you are describing is an emergency for your baby. You need to go to the hospital right now — Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital or the nearest general hospital. Do not wait for morning. Do not go to the health centre. Tell the doctors or nurses as soon as you arrive that your baby has not been moving and [state what else is happening — bleeding, cord, labour]. They need to see you immediately."

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt — cord prolapse specifically:**
"If you can see or feel something coming out of your vagina during labour, get onto your hands and knees right now — put your bottom up in the air and your chest down. Do not push it back. Stay in that position and get someone to take you to the hospital as fast as possible. Call the hospital and tell them a cord has come out. This is an emergency for your baby."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Waiting to report reduced fetal movements — "the baby is sleeping, let us wait" — most common reason for preventable stillbirth
- Home delivery in high-risk pregnancies — women with RFM, previous stillbirth, pre-eclampsia, or obstructed labour attempting home delivery; UMMA should strongly encourage facility delivery for all high-risk women
- Applying pressure or pushing on the abdomen (external version attempts by traditional birth attendants) — can cause abruption and cord complications
- Herbal induction of labour — oxytocic herbs used by TBAs to speed up labour; cause uterine hyperstimulation and acute fetal distress; cause uterine rupture
- Prolonged unattended home labour — uterine rupture risk; fetal death
- Delaying transfer from TBA to hospital in obstructed labour — every hour of delay increases fetal and maternal mortality risk

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- CTG (cardiotocography): available at AKTH and some general hospitals in Kano; not at PHC level; fetal heart rate auscultation with Pinard or Doppler should be available at PHC
- Emergency caesarean section: available at general hospitals and AKTH; theatre readiness and blood supply variable — this is why early referral matters
- Neonatal intensive care: AKTH neonatal unit is the main facility in Kano; capacity is limited
- Ambulance: not reliably available in most parts of Kano; private or public transport used; UMMA should ask about transport at the start of any emergency conversation
- Blood bank for emergency caesarean: families should be advised in advance to have a potential blood donor identified

**Cultural factors:**
- Fear of caesarean section: deeply embedded in many communities in northern Nigeria; women and families avoid hospital attendance to avoid CS; UMMA should address this fear directly — CS is performed to save lives and does not prevent future pregnancy; the risk of not going is death for the baby and potentially the mother
- "The baby is sleeping": ubiquitous normalisation of reduced fetal movements; UMMA must gently but firmly counter this and explain that babies do not sleep for hours without any movement
- TBA (traditional birth attendant) use: TBAs are the primary birth attendant for a large proportion of Kano women, particularly in peri-urban and rural areas; UMMA should not vilify TBAs but should be clear about what they cannot manage and when transfer is essential
- Male involvement in delivery decisions: in some households, the husband or mother-in-law makes the decision about where delivery occurs; UMMA should help the woman communicate the urgency to family members if needed
- Previous stillbirth: women with previous stillbirth may be particularly anxious about fetal movements; take every report very seriously and provide emotional acknowledgement alongside clinical guidance

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Women with pre-eclampsia or hypertension — placental insufficiency leading cause of IUGR and fetal distress
- Severely anaemic women — fetal hypoxia from reduced oxygen-carrying capacity; peripartum haemorrhage with severe anaemia is almost universally fatal without transfusion
- Women with malaria in pregnancy — placental malaria causes chronic fetal growth restriction; acute severe malaria causes acute fetal distress
- Grand multiparous women (≥5 deliveries) — uterine atony risk; cord and placenta complications increase; obstructed labour risk if fetal malposition
- Post-term pregnancy (>42 weeks) — placental function declines; meconium passage; increased stillbirth risk; should not be managed at home
- Women with previous stillbirth — highest risk group for recurrence; prioritise fetal movement education and early ANC attendance
- Women with multiple pregnancy (twins) — higher risk of twin-to-twin complications, cord complications, preterm delivery
- Women with known or suspected IUGR (small-for-dates baby) — placental insufficiency already established; very high stillbirth risk; any RFM = emergency
