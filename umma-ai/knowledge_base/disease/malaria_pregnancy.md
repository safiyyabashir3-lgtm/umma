# MALARIA IN PREGNANCY — DISEASE FILE
## For UMMA AI — Maternal & Child Health Companion (Kano/Northwest Nigeria Context)

---

## OVERVIEW

Malaria in pregnancy is caused predominantly by Plasmodium falciparum in Nigeria and carries significantly greater morbidity and mortality than malaria in non-pregnant adults. Pregnancy impairs malaria immunity, particularly in primigravidae and secondigravidae, allowing parasite sequestration in the placenta (placental malaria) even without symptomatic illness. In Kano and northwest Nigeria, malaria transmission is intense and seasonal — peak transmission July to October (rainy season) — but year-round exposure is significant.

**Kano/Northwest Nigeria epidemiology:**
- Nigeria accounts for approximately 27% of global malaria cases and over 30% of global malaria deaths
- Kano state: mesoendemic to hyperendemic transmission; virtually all pregnant women have been exposed
- Malaria is the commonest single cause of fever in pregnancy in northwest Nigeria
- Malaria in pregnancy causes: maternal anaemia (often severe), low birth weight (placental insufficiency), preterm labour, spontaneous abortion, and maternal death
- Primigravidae and secondigravidae are most susceptible to symptomatic malaria; higher parity women have more immunity but are not protected from placental malaria
- Severe malaria in pregnancy has case fatality rates of 10–50% in sub-Saharan Africa
- HIV co-infection dramatically increases malaria severity and frequency
- Severe anaemia (Hb <7g/dL) is frequently malaria-driven and is a major cause of maternal cardiac failure and peripartum collapse
- UMMA target population is precisely the group at highest risk

**Why it matters for UMMA:**
- Fever in pregnancy must never be trivialised — malaria is the commonest cause and can be lethal within 24–48 hours in pregnancy
- Intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp) with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) reduces placental malaria — UMMA should promote IPTp uptake at ANC
- Cerebral malaria and severe malaria are obstetric emergencies requiring IV artesunate and intensive monitoring
- Malaria co-exists with and mimics or worsens anaemia, preeclampsia, typhoid, UTI — UMMA must consider all

**Common delays and barriers:**
- Self-treatment with patent medicine vendor antimalarials (often subtherapeutic chloroquine or underdosed artemisinin monotherapy)
- Herbal treatment — "agbo" fever preparations — before facility attendance
- Delay in attributing maternal deterioration to malaria when afebrile (anaemic malaria without current fever)
- Referral from PHC without IV artesunate — inadequate treatment before transport
- Blood transfusion unavailable at PHC and many secondary facilities

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation (Uncomplicated malaria in pregnancy)
- Fever — may be classic periodic (every 48 hours for P. falciparum) but often irregular
- Chills and rigors ("cold") preceding fever spike — woman feels intensely cold then hot
- Headache — prominent, frontal or generalised
- Myalgia — generalised body aches, "whole body paining me"
- Nausea and vomiting — common
- Fatigue and malaise
- Mild pallor
- Positive RDT or thick/thin blood film (if tested at facility)
- No altered consciousness, no severe anaemia symptoms, no bleeding
- Fetal movements: woman still perceiving normal movements
- First or second trimester most symptomatic presentations

**Note:** Mild malaria in pregnancy is NOT the same as mild malaria in a non-pregnant adult — it carries risk of rapid deterioration and must be treated promptly.

### Moderate Presentation (Symptomatic malaria with early complications or anaemia)
- All features of uncomplicated malaria PLUS:
- Progressive pallor — conjunctival and palmar pallor increasingly marked
- Exertional dyspnoea — breathless walking, climbing stairs
- Palpitations — "heart beating fast"
- Persistent vomiting preventing oral medication
- Moderate abdominal pain
- Reduced fetal movement (subjective)
- High fever (>39°C) with no sign of defervescence after 24–48 hours
- Splenomegaly (tender left upper quadrant fullness)
- Jaundice — yellow discolouration of eyes (haemolysis)
- Known HIV positive with any malaria symptoms
- Recurrent malaria (third or more episode in this pregnancy)

### Severe Presentation (Severe malaria in pregnancy — any organ system involved)
**Cerebral malaria:**
- Altered consciousness — confusion, disorientation, inability to sit, unresponsive
- Seizures (may mimic eclampsia — both must be treated simultaneously)
- Coma — GCS <11

**Severe anaemia:**
- Severe pallor (conjunctivae, palms, tongue white or near-white)
- Breathlessness at rest, tachypnoea
- Cardiac failure signs: raised JVP, gallop rhythm, pulmonary oedema
- Hb <7g/dL (if measurable) — often estimated from pallor clinically
- Haemodynamic compromise: tachycardia, hypotension, extremities cold

**Hypoglycaemia (especially with quinine use):**
- Sudden deterioration, sweating, confusion, seizure
- Can occur without obvious symptoms — sudden unconsciousness
- Pregnant women especially susceptible — increased glucose consumption

**Pulmonary oedema (non-cardiogenic):**
- Sudden severe breathlessness
- Pink frothy sputum
- Cyanosis
- Often precipitates rapidly and is frequently fatal

**Hyperparasitaemia:**
- Very high parasite counts (>5% of red cells infected) — laboratory finding if available

**Haemoglobinuria (blackwater fever):**
- Dark brown or cola-coloured urine
- Haemolysis
- Renal failure

**Obstetric complications of severe malaria:**
- Premature labour triggered by high fever
- Fetal distress — absent or significantly reduced fetal movements
- Spontaneous abortion
- Intrauterine fetal death

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Malaria vs. Typhoid fever:**
- Typhoid: stepwise fever (rising over days), relative bradycardia, rose spots (rarely seen in dark skin), constipation more than diarrhoea early, prominent abdominal tenderness, usually no rigors
- Malaria: rigors prominent, fever spikes more abrupt, often with rapid deterioration
- Both common in Kano — both can co-exist; Widal test unreliable but used widely

**Malaria vs. Preeclampsia:**
- Preeclampsia: hypertension, proteinuria, oedema — no fever (unless eclampsia post-ictal)
- Malaria: fever dominant, no hypertension from malaria itself
- CRITICAL: Malaria can trigger preeclampsia — measure BP in all febrile pregnant women

**Cerebral malaria vs. Eclampsia:**
- Eclampsia: hypertension prominent, third trimester typical, no fever at seizure onset (usually)
- Cerebral malaria: fever, positive RDT, no hypertension necessarily, any trimester
- TREAT BOTH simultaneously if uncertain — give magnesium sulfate AND artesunate

**Malaria vs. UTI/Pyelonephritis:**
- UTI: dysuria, frequency, loin pain, fever lower grade usually
- Pyelonephritis: high fever, costovertebral angle tenderness, urine cloudy
- Malaria can co-exist with UTI — rigors and very high fever favour malaria

**Malaria vs. Viral hepatitis:**
- Hepatitis: jaundice prominent, dark urine, pale stools, no rigors, no fever pattern
- Malaria haemolysis: jaundice from RBC breakdown, dark urine, but rigors and fever pattern present

**Malaria vs. Sickle cell crisis:**
- Sickle cell: known SS/SC status, bone pain, may have jaundice, fever may or may not be present
- Sickle cell crises frequently triggered by malaria — both diagnoses must be considered together

**Severe anaemia — malaria vs. other causes:**
- Malaria anaemia: usually haemolytic pattern, rapid onset, fever history
- Nutritional anaemia (iron/folate deficiency): slower onset, no fever, microcytic morphology
- Hook worm: history of walking barefoot, abdominal discomfort, eosinophilia
- In practice, anaemia in pregnancy in Nigeria is typically multifactorial — malaria + nutritional + hookworm

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Fever with rigors, headache, myalgia, nausea
- Tolerating oral fluids and medications
- No altered consciousness
- No severe pallor
- Fetal movements normal
- No vomiting preventing oral treatment
- No significant breathlessness at rest
- First or second trimester, known immune status (multigravida, prior treated episodes)

### Moderate criteria
- Persistent vomiting preventing oral antimalarials
- Moderate pallor with exertional breathlessness
- Jaundice (yellow eyes)
- Fever persisting >48 hours on oral treatment
- Reduced fetal movement
- Splenomegaly with pain
- Known HIV positive with any malaria symptoms
- Primigravida or secondigravida with any moderate symptom
- Recurrent episode in this pregnancy
- Third trimester with moderate symptoms (higher risk of rapid deterioration)

### Severe criteria — ANY ONE = emergency
- Altered consciousness or inability to sit (cerebral malaria)
- Seizure
- Severe pallor at rest with breathlessness (severe anaemia)
- Breathlessness at rest, cyanosis, frothy sputum (pulmonary oedema)
- Haemodynamic shock (cold, clammy, rapid weak pulse)
- Dark brown/cola-coloured urine (haemoglobinuria)
- Absence of fetal movements
- Sudden confusion or unconsciousness (hypoglycaemia)
- Active preterm labour with malaria
- Any malaria symptoms in first trimester with fever >38.5°C (high spontaneous abortion risk)

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
**Clinical actions:**
- Same-day facility assessment — do not treat at home without confirmed diagnosis and appropriate drug
- RDT or blood film at PHC minimum
- Artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) is treatment of choice — artemether-lumefantrine in 2nd and 3rd trimester; quinine + clindamycin considered in 1st trimester (but ACT also acceptable)
- Ensure adequate oral hydration
- Paracetamol for fever and comfort
- Ensure IPTp is up to date — if missed doses, give at this visit (SP if not currently symptomatic, not if treating active malaria)
- Fetal movement monitoring — advise kick count
- Follow-up at 48 hours if not improving

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Fever in pregnancy is always serious and could be malaria. You need to go to the health centre today to get tested. Do not take malaria medicine from the chemist without a test — the wrong medicine can harm you and your baby. Drink plenty of water. If you feel more confused, very short of breath, or your baby stops moving, go to the hospital immediately. If the test shows malaria, the doctor will give you the right treatment."

### Moderate
**Clinical actions:**
- Same-day secondary facility attendance (General Hospital minimum)
- IV or IM antimalarials may be needed if vomiting persists
- Blood transfusion may be required for moderate-severe anaemia — PHC cannot provide this
- Monitor fetal wellbeing
- Admission likely needed for monitoring
- HIV status check if unknown
- Anaemia workup: haemoglobin, blood film

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your symptoms suggest you may have malaria that is getting more serious. You need to go to the hospital — not the local chemist, the hospital — today. You may need an injection instead of tablets if you are vomiting. Your blood level may also be low and you may need a blood drip. Please go now and take someone with you."

### Severe
**Clinical actions:**
- Emergency tertiary referral — AKTH or FMC Kano
- IV artesunate is treatment of choice — must be given as soon as possible; advise caller to tell receiving facility
- If eclampsia cannot be excluded (seizure + pregnancy): give magnesium sulfate AND treat for malaria
- Hypoglycaemia: oral or IV glucose if available — advise sugared water or glucose drink en route if woman conscious
- Severe anaemia: blood transfusion urgently — do not delay transport for this; go to facility with blood bank
- Positioning: left lateral for unconscious patient
- Airway: recovery position, do not put anything in mouth during seizure
- Absent fetal movements: fetal outcome may already be compromised — maternal survival takes priority
- Inform facility ahead if possible

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"This is a medical emergency. You must go to AKTH or a big hospital right now. [Specific instruction:] If she is unconscious, lay her on her left side and keep her airway clear. If she is conscious but confused, give her sugar water to drink carefully. Do not give any medicine at home. Tell the hospital she has malaria in pregnancy and is [specific symptom] so they can prepare. Go immediately."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Chloroquine self-treatment (widely available, subtherapeutic for P. falciparum, delays correct treatment)
- Artemisinin monotherapy without partner drug (promotes resistance, incomplete treatment)
- Quinine from patent medicine vendors without glucose monitoring — hypoglycaemia risk
- Substandard/counterfeit antimalarials widely sold in Kano markets — patient may have taken something but still be untreated
- Herbal fever treatments ("agbo jedi jedi", bush bark decoctions) given first — delays effective treatment by 24–48 hours
- Withholding food from febrile women — worsens hypoglycaemia
- Treating fever with vigorous cold water bathing — can trigger seizures through rapid temperature change

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- RDTs not universally available at PHC — empirical treatment often given; acceptable in high-transmission setting
- IV artesunate not available at PHC — all severe malaria requires secondary/tertiary referral
- Blood transfusion: only available at General Hospitals and above — severe anaemia management requires referral
- Laboratory: haemoglobin measurement, blood film reading variable quality at secondary level
- NICU: preterm deliveries triggered by malaria require AKTH/FMC
- Drug stock-outs common: ACT supplies variable at PHC; UMMA should advise facility attendance where stock is confirmed

**Cultural factors:**
- Malaria frequently attributed to "bad blood," hot sun, or spiritual causes — herbal and spiritual treatments pursued first
- Women may not associate fever and weakness with a disease needing hospital — "it is just malaria" normalises the condition
- In Ramadan: women may refuse oral medications or fluids during fasting hours — severe malaria with Ramadan fasting is high risk; religious exceptions for illness must be reinforced
- Severe malaria anaemia may be misattributed to spiritual causes ("blood sucked by jinn") — delay in blood transfusion acceptance
- Blood transfusion refusal: some religious objections exist; in life-threatening anaemia, counselling needed

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Primigravidae and secondigravidae — highest symptomatic malaria risk
- HIV-positive pregnant women — dramatically increased severity and frequency
- Sickle cell disease — malaria triggers crisis; both conditions interact catastrophically
- Women not on IPTp (missed ANC visits) — placental malaria and anaemia risk higher
- First trimester — antimalarial choice more restricted; miscarriage risk from high fever
- Late third trimester — preterm labour risk, severe disease risk
- Anaemic women at baseline (nutritional, hookworm) — less reserve to withstand haemolytic anaemia
