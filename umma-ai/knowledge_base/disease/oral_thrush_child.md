# ORAL THRUSH (OROPHARYNGEAL CANDIDIASIS) — CHILD (Under 5)

## OVERVIEW
Oral thrush is a fungal infection of the oral mucosa caused by Candida albicans (and occasionally other Candida species). It presents as white patches on the tongue, inner cheeks, palate, and gums that cannot be wiped off with a cloth — a key distinguishing feature from milk coating.

In Kano and northwest Nigeria, oral thrush is extremely common in neonates and infants under 3 months, and occurs at higher rates in older children compared to high-income settings, primarily because of high background rates of immunosuppression — undiagnosed HIV, severe acute malnutrition, and prolonged antibiotic use are major drivers.

Oral thrush is considered normal and transient in healthy neonates during the first few weeks of life — acquired during delivery through maternal vaginal candidiasis, or from breast skin. However, persistent or recurrent thrush beyond 6 weeks, or thrush in a child over 6 months who has not recently received antibiotics, must trigger clinical suspicion for HIV or immunodeficiency and requires healthcare evaluation.

In the breastfeeding dyad, Candida infection on the mother's nipples (nipple thrush) and infant oral thrush form a cycle of reinfection — treatment of one without the other will fail.

Why it matters for UMMA: Oral thrush causes significant feeding difficulty. In infants who feed poorly due to thrush pain, weight faltering is rapid. Mothers frequently interpret the white patches as "milk residue" and do not seek care. In a malnourished or HIV-exposed child, thrush that descends to the oesophagus (oesophageal candidiasis) causes severe dysphagia and rapid deterioration. UMMA must identify thrush early, recognise red flags for immunosuppression, and address the breastfeeding dyad.

Common delays and barriers:
- White patches confused with milk residue — no treatment sought
- Mothers attempt to scrape off the patches with cloth, causing bleeding and pain
- Patent medicine vendors give antifungal without addressing maternal nipple co-infection
- Recurrent thrush in HIV-exposed children not recognised as a warning sign — HIV testing not sought
- Nystatin suspension often unavailable at PHC level — mothers referred but may not reach pharmacy
- Oesophageal candidiasis not recognised — child's feeding refusal attributed to teething or behaviour

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
- White or creamy-white patches on tongue, inner cheeks, or palate
- Patches have a "cottage cheese" or curd-like appearance
- CRITICAL FEATURE: patches CANNOT be easily wiped off — distinguishes from milk residue. If wiped, leave a raw, slightly bleeding red base.
- Minimal discomfort — infant feeding normally or nearly normally
- No fever
- Patches localised (tongue only, or one cheek only)
- Mother may report child is "fussy" at breast but feeding adequately
- In older infants/toddlers: child may refuse certain foods or show mild feeding reluctance

### Moderate Presentation
- Extensive white plaques covering tongue, both cheeks, palate — "painted" appearance inside the mouth
- Clear feeding difficulty — infant pulling off the breast, crying during feeds, reduced feed duration
- Mother may have sore, cracked, or burning nipples (nipple thrush) — red, shiny, or flaky nipple skin
- Weight faltering beginning
- Child irritable, especially around feeding times
- Angular cheilitis (cracks at corners of mouth)
- No dysphagia (child swallows normally — distinguish from oesophageal involvement)
- Concurrent nappy/diaper rash with satellite lesions suggesting Candida spread to perianal/gluteal skin

### Severe Presentation
- Oesophageal involvement: child refuses to swallow, gags or chokes on attempting feeds, drools excessively, cannot take breast or bottle — dysphagia is the key feature
- Severe feeding refusal leading to significant dehydration or acute weight loss
- Child in pain — crying, inconsolable, arching during any feeding attempt
- Extensive oral involvement in a child over 6 months old with no recent antibiotic history — RED FLAG for HIV or severe immunosuppression
- Thrush persisting despite adequate antifungal treatment — suggests underlying immunodeficiency
- Thrush in a child known to be HIV-exposed but untested
- Systemic candidiasis (rare, usually only in hospitalised or severely immunocompromised): fever, hepatosplenomegaly, skin lesions — requires ICU-level care

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Oral thrush vs milk residue:**
- Milk residue: wipes off cleanly with damp cloth, leaves normal pink mucosa underneath, not adherent, usually on tongue dorsum
- Oral thrush: does NOT wipe off cleanly, leaves raw/red/bleeding base, more extensive, involves cheeks and palate (not just tongue), child may show feeding difficulty

**Oral thrush vs geographic tongue:**
- Geographic tongue: irregular smooth red patches with white borders that change over time, NOT raised white plaques, no feeding difficulty, benign

**Oral thrush vs aphthous ulcers:**
- Aphthous: discrete shallow ulcers with white/yellow base and red halo, extremely painful, not raised plaques, fewer in number

**Oral thrush vs Koplik's spots (measles):**
- Koplik's spots: tiny, white, sand-grain appearance on buccal mucosa opposite lower molars specifically, appear just before measles rash, accompany fever and 3 Cs, do NOT wipe off
- Thrush: broader plaques, any oral surface, no fever pattern or measles prodrome

**Oral thrush vs herpetic gingivostomatitis:**
- Herpetic: vesicles that rupture to form ulcers, very painful, fever and lymphadenopathy, gums red and swollen, drooling, not white plaques

**Features raising suspicion for HIV/immunodeficiency:**
- Oral thrush in child over 6 months with no recent antibiotic exposure
- Recurrent oral thrush (more than 2 episodes)
- Oral thrush that fails to respond to adequate antifungal treatment
- Oral thrush with other features: persistent lymphadenopathy, recurrent serious infections, failure to thrive, chronic diarrhoea, parotid swelling
- Known HIV-exposed infant (born to HIV-positive mother)

**Common coexistences in Nigerian context:**
- Oral thrush + severe acute malnutrition (very common — Candida thrives in immunosuppressed malnourished child)
- Oral thrush + HIV (exposure or infection)
- Oral thrush + recent amoxicillin or ampicillin course (disrupts normal oral flora)
- Oral thrush + maternal vaginal candidiasis / nipple thrush (breastfeeding dyad)
- Oral thrush + Candida nappy rash (same organism, same child)
- Oral thrush + TB (immunosuppression from TB or TB treatment)

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- White patches present but localised
- No or minimal feeding difficulty — infant completing feeds
- Child aged under 6 months (thrush in this age group is common and usually benign)
- No prior episodes of thrush
- Recent antibiotic use explains presentation
- No maternal nipple symptoms OR mother willing to be co-treated
- No signs of dehydration or weight faltering

### Moderate criteria
- Extensive plaques across oral cavity
- Significant feeding difficulty — feeds shorter, child pulls off, weight faltering beginning
- Maternal nipple thrush co-existing
- Child over 6 months — needs assessment for underlying cause
- Angular cheilitis present
- Concurrent Candida nappy rash
- No dysphagia — swallowing intact

### Severe criteria — any ONE = severe
- Oesophageal involvement: dysphagia, feeding refusal, gagging, drooling — child cannot swallow safely
- Dehydration from feeding refusal
- Child over 6 months with no antibiotic history — requires HIV and immunodeficiency assessment at hospital level
- Recurrent thrush (any age) — requires investigation
- Thrush persisting after adequate antifungal course — requires investigation
- Known HIV-exposed infant with thrush — requires urgent HIV PCR/testing and specialist assessment
- Thrush with systemic features: fever, hepatosplenomegaly

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- Visit PHC for assessment and antifungal prescription (nystatin oral suspension is first-line if available)
- Continue breastfeeding — do not stop; thrush is not a reason to discontinue breastfeeding
- If mother has sore, itchy, or burning nipples, she must tell the health worker — she needs antifungal cream for her nipples at the same time as the baby's treatment, or the infection will cycle back
- Do NOT attempt to scrape the patches off — this causes pain and bleeding and does not treat the infection
- Advise on hygiene: wash hands before breastfeeding, keep feeding utensils clean
- Gentian violet 0.25% aqueous solution applied to oral mucosa is an alternative where nystatin is unavailable — but advise PHC clinician to guide, as it stains tissue and should not be used in excessive amounts; there are safety concerns with concentrations above 0.5% — always defer to clinician
- Avoid giving sugary foods or drinks — Candida thrives on sugar
- Follow up in 5–7 days — thrush should be responding

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"The white patches inside your baby's mouth look like oral thrush — a common fungal infection. It is not dangerous if treated early. Go to the clinic so a health worker can prescribe the right medicine — usually a liquid called nystatin that you put inside the mouth. Do not try to wipe or scrape the patches off — it hurts the baby and doesn't help. Keep breastfeeding — thrush is not a reason to stop. If your nipples are sore, itchy, or burning, tell the health worker because you may also need treatment — otherwise the infection can keep going back and forth between you and the baby."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Go to PHC or clinic today — child needs antifungal treatment and feeding assessment
- Both mother and child must be treated if mother has nipple symptoms
- Weight check at clinic — if weight has fallen, supplementation or high-frequency feeding support needed
- If nystatin unavailable at PHC, ask clinician about alternatives — do not buy antifungals from patent medicine vendor without guidance as formulations vary
- Child over 6 months: clinician should take history for HIV risk factors and consider testing
- If Candida nappy rash also present, this needs antifungal cream to gluteal area simultaneously
- Review in 5–7 days — if not improving, escalate

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your baby's thrush is quite extensive and is making feeding difficult. You need to go to the clinic today. The health worker will give medicine for the thrush and check your baby's weight. If your nipples are painful or itchy, please tell the health worker — you and your baby need to be treated at the same time. If your baby is older than 6 months and has not recently taken antibiotics, the health worker may want to do a blood test to check why the thrush has developed — this is important and you should not be alarmed by it."

### Severe
UMMA should advise — EMERGENCY RESPONSE:
- Go to hospital with paediatric capacity NOW
- Oesophageal candidiasis requires IV or oral systemic antifungal (fluconazole) — not nystatin alone, and not available at PHC level
- Dehydrated child from feeding refusal needs IV fluids — cannot be managed at home
- HIV-exposed child with thrush needs urgent HIV testing, specialist paediatric HIV input — refer to nearest PMTCT/HIV paediatric service (AKTH, Murtala Muhammad Specialist Hospital, designated ART sites in Kano)
- Child who has failed antifungal treatment needs specialist assessment — systemic antifungal, immunological workup
- While travelling: if child can take any liquid at all, give small sips of ORS; do not force feeding

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child's condition is serious and needs hospital care today. [For swallowing difficulty:] Your child cannot swallow properly because of the thrush — this is dangerous and they need hospital treatment and possibly a drip. [For HIV-exposed child:] Because your child has had this infection repeatedly / because you were told you have HIV during pregnancy, your child needs urgent testing and specialist care. Please go to [nearest facility] today. This is not something the patent medicine shop can treat."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Scraping oral patches with cloth or finger: extremely common practice — mothers try to "clean" the white patches. Causes pain, mucosal trauma, and bleeding without treating the infection. UMMA must address this directly.
- Applying salt, groundnut oil, or local herbs to oral thrush: does not treat Candida, may cause mucosal irritation or aspiration
- Stopping breastfeeding when thrush diagnosed: unnecessary and harmful — breastfeeding should continue with simultaneous treatment of dyad
- Treating child without treating mother: very common — thrush recurs within days
- Gentian violet in excessive concentration (above 0.5%) or on broken mucosa: occasionally causes ulceration; should only be used under clinician guidance

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Nystatin oral suspension is on Nigeria's Essential Medicines List but frequently out of stock at PHC level — mothers may need to be directed to a pharmacy. UMMA should advise going to clinic first for proper assessment, then pharmacy if clinician prescribes.
- Fluconazole (for oesophageal or severe candidiasis) generally not stocked at PHC — hospital level required
- Paediatric HIV services (for HIV-exposed children with thrush) are available at designated ART sites in Kano — UMMA should know these referral points
- Nutritional rehabilitation for malnourished children with thrush: CMAM (Community Management of Acute Malnutrition) programme sites in Kano — PHC-level entry point for SAM management with RUTF (ready-to-use therapeutic food)

**Cultural factors:**
- White coating on a baby's tongue is often seen as normal and associated with breastmilk — mothers may not recognise it as requiring treatment
- In some communities, thrush in a new baby is attributed to the birth environment or mother's diet — home remedies applied first
- Mothers may not disclose HIV status or exposure when discussing child's recurrent infections — UMMA should create a non-judgmental space around HIV questions

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Neonates (very common, usually benign but requires treatment)
- HIV-exposed infants — born to HIV-positive mothers; must be distinguished from HIV-infected
- Children with severe acute malnutrition — immune suppression makes Candida invasive
- Children on prolonged antibiotic courses (amoxicillin, ampicillin, cotrimoxazole)
- Children on inhaled corticosteroids (less common in this setting but relevant if receiving treatment for wheeze)
- Children with TB or on TB treatment
- Breastfed infants whose mothers have untreated vaginal or nipple candidiasis
