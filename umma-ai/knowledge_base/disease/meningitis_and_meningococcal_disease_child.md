# DISEASE FILE: MENINGITIS AND MENINGOCOCCAL DISEASE — CHILD
**Category:** Child (0–5 years)
**Subcategory:** Neurological / Infectious emergency
**Version:** 1.0
**Context:** Kano and northwest Nigeria

---

## OVERVIEW

Meningitis is one of the most feared and lethal childhood illnesses in northern Nigeria. Kano and the northwest fall within the African "meningitis belt" — a band of sub-Saharan Africa stretching from Senegal to Ethiopia with hyperendemic Neisseria meningitidis transmission and periodic epidemic surges, typically during the dry harmattan season (December–May).

Key pathogens by age in Nigerian context:
- **Neonates (0–28 days):** Group B Streptococcus, E. coli, Listeria, Gram-negative bacilli
- **Infants 1–3 months:** Above pathogens + Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib)
- **Children 3 months–5 years:** S. pneumoniae (most common bacterial cause), Neisseria meningitidis (epidemic threat), Hib (declining with vaccination), Mycobacterium tuberculosis (TB meningitis)
- **Viral (aseptic) meningitis:** Enterovirus, mumps — less severe but important differential

Meningococcal disease (Neisseria meningitidis) deserves specific attention:
- Serogroup A historically dominant in belt; serogroup C and W135 increasingly important
- Meningococcal meningitis can be epidemic — clusters in families and communities
- Meningococcemia (septicaemia without meningitis) may present without meningeal signs — purpuric rash is emergency
- MenAfriVac (serogroup A conjugate vaccine) has been introduced but coverage incomplete; other serogroups not covered

TB meningitis is specifically important in northwest Nigeria:
- May present subacutely over days to weeks
- Often initially diagnosed as simple febrile illness
- High mortality and severe neurological sequelae if not treated early
- Must be considered in any child with meningitis not responding to standard antibiotics

UMMA note: Any child with signs of meningitis requires immediate emergency referral. There is no safe home management of suspected meningitis.

Common delays:
- First 24–48 hours attributed to malaria or "body heat" — antipyretics given
- Patent medicine vendor treatment for headache
- Religious/spiritual consultation before hospital
- Transport cost and distance from tertiary centres

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild / Early Presentation
- Fever (may be high, >39°C)
- Severe headache in older children (under 2 cannot verbalise)
- Irritability, unsettled — infant cries when lifted or when head/neck moved
- Poor feeding, refusing breast
- Photophobia (child turns away from light, eyes screwed shut)
- Vomiting (may be projectile)
- Bulging anterior fontanelle in infants under 18 months — important positive sign; note: fontanelle normally bulges when child cries — assess when calm
- Early meningococcal disease: fever + cold hands/feet + mottled skin — may precede rash

### Moderate Presentation
- High fever with neck stiffness (child resists neck flexion, turns head en bloc)
- Positive Kernig's sign (pain/resistance to knee extension with hip flexed) — difficult to assess in under 2 years
- Positive Brudzinski's sign (passive neck flexion causes involuntary knee flexion)
- Increasingly drowsy — not easily roused
- Prolonged seizure or postictal state
- Focal neurological signs developing (eye deviation, facial asymmetry, limb weakness)
- Petechiae (small non-blanching spots) — EMERGENCY if present: meningococcal septicaemia
- Opisthotonos (severe neck/back arching) — sign of meningeal irritation

### Severe Presentation
- **Purpuric/petechial non-blanching rash** — especially spreading rapidly — meningococcaemia until proven otherwise
- Seizures that are prolonged, repeated, or not stopping (status epilepticus)
- Coma or deep unresponsiveness (GCS <10; or on AVPU: P or U)
- Signs of raised intracranial pressure: irregular breathing, Cushing's triad (hypertension + bradycardia + irregular breathing), papilloedema, dilated unreactive pupil(s), posturing (decerebrate/decorticate)
- Septic shock (cold peripheries, capillary refill >3 seconds, weak rapid pulse, hypotension)
- Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC): bleeding from IV sites, purpura fulminans
- **TB meningitis (late):** cranial nerve palsies (6th nerve palsy — eye cannot abduct), hydrocephalus, progressive drowsiness over days to weeks

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**From malaria (cerebral malaria):**
- Both can present with fever, seizures, altered consciousness
- Positive malaria RDT/smear supports malaria — but does not exclude co-existing meningitis
- Neck stiffness and bulging fontanelle suggest meningitis rather than cerebral malaria
- Purpuric rash = meningococcal disease (not malaria)
- In practice: treat both empirically when uncertain — lumbar puncture differentiates definitively if available
- Co-infection with malaria AND bacterial meningitis occurs in this region

**From febrile seizure:**
- Simple febrile seizure: brief (<15 min), generalised, single episode, child recovers fully and rapidly, no neck stiffness
- Meningitis: prolonged seizure, altered consciousness after seizure, neck stiffness, other meningeal signs, toxic appearance
- Treat any febrile seizure with meningeal signs as meningitis until proven otherwise

**From viral encephalitis:**
- More prominent behavioural/personality change, hallucinations, focal signs
- Fever + altered consciousness + focal signs = consider herpes encephalitis (if available, acyclovir should be added empirically)
- Nigerian context: arboviral encephalitides (e.g. West Nile) possible but rarely distinguished clinically

**From TB meningitis:**
- Subacute onset (days to weeks rather than hours)
- History of TB contact, BCG scar absent
- Cranial nerve palsies
- Chronic systemic illness — weight loss, night sweats
- Poor response to standard antibiotics
- CSF: very high protein, very low glucose, lymphocytic pleocytosis

**From raised ICP from other causes:**
- Cerebral malaria, intracranial abscess, subdural empyema
- Clinical differentiation difficult — all require neuroimaging at tertiary level

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Alert, responsive child
- Fever + irritability + photophobia + headache (or fontanelle full)
- No neck stiffness (young infants: absent meningeal signs do NOT exclude meningitis)
- No altered consciousness
- No seizure
- No rash
- Note: even "mild" meningitis is a medical emergency — this category exists only to guide that urgent (same-day) referral is needed

### Moderate criteria
- Clear neck stiffness / meningeal signs
- Drowsiness but rousable
- Single brief seizure that has resolved
- Bulging fontanelle
- Petechiae (non-blanching) without spread — go immediately regardless

### Severe criteria — any ONE = life-threatening emergency
- Non-blanching petechial or purpuric rash (especially spreading)
- Coma or deep altered consciousness (AVPU = P or U)
- Status epilepticus (seizure >5 minutes or repeated seizures)
- Signs of raised ICP (irregular breathing, Cushing's triad, posturing, unequal pupils)
- Signs of septic shock
- Purpura fulminans or DIC signs
- Neonatal meningitis (all neonates with suspected meningitis = immediate emergency regardless of apparent severity)

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild (alert child with early meningeal signs — still urgent)
UMMA should advise:
- Go to hospital with inpatient paediatric capacity today — urgently, not tomorrow
- Do not give antibiotics at home and then wait — this does not eliminate the need for hospital assessment and may interfere with CSF results without curing the disease
- No food or water by mouth if consciousness is in any way altered
- Keep child as calm and comfortable as possible during transport
- Note time symptoms started — relevant for clinical assessment

**Plain language for UMMA:** "Your child has signs that could mean meningitis — a serious infection of the brain covering. Please go to a hospital today, urgently. Do not wait to see if it gets better. Do not give antibiotics at home first. This infection can get much worse very quickly. The hospital needs to examine your child and do tests today."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Emergency hospital admission required — go now
- Do not stop at pharmacy or patent medicine vendor
- If child has a seizure on the way: position on their side, protect from injury, do not put anything in mouth
- Bring vaccination card if available — vaccination history is relevant
- If petechiae (small dark spots that do not go white when pressed) appear anywhere — go to the nearest hospital immediately by the fastest route

**Plain language for UMMA:** "This is an emergency. Please go to the hospital right now. Your child needs urgent treatment. If you see small dark red spots appearing on the skin that do not go white when you press them, this is a very dangerous sign — go to the hospital as fast as possible by any means. Do not wait for morning."

### Severe
UMMA should advise:
- Call for help and go to AKTH or nearest hospital with emergency paediatric care immediately — this is life-threatening
- Meningococcal disease with rash can kill within hours
- If child is fitting: lay on side, do not restrain, do not put anything in mouth, note time
- If child is unconscious: recovery position, ensure airway is open
- Do not give anything by mouth
- Do not delay for any reason — cost, distance, or time of day

**Plain language for UMMA:** "Your child is in a life-threatening emergency. Go to AKTH or the nearest hospital with an emergency department right now — call someone to help you go faster. A spreading rash with dark spots, unconsciousness, or continuous fitting means this infection is attacking your child's blood and brain. Every minute matters. Go immediately."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Treating meningitis symptoms with paracetamol for 2–3 days before seeking hospital care — most dangerous delay
- Patent medicine vendor management of "headache and fever" — antibiotics given at sub-therapeutic doses may partially treat but not cure, masking diagnosis and creating delay
- Enemas and traditional emetics given for fever with vomiting — risk of aspiration in altered-consciousness child
- Cranial application of hot poultices or scarification for "head sickness"
- Delay for spiritual/prayer consultation — actively dangerous in meningococcal disease

**Epidemic context:**
- Meningococcal epidemics occur in Kano and northwest Nigeria, typically December–May (dry harmattan season)
- During outbreaks: one case in a household = high risk for contacts — advise family to seek immediate care for ANY household member developing fever + headache
- UMMA should be calibrated to increase meningitis suspicion during harmattan months
- Meningococcal A vaccination (MenAfriVac) given in campaigns — ask vaccination history; incomplete protection

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Lumbar puncture (LP) not available at primary health centre level
- Parenteral antibiotics (ceftriaxone, penicillin) not consistently stocked at secondary level
- Blood culture rarely available; empiric treatment is the standard
- Dexamethasone (adjunctive treatment for pneumococcal meningitis) not consistently used
- Neuroimaging not available at most facilities in Kano — management is largely clinical
- TB meningitis confirmation requires CSF AFB/GeneXpert — available at AKTH and limited sites

**Cultural factors:**
- Neck stiffness in infants may be attributed to "baby's neck not yet strong" — not recognised as danger sign by some mothers
- Fontanelle assessment: mothers often fear touching fontanelle (beliefs about vulnerability) — may not notice bulging
- Photophobia interpreted as "child doesn't like light/noise" — not recognised as meningeal sign

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Unvaccinated or incompletely vaccinated children (Hib, PCV13 coverage gaps)
- Children with SCD — pneumococcal meningitis risk dramatically elevated in functionally asplenic child
- Malnourished children — immune impairment
- HIV-positive children
- Children in crowded household environments (meningococcal transmission)
- Neonates born to mothers with group B Streptococcus (GBS) colonisation — GBS screening not routine in Nigeria
- Children with recent viral URTI (secondary bacterial meningitis)
