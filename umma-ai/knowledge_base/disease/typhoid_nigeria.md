# DISEASE FILE: TYPHOID FEVER AND SALMONELLA BACTEREMIA
## Nigerian Context (Kano / Northwest Priority) — Awaiting Clinician Verification

---

## OVERVIEW
Typhoid fever — caused by Salmonella Typhi — and related non-typhoidal
Salmonella (NTS) bacteremia are among the most important causes of serious
febrile illness in Nigerian children. Research conducted specifically in
Kano and northwest Nigeria shows that Salmonella species account for
24 to 60% of all bacteremia in children under 5 presenting to hospital —
making it the leading identified bacterial cause of serious childhood
illness in this region. One in six febrile children under 5 presenting
to emergency units in northwest Nigeria has bacteremia, mostly Salmonella.
Typhoid is endemic in Kano due to poor sanitation, unsafe water supply,
and widespread use of street food. Typhoid and malaria frequently coexist —
one does not exclude the other. Intestinal perforation — a life-threatening
surgical complication — is more common in Nigerian typhoid than in other
settings. Multidrug-resistant Salmonella strains are present in Nigeria
and treatment failure with ampicillin, chloramphenicol, and cotrimoxazole
is common. Blood culture is the gold standard for diagnosis but is
unavailable at most Kano primary and secondary facilities.

---

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN CHILDREN AND PREGNANT WOMEN

### Early Presentation — Week 1
- Fever — gradual onset, rising progressively each day
- Headache
- Vomiting
- Abdominal discomfort — diffuse, not sharp
- Poor appetite
- Constipation more common than diarrhoea in early stages
- Child or adult looks unwell but may still be ambulatory
- Fever does not have the episodic chills and sweating pattern of malaria

### Moderate Presentation — Week 2
- Persistent fever — now high and sustained, not episodic
- Child looks increasingly unwell — lethargy pronounced
- Abdominal distension — tummy looks swollen
- Diarrhoea may develop — loose stools, sometimes described as pea soup
- Hepatosplenomegaly — enlarged liver and spleen, tender abdomen
- Rose spots — faint pink spots on trunk — rarely seen in dark skin
- Marked weakness — child not wanting to move
- Confusion beginning in severe cases

### Severe Presentation — Emergency
- Intestinal perforation — sudden severe abdominal pain, rigid abdomen,
  child deteriorates rapidly — surgical emergency
- Typhoid encephalopathy — severe confusion, agitation, seizures, coma
- Severe bleeding — blood in stool
- Myocarditis — fast irregular heartbeat, chest pain
- Hepatitis — jaundice developing
- Shock — collapse, very low blood pressure
- Respiratory deterioration

---

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES
- Fever beyond 3 days that worsens progressively each day —
  not episodic like malaria
- Lethargy disproportionate to the fever level — child looks
  more unwell than expected
- Abdominal distension alongside fever
- Constipation in early stage — unusual in malaria
- History of eating street food, drinking unsafe water, or poor
  sanitation in household
- Fever not responding to antimalarials after confirmed or empirical
  malaria treatment
- In northwest Nigeria — prolonged fever with lethargy and tachypnea
  without clear chest focus should always raise typhoid suspicion
- Typhoid and malaria can and do coexist — treating one does not
  exclude the need to treat the other

---

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild — Week 1, child still ambulatory
- Fever less than 5 days, child still drinking and eating some,
  no abdominal distension, no altered consciousness

### Moderate — Week 2 features
- Persistent fever beyond 5 days, lethargy, abdominal distension,
  vomiting, child not improving

### Severe — Emergency
- Any of: intestinal perforation signs, encephalopathy, shock,
  severe bleeding, jaundice, seizure, inability to drink

---

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
- Urgent clinic visit — same day
- Blood test to exclude malaria concurrently
- Antibiotics needed — prescribed by clinician after assessment
- Oral ciprofloxacin or azithromycin — where drug sensitivity allows
- Oral fluids — paracetamol for fever
- Soft bland diet — avoid hard foods that stress inflamed bowel
- Strict rest
- Return immediately if abdominal pain worsens suddenly

Plain language for caregiver:
"This fever may be caused by a serious infection from contaminated food
or water that needs antibiotic medicine. Your child needs to go to the
clinic today for a blood test and treatment. Give paracetamol for the
fever and keep giving fluids. Come back immediately if the tummy pain
gets suddenly much worse."

### Moderate
- Hospital today — not just a clinic
- IV antibiotics may be needed if vomiting prevents oral medication
- Blood culture where available
- IV fluids if dehydrated
- Close monitoring for intestinal perforation

Plain language for caregiver:
"This infection is getting more serious and your child needs to be
admitted to hospital today for proper treatment through a drip if needed.
Please go now."

### Severe — Perforation or Encephalopathy
- Immediate emergency — surgical assessment needed for perforation
- IV antibiotics, IV fluids, surgical referral
- Tertiary centre — AKTH Kano for surgical cases

Plain language for caregiver:
"This is now a surgical and medical emergency. Your child needs to
go to the largest hospital immediately. Please go now — do not stop anywhere."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS
- Intestinal perforation from typhoid is more common in Nigeria than
  in other countries — sudden severe worsening of abdominal pain in a
  child with prolonged fever must always be treated as perforation until
  proven otherwise
- Multidrug resistance — ampicillin, chloramphenicol, and cotrimoxazole
  resistance exceeds 38% in Kano strains — these drugs should not be
  used empirically
- Widal test — widely used in Kano for typhoid diagnosis — has very
  poor specificity and sensitivity in endemic areas; a positive Widal
  does NOT confirm typhoid and a negative does NOT exclude it
- Blood culture is the only reliable diagnostic tool — where available
  at AKTH and MMSH Kano — request it
- Typhoid and malaria coexist frequently — a child diagnosed with
  malaria whose fever does not improve by day 3 to 4 should have
  typhoid actively reconsidered
- Street food — suya, kunu, masa, and other foods sold in Kano markets
  — is a major transmission vector; caregivers should be counselled
- Safe water access is limited in many Kano communities — boiling water
  and safe storage must be reinforced
- Typhoid vaccine (Vi polysaccharide or TCV conjugate) is available and
  effective — not yet in the routine Nigerian immunisation schedule but
  available privately in Kano; TCV is WHO-recommended for endemic areas
- Relapse occurs in 5 to 10% of cases even after treatment — caregiver
  should be warned and instructed to return if fever recurs
