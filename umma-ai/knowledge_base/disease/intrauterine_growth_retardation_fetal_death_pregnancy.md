# UMMA Disease File — Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR) & Intrauterine Fetal Death (IUFD)
# Category: Pregnancy illness | Subcategory: Fetal wellbeing
# Last reviewed: 2025

---

## OVERVIEW

This file covers two related but distinct conditions joined in one file because IUFD is frequently the endpoint of unrecognised or unmanaged IUGR in the Nigerian context, and UMMA's clinical reasoning for both shares the same maternal symptom cluster — reduced fetal movements, a small-feeling pregnancy, and risk factor identification.

**Intrauterine Growth Restriction (IUGR):**
IUGR is the failure of the fetus to reach its genetically determined growth potential, defined clinically as estimated fetal weight or abdominal circumference below the 10th centile for gestational age. It is caused by placental insufficiency (most common), maternal disease, fetal abnormality, or infection. In Kano and northwest Nigeria, IUGR is extremely common — driven by the high prevalence of malaria in pregnancy, severe anaemia, pre-eclampsia, and maternal malnutrition. Many cases are undetected because symphysis-fundal height (SFH) measurement is inconsistently performed at ANC and ultrasound access is limited.

**Intrauterine Fetal Death (IUFD):**
IUFD is the death of a fetus at or after 28 weeks gestation (the Nigerian definition, consistent with WHO for low-resource settings). Nigeria has one of the highest stillbirth rates globally — approximately 28 per 1,000 births nationally, with rates significantly higher in the northwest. The majority of IUFDs in Kano are associated with preventable causes: antepartum haemorrhage, pre-eclampsia, prolonged obstructed labour, and severe malaria. Many stillbirths occur in women who had prior warning signs — particularly reduced fetal movements — that were not acted upon.

**Why it matters for UMMA:** UMMA's primary role for both conditions is early detection through fetal movement monitoring and risk factor identification, combined with urgent referral. For IUFD, UMMA has an additional critical role: breaking the news sensitively, providing emotional support, directing the woman to hospital for safe delivery management, and preventing dangerous home management. Retained dead fetus beyond 4 weeks risks disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) — a life-threatening maternal complication.

**Common delays and barriers:**
- Reduced fetal movements not recognised as a warning sign — normalised as "sleeping"
- ANC attendance too infrequent or too late to detect IUGR by SFH measurement
- Ultrasound not routinely available at PHC level — IUGR undiagnosed
- Women with IUFD delaying hospital attendance due to grief, fear, or denial
- Belief that the baby will "wake up" — waiting for movement to return
- Shame around fetal death — fear of blame from husband or family
- Home management of IUFD by TBAs using herbal oxytocics — dangerous

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### IUGR Presentations

**Mild Presentation — suspected IUGR, not yet confirmed:**
- SFH measuring small for gestational age (>2 cm below expected for gestation) — detected at ANC
- Mother may report that her pregnancy "does not look big enough" compared to previous pregnancies
- Fetal movements present but mother may notice they feel less forceful than previous pregnancies
- No maternal symptoms directly attributable to IUGR
- Risk factors present: hypertension, anaemia, malaria history, poor nutrition, smoking (rare in this context)
- Fetus active — not yet compromised

**Moderate Presentation — IUGR with fetal compromise developing:**
- SFH significantly small — 3 or more weeks behind expected gestation
- Reduced fetal movements noted by mother — genuine sustained reduction from her baseline
- Maternal underlying condition worsening: uncontrolled hypertension, pre-eclampsia features, recurrent malaria
- Oligohydramnios — mother may notice the abdomen looks and feels smaller than expected; less fluid cushioning felt
- On ultrasound (where available): reduced amniotic fluid index, reversal of end-diastolic flow on Doppler — tertiary level assessment

**Severe Presentation — IUGR with acute fetal compromise or impending IUFD:**
- Markedly reduced or absent fetal movements — baby has been still for many hours
- Very small SFH — abdomen significantly smaller than expected
- Signs of maternal precipitant: severe pre-eclampsia (headache, visual disturbance, severe hypertension), severe malaria, severe anaemia with cardiac compromise
- Antepartum haemorrhage — placental abruption precipitating acute fetal compromise
- Any acute maternal collapse

### IUFD Presentations

**Recognition of IUFD:**
- Complete absence of fetal movements — typically noticed by the mother as a sudden stop or a gradual cessation over 12–24 hours
- Abdomen may feel different — softer, less taut; woman may sense something has changed
- Loss of pregnancy symptoms — breast tenderness reduced, nausea gone (in women who still had these)
- Woman may describe a sensation that "something happened" — this intuitive perception is clinically meaningful
- No fetal heart heard on auscultation — confirmed at facility
- On ultrasound: absent fetal cardiac activity — confirmatory

**Signs that IUFD has been present for some time (maceration):**
- Significant reduction in SFH — fetus has lost fluid and volume
- Absence of fetal heart sounds on multiple checks
- Woman may report a brownish or dark discharge — not common but can occur

**IUFD complication — DIC (if fetus retained >4 weeks):**
- Bruising appearing spontaneously
- Bleeding from gums, venepuncture sites
- Heavy vaginal bleeding
- Collapse — this is a life-threatening emergency

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**IUGR vs Normal small pregnancy:**
- Normal variation: SFH within 2 cm of expected; fetal movements normal; no risk factors; no maternal disease
- IUGR: SFH >2 cm below expected; risk factors present; may have reduced movements; ultrasound confirms if available

**IUGR vs Incorrect dates:**
- In northern Nigeria, gestational age is frequently uncertain — many women do not know LMP and have had no first trimester ultrasound
- UMMA should not attempt to resolve this — refer for ultrasound dating + growth assessment

**IUFD vs Reduced fetal movements (baby still alive):**
- The distinction cannot be made at home — requires fetal heart rate auscultation or ultrasound
- UMMA must not attempt to reassure that the baby is alive based on maternal report alone
- ANY report of absent fetal movements for several hours = attend hospital for fetal heart assessment today

**IUFD vs Fetal sleep cycle:**
- Normal fetal sleep lasts 20–40 minutes maximum — not hours
- Movements should always return within 2 hours
- A mother who reports no movement for more than 2 hours after trying to stimulate the baby (eating, cold drink, lying on side) must be assessed at hospital

**IUFD vs Placental abruption causing IUFD:**
- Abruption causes acute fetal death — painful, hard uterus, bleeding (may be concealed), sudden cessation of movements
- Both require emergency hospital attendance
- UMMA need not distinguish — both are emergencies

**Causes of IUFD in Kano context most commonly:**
- Antepartum haemorrhage (abruption, praevia) — see placenta_previa_pregnancy.md
- Pre-eclampsia / eclampsia — placental insufficiency
- Severe malaria in pregnancy — placental malaria + acute haemolysis
- Severe anaemia — fetal hypoxia
- Prolonged obstructed labour — intrapartum asphyxia
- Umbilical cord accident — cord prolapse or true knot
- Congenital abnormality — less assessable in this context without anomaly scan

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria — IUGR suspected, fetus currently stable
- SFH measuring small at ANC
- Fetal movements present and felt regularly
- No acute maternal illness
- No reduced movements
- Detected incidentally at routine ANC

### Moderate criteria — IUGR with developing concern or risk factor present
- Confirmed or suspected IUGR with any reduction in fetal movements
- IUGR in context of pre-eclampsia, uncontrolled hypertension, or severe anaemia
- SFH significantly behind dates with no ultrasound confirmation
- Mother with previous IUFD now reporting reduced movements in current pregnancy

### Severe criteria — IUFD confirmed or strongly suspected, or acute fetal compromise
ANY ONE:
- Absent fetal movements for >2 hours despite stimulation attempts — hospital today
- Absent fetal movements for >12 hours — emergency
- No fetal heart heard at facility — confirmed IUFD — manage at hospital
- Signs of DIC (bleeding, bruising) in woman with known or suspected IUFD — emergency
- Acute maternal collapse with absent fetal movements
- Antepartum haemorrhage with absent fetal movements

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild — IUGR suspected
UMMA should advise:
- Attend ANC as soon as possible for SFH measurement, fetal heart rate check, and referral for ultrasound if available
- Fetal movement awareness: count movements every day — expect at least 10 movements in 2 hours; if not felt, attend hospital same day
- Treat underlying causes aggressively: malaria prevention (IPTp), iron supplementation for anaemia, ANC management of hypertension
- Increase ANC visit frequency — IUGR pregnancies should be seen every 2 weeks minimum
- Avoid heavy physical labour — rest improves uteroplacental blood flow
- Nutrition: maximise protein and caloric intake within household means
- Do not miss any scheduled ANC appointment

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"The nurse has noted that your belly may be measuring a little small for how many months pregnant you are. This needs to be watched carefully. The most important thing you can do at home is count your baby's movements every day — you should feel at least 10 movements in two hours. If you do not feel that many, go to the clinic the same day. Make sure you take your iron tablets and malaria prevention medicine, and go to all your ANC appointments. Do not miss them."

### Moderate — IUGR with concern or developing compromise
UMMA should advise:
- Hospital or specialist ANC attendance within 24 hours — not routine PHC
- Ultrasound assessment of fetal growth and liquor volume needed — refer to facility with ultrasound
- Fetal movement monitoring: any reduction = attend hospital same day without delay
- Maternal conditions must be treated urgently and concurrently — hypertension, anaemia, malaria
- Birth plan discussion: if IUGR is severe, delivery timing will be planned by specialist — UMMA should prepare woman for the possibility of early delivery

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Because your baby may not be growing as well as expected, you need to be seen at the hospital soon — not just the health centre. They may want to do a scan to check the baby's size and the fluid around the baby. Until then, check the baby's movements every single day. If you do not feel the baby moving properly — ten movements in two hours — go to the hospital the same day. Your own health — your blood pressure and blood level — is also directly affecting the baby, so keep taking all your medicines."

### Severe — IUFD confirmed or strongly suspected

**When UMMA suspects or receives report of IUFD:**

Step 1 — Do not confirm or deny fetal death. UMMA cannot confirm IUFD — only auscultation or ultrasound can. State clearly that the baby needs to be checked at hospital today.

Step 2 — Emotional acknowledgement first. Before giving instructions, briefly acknowledge the fear and distress the mother is experiencing.

Step 3 — Urgent but not panic-inducing directive. The woman must go to hospital today — but she is not actively haemorrhaging (unless DIC has started) so she does not need to run out the door this second.

Step 4 — If DIC signs (bleeding, bruising) — this IS an emergency; go now.

UMMA should advise:
- Go to the hospital today — they need to listen for the baby's heartbeat and do a scan
- Do not try to confirm the diagnosis at home
- Do not attempt to induce labour at home with any herbal preparation or traditional method — this is extremely dangerous
- At hospital: they will confirm whether the baby's heart is beating; if IUFD is confirmed, they will manage the delivery safely
- Medical or surgical management of delivery is essential — retained dead fetus risks serious infection and bleeding problems for the mother
- Family support: encourage the woman to bring her husband or a trusted female family member with her

**If IUFD is confirmed at hospital and woman is calling UMMA from facility:**
- Provide emotional support
- Confirm that the hospital's plan (induction or surgical management) is the safe and right path
- Do not encourage leaving the hospital against advice
- Grief acknowledgement — this is a devastating loss; UMMA should say so plainly and kindly

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt — suspected IUFD:**
"I understand you are very frightened because you have not felt your baby move. I cannot tell from here whether your baby is well — only the hospital can check that properly. Please go to the hospital today so they can listen to the baby's heartbeat and do a scan. Take someone with you. If there is any bleeding or if you notice bruising on your skin, go immediately — do not wait."

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt — confirmed IUFD, supporting the mother:**
"I am so deeply sorry for what you are going through. Losing a baby is one of the hardest things a mother can experience. Please stay at the hospital and let the doctors help you — they need to deliver the baby safely so that you are protected. You have not done anything wrong. Please lean on your family right now. I am here whenever you need me."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Herbal oxytocics administered by TBAs to expel a dead fetus — causes uterine rupture, catastrophic haemorrhage, and maternal death; extremely common in parts of Kano
- Waiting at home with a confirmed or suspected IUFD — coagulopathy (DIC) risk increases with time; beyond 4 weeks, DIC is a near-certain complication
- Vigorous abdominal massage to "wake" the baby — can cause abruption
- Spiritual interventions delaying hospital attendance for IUFD — faith is important to acknowledge, but the mother's physical safety requires hospital management
- Concealing fetal death from family — some women try to hide the absence of movement out of fear of blame; this delays essential medical care

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Ultrasound: not available at PHC level in Kano; available at general hospitals and AKTH; some private clinics; waiting times variable
- SFH measurement: should be performed at every ANC visit but quality is inconsistent; UMMA cannot rely on SFH having been done
- Fetal Doppler: available at some general hospitals; Pinard stethoscope at PHC — adequate for presence/absence of fetal heart
- Induction of labour for IUFD: misoprostol for induction available at general hospitals in Kano; surgical management (D&E for early losses) at AKTH
- Bereavement support: formal counselling services not available at most facilities in Kano; UMMA may be the primary source of emotional support for some women

**Cultural factors:**
- Blame: IUFD is frequently attributed to the mother's behaviour, diet, movement, or moral failings in northern Nigerian communities; UMMA should proactively and gently counter this — IUFD is a medical event, not a punishment
- Husband's reaction: fear of husband's anger or blame may cause women to delay reporting absent movements; UMMA should help the woman understand that her own health is now at risk and action is urgent regardless of family dynamics
- Grief expression: grief may be suppressed in women who have had multiple losses — normalised as fate (Allah's will); UMMA should acknowledge both the spiritual framing and the medical dimension without dismissing either
- Naming and burial: in Islamic tradition, a stillborn baby is typically given a name and buried; UMMA can acknowledge this and confirm that hospital management does not prevent these rites

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Women with pre-eclampsia or hypertension — highest IUFD risk; placental insufficiency
- Women with severe or recurrent malaria in pregnancy
- Grand multiparas — uterine atony, cord accidents, abnormal placentation
- Women with previous IUFD — recurrence risk significantly elevated; highest priority for enhanced fetal movement monitoring from 26 weeks
- Adolescent pregnant women — higher IUGR risk from competition between maternal and fetal growth demands
- Women with poorly nourished / low pre-pregnancy BMI — inadequate placental development
- Post-term pregnancies (>42 weeks) — placental senescence; rapidly increasing IUFD risk after 41 weeks
