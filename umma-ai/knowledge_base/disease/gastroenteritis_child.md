# DISEASE FILE: GASTROENTERITIS (CHILD)
**Category:** Child (0–5 years)
**Subcategory:** Gastrointestinal
**Version:** 1.0

---

## OVERVIEW

Acute gastroenteritis (AGE) is the leading cause of childhood morbidity and one of the top causes of under-5 mortality in Nigeria. It encompasses infectious diarrhoea with or without vomiting caused by viral, bacterial, or parasitic pathogens. In Kano and northwest Nigeria, rotavirus is the dominant viral cause; bacterial causes include enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), Shigella, Salmonella non-typhi, and Campylobacter. Cholera (Vibrio cholerae) causes periodic epidemic outbreaks particularly during the rainy season and remains a critical diagnosis to consider in Kano given recurring outbreaks.

**Epidemiology in Kano/northwest Nigeria:**
- Peak incidence in children 6–24 months (weaning period, declining maternal antibody protection)
- Two seasonal peaks: rainy season (June–September) — waterborne bacterial/cholera outbreaks; harmattan dry season (November–February) — rotavirus peaks
- Prevalence is amplified by open defaecation, contaminated well/borehole water, unsafe weaning foods, crowded housing, and low handwashing rates
- Malnutrition and gastroenteritis exist in a vicious cycle — each worsens the other and both are highly prevalent in northwest Nigeria
- Rotavirus vaccination coverage in Kano state remains suboptimal, leaving large susceptible cohorts

**Why it matters for UMMA:**
Diarrhoeal disease is the condition mothers in this population most commonly seek advice about. The critical task is distinguishing mild self-limiting gastroenteritis from moderate-to-severe dehydrating disease and from cholera or dysentery, both of which require urgent facility care. Delay is frequently fatal in children under 2. Common barriers include: mothers attributing diarrhoea to teething (haƙori — a persistent cultural belief), use of local herbal concoctions (agbo, dawo) that worsen fluid loss, patent medicine vendor visits ahead of clinic, and distance/cost barriers to facility access.

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
- Loose or watery stools 3–5 times per day for less than 3 days
- No blood in stool
- Vomiting may be present but child can retain some oral fluids
- Child is alert, irritable but consolable
- Eyes appear normal (not sunken)
- Mouth and tongue moist or only slightly dry
- Skin pinch returns to normal immediately
- Child is passing urine — last urine within 6 hours
- Able to drink and breastfeed
- No fever or low-grade fever (< 38°C)
- Weight loss minimal or not apparent to mother

### Moderate Presentation
- Watery stools 6–10 times per day
- Vomiting that reduces oral intake substantially
- Child is restless, irritable, crying with tears reduced
- Eyes appear sunken — mother may notice or confirm when asked
- Mouth dry, tongue dry
- Skin pinch returns slowly (1–2 seconds)
- Thirsty and drinks eagerly when offered water
- Urine output reduced — last urine more than 6–8 hours ago
- Fontanelle may be sunken in infants under 18 months
- Moderate lethargy — less active than usual but rousable
- Fever 38–39°C common
- Some weight loss visible (estimated 5–10% body weight)

### Severe Presentation
- Profuse watery stools — may describe as "rice water" (suspect cholera)
- Very frequent vomiting, child cannot retain any fluids
- Child very lethargic or unconscious — cannot be roused normally
- Eyes deeply sunken
- Mouth and tongue very dry, lips cracked
- Skin pinch returns very slowly (> 2 seconds), skin may tent
- Child not able to drink or drinking poorly — too weak to suck or swallow
- No urine output for > 8–12 hours
- Fontanelle deeply sunken in infants
- Rapid weak pulse, cold extremities
- Rapid deep breathing (acidosis — Kussmaul breathing)
- Convulsions (severe hyponatraemia or hypoglycaemia)
- Blood in stool (dysentery — Shigella, EIEC, or amoeba)
- Abdominal distension (paralytic ileus from severe electrolyte disturbance)
- In cholera: massive fluid loss disproportionate to degree of illness, little or no fever

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Gastroenteritis vs Cholera:**
- Cholera: sudden onset profuse rice-water stools, minimal fever, rapid dehydration, often affects multiple family/community members simultaneously (outbreak pattern), no blood in stool, stools described as odourless or slightly fishy, older children and adults also affected nearby
- Gastroenteritis (rotavirus): more gradual onset, fever more prominent, vomiting often precedes diarrhoea, seasonal (harmattan), affects mainly under-3s

**Gastroenteritis vs Dysentery (Shigella/amoeba):**
- Dysentery: blood and mucus in stool, tenesmus (straining), fever often high, child looks toxic, abdominal cramps prominent; requires specific antimicrobial treatment
- Amoebic dysentery: more gradual onset than Shigella, less systemic toxicity, blood-streaked mucousy stool, recurrent pattern, confirm on stool microscopy

**Gastroenteritis vs Typhoid with diarrhoea:**
- Typhoid: prolonged fever preceding diarrhoea, relative bradycardia, abdominal distension, rose spots (rarely seen in dark skin), stepladder fever pattern, diarrhoea is a later feature — see typhoid_child.md

**Gastroenteritis vs Malaria with diarrhoea:**
- Malaria can present with loose stools especially in young infants; fever pattern (cyclical), no vomiting predominance, splenomegaly if chronic, rapid diagnostic test (RDT) or blood film positive — always test for malaria in febrile child with diarrhoea in Kano

**Gastroenteritis vs Intussusception:**
- Critical mimic: intussusception causes "redcurrant jelly" bloody stool with mucus, intermittent severe colicky pain (infant draws up legs, screams then becomes quiet), vomiting, palpable abdominal mass; no profuse watery diarrhoea initially — this is a surgical emergency

**Gastroenteritis vs Surgical abdomen (appendicitis, volvulus):**
- Surgical abdomen: focal abdominal tenderness, rigidity, guarding; diarrhoea is not prominent; child looks toxic; requires urgent surgical referral

**Common coexistences in Nigerian children:**
- Gastroenteritis + malaria (very common — both cause fever and loose stools)
- Gastroenteritis + severe acute malnutrition (SAM) — worst outcomes, persistent diarrhoea
- Gastroenteritis + pneumonia (combined infection is a major mortality driver)
- Gastroenteritis + intestinal worms (worm burden increases diarrhoeal frequency)

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Fewer than 6 stools per day
- Vomiting present but child retaining some fluid
- Alert and active
- Eyes not sunken
- Skin pinch normal
- Mouth moist or minimally dry
- Passing urine in last 6 hours
- Drinking normally or near-normally
- No blood in stool
- Duration less than 3 days

### Moderate criteria
- 6–10 stools per day
- Vomiting reducing fluid intake substantially
- Restless, irritable, reduced activity
- Sunken eyes (ask mother: "Do his/her eyes look different from normal?")
- Skin pinch slow (1–2 seconds)
- Mouth dry
- Urine markedly reduced (6–8+ hours since last void)
- Drinks eagerly when offered
- May have low-grade fever
- No blood in stool
- Duration may be 3–5 days with no improvement

### Severe criteria — ANY ONE = severe/emergency
- Lethargic, unconscious, or very weak — cannot sit or stand
- Unable to drink or drinking very poorly
- Skin pinch returns very slowly (> 2 seconds) or tents
- Eyes deeply sunken, no tears with crying
- No urine for > 8–12 hours
- Blood in stool with systemic illness (dysentery)
- Suspected cholera (profuse rice-water stools, community outbreak)
- Convulsions
- Cold extremities, rapid weak pulse
- Abdominal distension with reduced or absent bowel sounds
- Severe malnutrition (SAM) with any diarrhoea — automatically moderate-to-severe management

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild — UMMA advice
**Clinical approach:** Home management with ORS and continued feeding. UMMA should explain ORS preparation, zinc supplementation, continued breastfeeding, and signs to escalate.

- Continue breastfeeding — do NOT stop. Breast milk reduces stool frequency and provides antibodies.
- Offer ORS (oral rehydration salts) after every loose stool. Explain preparation: one sachet in 1 litre of clean, cooled boiled water. Use a clean cup and spoon — not a bottle.
- If ORS sachets unavailable: home fluids such as rice water with a pinch of salt, or clean water. Do not give plain sugar-salt solution unless properly measured.
- Zinc supplementation for 10–14 days: available at PHC — reduces duration and severity and prevents recurrence. Advise mother to get zinc from the clinic.
- Continue normal foods — do not fast the child. Soft foods (pap, rice, boiled yam) are appropriate.
- Do NOT give anti-diarrhoeal drugs (Imodium, loperamide) — dangerous in children.
- Do NOT give herbal concoctions — can worsen diarrhoea and cause toxicity.
- Watch for worsening: if stools increase, vomiting worsens, child becomes weak, eyes sink, or no urine — go to clinic immediately.
- Return to PHC if no improvement within 2 days.

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child has mild diarrhoea that can be managed at home but needs close watching. Keep breastfeeding — do not stop. Mix ORS in clean cooled boiled water and give a small cup after each watery stool. Continue feeding soft foods. Do not give any herbs or diarrhoea medicines. Watch your child — if they become weak, stop urinating, their eyes look sunken, or the diarrhoea gets worse, take them to the clinic immediately. If they are no better in 2 days, go to the clinic for zinc tablets."

---

### Moderate — UMMA advice
**Clinical approach:** Needs PHC/clinic assessment same day for supervised ORS therapy and evaluation. Do not manage at home.

- Child needs to go to a clinic or PHC TODAY — same day, do not wait until tomorrow.
- At the facility, the child will receive supervised ORS therapy (Plan B rehydration: approximately 75 ml/kg over 4 hours).
- Continue breastfeeding throughout.
- Advise mother to start giving ORS or breast milk on the way to the clinic — do not wait.
- If the child is vomiting, advise to give ORS in small sips frequently (teaspoon every 1–2 minutes) rather than large amounts.
- If blood in stool is present alongside moderate features, this changes urgency — treat as severe and seek higher facility.
- Malaria RDT should be done at facility — co-infection is common.
- Advise against stopping at a patent medicine vendor on the way — the child needs proper assessment.

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child is becoming dehydrated and needs to be seen at a clinic today — do not wait. On your way, keep giving small sips of ORS or keep breastfeeding. When you arrive at the clinic, tell them how many times your child has had diarrhoea and vomiting today, and that they have not passed urine for [X hours]. Do not buy medicines from a chemist before going — the clinic needs to check your child first."

---

### Severe — UMMA advice
**Clinical approach:** Emergency. Needs immediate referral to a facility with IV fluid capability — general hospital or teaching hospital (AKTH in Kano). Call for help now.

- This is a medical emergency. The child needs IV fluids urgently — this cannot wait.
- Go to the nearest general hospital or emergency department NOW. Do not go to a PHC first if a higher facility is accessible.
- In Kano: Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital (AKTH) emergency, or the nearest general hospital.
- If the child is unconscious or having seizures: call for help immediately and do not give anything by mouth — risk of aspiration.
- If the child can still swallow despite weakness: continue small sips of ORS or breast milk in the car/on the way.
- Do not delay for any traditional treatment. Do not give herbs, agbo, or any fluids other than ORS or breast milk.
- If cholera is suspected (community outbreak, rice-water stools): alert the facility on arrival — this has public health implications and contacts need to be traced.
- UMMA should stay available to support the mother through the referral process.

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child is very seriously ill and is severely dehydrated. This is an emergency. You must go to a hospital with an emergency unit right now — not a patent medicine store, not a small clinic. Your child needs a drip (IV fluids) urgently. If your child is unconscious or fitting, do not put anything in their mouth. Go immediately."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous local practices:**
- Teething belief (haƙori): Diarrhoea widely attributed to teething in northern Nigeria. Mothers delay seeking care, use herbal lancing of gums, and apply traditional medicines including caustic substances to the gums. UMMA must acknowledge the belief respectfully while redirecting to treatment — diarrhoea is dangerous regardless of cause and needs treatment now.
- Herbal concoctions (dawo, agbo, masa): Widely used first-line. Some contain laxative compounds that worsen diarrhoea; others contain hepatotoxic or nephrotoxic ingredients. Some practitioners give enemas which increase dehydration risk.
- Anti-diarrhoeal drugs from patent medicine vendors: Loperamide (Imodium) is dangerous in children — causes toxic megacolon, prolongs bacterial infection, and can cause fatal ileus. Commonly sold without restriction.
- Withholding food and fluids: Some mothers believe resting the gut helps — this worsens dehydration and malnutrition.
- Diluted ORS: Mothers sometimes add extra water thinking it is safer — dilutes electrolyte content and reduces effectiveness.

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- ORS sachets are frequently out of stock at PHCs — UMMA should mention home fluid alternatives as backup
- Zinc is often unavailable at lower-level facilities despite being on the essential medicines list
- IV fluid supplies at PHCs are unreliable; moderate-severe cases should be directed to general hospitals
- Transport costs and distance are major barriers in peri-urban and rural Kano — UMMA should acknowledge this and help mothers problem-solve (community emergency transport schemes where available, neighbours, motorcycle taxis for emergencies)
- Power outages affect refrigeration and equipment at facilities

**Cultural factors:**
- Fathers or mothers-in-law may be the decision-makers for seeking care — UMMA should consider this in how it frames urgency
- Shame around open discussion of stool frequency — UMMA should normalise and use clear language
- Ramadan: fluid restriction during fasting hours can critically worsen dehydration in children — infants and young children should not fast; UMMA should clarify this if relevant

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Children with severe acute malnutrition (SAM): impaired immune response, electrolyte disturbances (low potassium/magnesium), risk of refeeding syndrome — standard ORS may cause fluid overload; need specialist nutritional rehabilitation centre (NRC) management
- Infants under 6 months: rapid deterioration, harder to assess; any diarrhoea in a neonate or infant under 2 months = urgent facility referral
- Children not breastfeeding: higher risk of contaminated water exposure and worse outcomes
- HIV-exposed or positive children: persistent or recurrent diarrhoea, unusual pathogens (Cryptosporidium), higher mortality
- Post-measles children: measles damages gut mucosa, prolonged diarrhoea common — ensure measles vaccination status is checked
