# CHICKENPOX (VARICELLA) — CHILD (Under 5)

## OVERVIEW
Chickenpox is a highly contagious primary infection caused by varicella-zoster virus (VZV), characterised by a pruritic vesicular rash appearing in successive crops, beginning on the trunk and spreading centripetally. It is one of the most recognisable childhood infectious diseases.

In Kano and northwest Nigeria, chickenpox occurs in epidemic cycles, with most children acquiring primary infection between ages 1–7 years. Unlike in high-income countries where varicella vaccination is routine, Nigeria does not include varicella vaccine in its national immunisation schedule — virtually all Nigerian children are susceptible and will acquire chickenpox unless naturally infected earlier. Herd immunity from vaccination is absent.

Most chickenpox in immunocompetent children is self-limiting. However, in Kano context, severe and complicated chickenpox is disproportionately common because of high rates of coexisting conditions — severe acute malnutrition significantly worsens outcome, secondary bacterial skin infection (with S. aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes) is extremely common due to poverty, overcrowding, poor wound hygiene, and fingernail contamination from scratching. Invasive Group A streptococcal infection complicating chickenpox is life-threatening.

In children with HIV, undiagnosed or untreated, chickenpox can be severe, prolonged, atypical, and fatal. Visceral dissemination (pneumonia, hepatitis, encephalitis) occurs in immunocompromised children.

Neonatal varicella — occurring when a newborn is exposed to maternal chickenpox within 5 days before to 2 days after delivery — is a severe and potentially fatal form requiring urgent intervention.

Why it matters for UMMA: Mothers in Kano generally recognise chickenpox ("agange" in Hausa, or sometimes called "small pox" colloquially — must be differentiated from actual smallpox historically; smallpox is eradicated). Common traditional responses — bursting blisters, applying paste substances to rash, bathing in herbal water — increase the risk of severe secondary bacterial infection. UMMA must identify complicated chickenpox, distinguish from smallpox concern/monkeypox (important differential in current epidemiological context), and escalate immunocompromised children urgently.

Common delays and barriers:
- Chickenpox managed entirely at home with traditional practices — bacterial superinfection develops before clinic presentation
- Secondary bacterial skin infection of chickenpox lesions misdiagnosed as a separate skin condition
- Disseminated chickenpox in HIV-positive child not recognised — child presents late with multi-organ involvement
- Neonatal varicella not recognised as an emergency — managed as simple chickenpox
- Transport barriers to facilities with IV aciclovir for severe cases

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
- Prodrome: 1–2 days of low-grade fever, malaise, reduced appetite before rash appears (may be absent or unnoticed in young children)
- Rash: begins as small red macules (flat spots) → rapidly progresses to papules (raised) → vesicles ("dew drops on a rose petal" — thin-walled blister on red base) → pustules → crusts/scabs
- KEY FEATURE: rash appears in successive crops — multiple stages present simultaneously on the body at any one time (some spots, some blisters, some scabs)
- Distribution: starts on trunk/scalp/face, spreads to limbs — trunk-predominant (centripetal)
- Pruritus (itching) is typically intense and is the dominant symptom causing distress
- Oral/mucosal ulcers: vesicles in mouth or on palate that rupture — cause oral pain and feeding difficulty
- Mild fever (37.5–38.5°C)
- Child is uncomfortable, itching, irritable but alert, feeding, and breathing normally
- Total skin lesions: 200–500 in typical case
- No signs of bacterial skin infection

### Moderate Presentation
- Higher fever (38.5–39.5°C) persisting through rash phase
- Extensive rash — numerous lesions, dense on trunk and face
- Significant scratching with early excoriation (broken blisters from scratching)
- Some lesions beginning to show signs of secondary bacterial infection: increasing redness around individual lesions, yellow/green discharge, increased local warmth — early impetiginisation
- Significant oral lesion burden causing clear feeding difficulty
- Child uncomfortable, not sleeping well, irritable but responsive
- In malnourished children: rash more extensive, healing slower, bacterial superinfection appearing earlier

### Severe Presentation
- **Secondary bacterial skin infection (impetiginisation / ecthyma):** lesions with confluent spreading erythema, purulent discharge, bullae (large fluid-filled blisters), ulceration — can involve large body surface area. Associated fever, child systemically unwell.
- **Invasive Group A Streptococcal / Staphylococcal infection:** fever, rapidly spreading cellulitis, toxic appearance, hypotension, child extremely unwell — necrotising fasciitis is rare but occurs and is rapidly fatal
- **Varicella pneumonia:** increased respiratory rate for age, chest indrawing, cough, hypoxia — can be primary VZV pneumonia or secondary bacterial pneumonia. More common in immunocompromised.
- **Encephalitis / cerebellar ataxia:** ataxia (unsteady gait, stumbling) is the most common neurological complication — often presents 1 week after rash onset. Encephalitis proper: altered consciousness, seizures, focal neurological signs — less common but high mortality.
- **Reye's syndrome:** acute liver failure and encephalopathy associated with aspirin use during chickenpox — MUST WARN AGAINST ASPIRIN IN ANY CHILD WITH CHICKENPOX
- **Disseminated VZV in immunocompromised child (HIV, malnutrition):** prolonged rash (lasting weeks), new crops appearing beyond day 10, haemorrhagic lesions, visceral involvement — hepatitis (jaundice, abdominal pain), pneumonitis, encephalitis
- **Haemorrhagic chickenpox:** bleeding into lesions (purpuric/petechial rash within or around lesions), systemic haemorrhage — rare, very high mortality
- **Neonatal varicella:** neonate under 28 days (especially 5 days before to 2 days after maternal delivery rash) — can develop widespread disseminated infection, pneumonia, encephalitis. High mortality without treatment.

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Chickenpox vs monkeypox (mpox):**
- Monkeypox: CRITICAL differential in current Nigerian epidemiological context. Nigeria has endemic monkeypox.
- Monkeypox: lesions are DEEPER, more indurated, all in same stage simultaneously (unlike chickenpox crops), more painful than itchy, prominent lymphadenopathy (especially cervical, axillary, inguinal) BEFORE rash, lesion progression is synchronous, lesions commonly on palms and soles (rare in chickenpox), more severe systemic illness, longer incubation
- Chickenpox: superficial vesicles, successive crops in different stages simultaneously, intense itch, lesions start centrally (trunk/scalp), synchronous lymphadenopathy less prominent before rash
- Any suspected monkeypox requires notification and public health reporting — do not manage as chickenpox without clinical assessment

**Chickenpox vs hand, foot and mouth disease (HFMD):**
- HFMD: vesicles on palms, soles, buttocks and oral ulcers — specific distribution, not generalised
- HFMD: vesicles DO NOT progress to crust in the same way, child not as systemically unwell in typical cases
- Chickenpox: generalised trunk-predominant distribution

**Chickenpox vs impetigo:**
- Impetigo: honey-coloured crusting without preceding vesicle stage, no crops, no fever in uncomplicated form, no mucosal involvement
- Chickenpox WITH secondary impetigo: existing chickenpox lesions with superimposed bacterial infection — both present simultaneously

**Chickenpox vs insect bite reactions / papular urticaria:**
- Papular urticaria: pruritic papules, no vesicles, no successive crops, same stage lesions, history of insect exposure, no fever or mucosal involvement

**Chickenpox vs measles:**
- Measles: maculopapular rash (NO vesicles), cephalocaudal progression, rash not itchy, Koplik's spots, 3 Cs prodrome (cough/coryza/conjunctivitis), no successive crops

**Chickenpox vs scabies:**
- Scabies: papular lesions with burrows, interdigital web spaces, periumbilical, persistent weeks-months, household members affected, no systemic illness, no successive crops

**Early chickenpox (macule/papule stage) on dark skin:**
- Early lesions may be less visible on very dark skin — the vesicle (blister) stage is the most recognisable
- Mother may describe "small water bumps" — the local recognition of vesicles is generally good across Nigerian communities

**Common coexistences in Nigerian context:**
- Chickenpox + severe acute malnutrition — dramatically worsens course and bacterial superinfection risk
- Chickenpox + HIV (undiagnosed) — atypical, prolonged, disseminated
- Chickenpox + secondary bacterial infection (S. aureus, S. pyogenes) — extremely common
- Chickenpox + malaria — concurrent febrile illness complicates assessment; both common in same population
- Chickenpox + measles (unimmunised child may be susceptible to both) — rare but both circulate in same communities

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Age over 28 days, immunocompetent (well-nourished, no HIV risk factors, no known immunodeficiency)
- Typical vesicular rash in successive crops
- Fever low-grade or absent
- Child itchy and uncomfortable but alert, feeding, and breathing normally
- No signs of bacterial superinfection of skin lesions
- No neurological symptoms
- No respiratory symptoms beyond mild cough

### Moderate criteria
- Higher fever (above 38.5°C) persisting beyond rash onset
- Extensive rash with dense involvement of face, trunk, and limbs
- Significant oral lesions causing feeding difficulty
- Early impetiginisation of some lesions (local signs of bacterial infection) without systemic spread
- Moderate malnutrition present (but not SAM)
- Child uncomfortable, sleep-disturbed, but maintaining some oral intake
- No respiratory distress, neurological changes, or systemic toxicity

### Severe criteria — any ONE = severe/emergency
- Neonatal varicella (age 0–28 days)
- Signs of secondary bacterial infection with systemic spread: spreading cellulitis, toxic appearance, high fever with very unwell child, purulent large lesions
- Respiratory distress: fast breathing for age + chest indrawing + grunting or cyanosis (varicella/bacterial pneumonia)
- Neurological: ataxia (unsteady, falling, cannot walk), altered consciousness, seizures
- Haemorrhagic lesions (bleeding into rash)
- Prolonged rash beyond day 10 with new crops appearing (immunocompromised — HIV, SAM)
- Known or suspected HIV-positive or severely malnourished child (SAM) with chickenpox — automatic severe
- Disseminated VZV signs: jaundice + rash, hepatomegaly, bleeding
- Child unable to maintain any oral intake — dehydration risk
- Reye's syndrome features: vomiting + encephalopathy after aspirin use during illness

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- Chickenpox is usually manageable at home in healthy children — reassure mother
- DO NOT burst or scratch the blisters — key message. Keep child's fingernails cut short and clean. Mittens on hands for infants.
- Bathe child daily in clean cool water — plain water, no herbal or salt baths. Pat dry gently.
- Apply calamine lotion to the rash if available — provides itch relief. Where unavailable, cool damp cloth can give temporary relief.
- Give paracetamol syrup for fever and discomfort — appropriate dose for weight. DO NOT give aspirin or ibuprofen — aspirin can cause Reye's syndrome (fatal liver/brain complication); ibuprofen may worsen bacterial skin infection.
- Oral lesions: offer cool soft foods, encourage breastfeeding, small frequent feeds
- Isolate child from other children and particularly from newborns, pregnant women, and immunocompromised people — highly contagious from 2 days before rash until all lesions have crusted
- Child is no longer infectious when all lesions have crusted (usually 5–7 days after rash starts)
- Monitor daily for danger signs: spreading redness/pus from lesions, fast breathing, unusual sleepiness or fits, new crops of blisters after day 10

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child has chickenpox. In most healthy children, chickenpox gets better on its own in about a week. The most important things are: do not let your child scratch or burst the blisters — cut their nails short today and keep hands clean; bathe your child in cool clean water every day and pat dry gently; give paracetamol syrup for the fever and discomfort — but do not give aspirin or Ibuprofen, these can be very dangerous in chickenpox. If you have calamine lotion, apply it to the spots to reduce itching. Keep your child away from other children, especially babies and pregnant women — chickenpox spreads very easily. Watch carefully every day — if the spots become very red and oozing pus, if your child starts breathing fast, becomes very sleepy, or has a fit, go to hospital immediately."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Go to PHC or clinic for assessment — high fever with extensive rash needs evaluation
- Clinician should assess skin for early bacterial superinfection requiring topical or systemic antibiotic
- Clinician should assess nutritional status — if SAM present, manage as severe
- Pain and fever management with paracetamol — reiterate no aspirin
- Oral hygiene and soft diet for oral lesions — maintain breastfeeding
- Daily skin review by mother — escalation criteria provided
- If signs of bacterial superinfection appear (spreading redness, pus, warm swollen skin around lesions): return to clinic immediately
- Antihistamine (chlorpheniramine) may be appropriate for severe itch — clinician to prescribe

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child's chickenpox is quite severe with a lot of spots and a high temperature. Please take them to the clinic today so a health worker can check whether any of the spots have become infected with bacteria — which is common with chickenpox and needs different treatment. Keep doing what we discussed: no scratching, no aspirin, bathe daily, give paracetamol. If you notice any spots becoming very red with pus coming out, or the skin around spots swelling and spreading, or if your child starts breathing fast or becomes very sleepy, go to hospital straight away without waiting."

### Severe
UMMA should advise — EMERGENCY RESPONSE:
- Go to hospital with emergency paediatric capacity NOW
- Bacterial skin superinfection with systemic spread: child needs IV antibiotics — cannot be treated at home or PHC
- Respiratory distress: child needs oxygen, IV antivirals (aciclovir), IV antibiotics — hospital only
- Neurological (ataxia, altered consciousness, seizures): neurological emergency — go to AKTH or nearest hospital with paediatric capacity
- Neonatal varicella: go to AKTH or hospital with neonatal unit — neonate needs IV aciclovir urgently; mortality without treatment is very high
- HIV-positive or suspected immunocompromised child: IV aciclovir is standard of care — only available at tertiary level
- While travelling: keep child cool, maintain airway, give paracetamol if child can swallow, do not apply anything to the skin
- If Reye's syndrome suspected (vomiting + confusion/encephalopathy after aspirin use): tell the hospital immediately — this is a different emergency from chickenpox alone
- CRITICAL message to all mothers at any severity level: DO NOT GIVE ASPIRIN TO A CHILD WITH CHICKENPOX

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child's chickenpox has become a medical emergency and they need to go to hospital immediately. [For skin infection:] The spots have become badly infected and the infection is spreading — your child needs injection antibiotics in hospital. [For breathing difficulty:] Your child is struggling to breathe — they need oxygen and antiviral medicine through a drip. [For neurological signs:] Your child is unsteady / is not responding properly / has had a fit — this is a brain complication of chickenpox and needs emergency hospital treatment. [For neonate:] A newborn baby with chickenpox is in serious danger — go to hospital right now, this baby needs urgent treatment. Please go to the hospital now, not the chemist. Tell the doctor or nurse your child has chickenpox."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Bursting blisters deliberately: practised in some communities to "release the poison" or speed healing — dramatically increases secondary bacterial infection risk and scarring. UMMA must address this clearly.
- Applying salt, potash (kaun), or ash to rash: causes chemical irritation, breaks skin barrier, dramatically increases bacterial infection risk
- Herbal baths with locally prepared plant decoctions: may contain irritants, risk of secondary infection; some herbal preparations contain salicylates (natural aspirin-like compounds) — theoretical Reye's risk
- Giving aspirin for fever in chickenpox: paracetamol is not always the default antipyretic — aspirin is used in some households for fever. This is potentially fatal in chickenpox. UMMA must proactively warn against aspirin/acetylsalicylic acid in every chickenpox case.
- Restricting bathing during chickenpox: common belief that bathing worsens chickenpox. Opposite is true — daily bathing reduces bacterial load on skin. UMMA must counter this clearly.
- Traditional scar prevention applications (shea butter, oils applied to open lesions during illness) — not harmful in themselves but timing matters; application to open lesions increases infection risk

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- IV aciclovir: available at AKTH and major secondary hospitals in Kano but not routinely stocked at PHC or many general hospitals
- Oral aciclovir: available at pharmacies in Kano — may be prescribed for high-risk immunocompetent children (severe rash, older adolescents) but paediatric dosing and availability varies
- Varicella immunoglobulin (VZIG): not routinely available in Nigeria — may be available at AKTH in very limited supply for neonatal varicella or immunocompromised contacts
- Isolation facilities: not available at home level in compound living with large families; UMMA should advise practical isolation measures within the compound
- Monkeypox reporting: Nigeria Centre for Disease Control (NCDC) requires notification of suspected monkeypox — UMMA should flag if chickenpox presentation has atypical features (synchronous lesions, palms/soles involvement, prominent lymphadenopathy, very severe systemic illness)

**Cultural factors:**
- Chickenpox is generally recognised across northern Nigerian communities ("agange" or regional variants) — mothers usually know when a child has it
- Community belief that chickenpox must "run its course" — reduces care-seeking even when complications develop
- Child isolation is practiced culturally (which is correct) — but may delay seeking care if family feels the child should be "kept hidden" at home
- Previous chickenpox is generally protective (correct understanding) — but may lead to deliberate exposure of other children ("pox parties") — potentially dangerous for immunocompromised siblings
- Concern about scars from chickenpox is significant — mothers may apply traditional scar-prevention remedies to active lesions (risk of infection)

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Neonates (0–28 days) — emergency; especially those born to mothers with peripartum rash
- Children with severe acute malnutrition (SAM) — bacterial superinfection, dissemination
- Children with undiagnosed or untreated HIV — disseminated, haemorrhagic, or chronic varicella
- Children on oral corticosteroids (e.g. nephrotic syndrome, reactive airways) — severe disseminated disease
- Children with haematological malignancy (acute leukaemia) — relevant but less common presentation in this setting
- Adolescents and adults with no prior history of chickenpox — primary varicella more severe with increasing age; adults at risk of varicella pneumonia
- Pregnant household contacts — chickenpox in pregnancy causes congenital varicella syndrome (first trimester) or neonatal varicella (peripartum); UMMA should flag this if a pregnant woman in the household is exposed
