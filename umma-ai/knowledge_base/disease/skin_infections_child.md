# SKIN INFECTIONS — CHILD (Under 5)
*UMMA Disease File | Paediatric Category*

---

## OVERVIEW

Skin infections are among the most common presentations in children under 5 in Kano and northwest Nigeria. They encompass a spectrum from superficial bacterial infections (impetigo, folliculitis) to deeper infections (cellulitis, abscess) and fungal infections (tinea capitis, tinea corporis, candidiasis). In this context, they frequently arise as secondary infections complicating heat rash, insect bites, scabies, and eczema — so identifying the primary cause is essential.

**Epidemiology (Kano/Northwest Nigeria):**
- Hot humid climate, overcrowded housing, and limited access to clean water drive high prevalence
- Impetigo is extremely common in children under 5, often in clusters within households and schools/Quranic schools (tsangaya)
- Tinea capitis (scalp ringworm) is endemic; caused predominantly by *Trichophyton violaceum* in this region — different from Western predominant species
- Scabies is co-endemic and frequently complicated by secondary bacterial infection (impetiginised scabies)
- Neonates are at particular risk for neonatal skin infections including omphalitis and staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome (SSSS)
- Malnutrition dramatically increases risk and severity of skin infections — common co-morbidity in this population

**Why it matters for UMMA:**
- Skin infections are frequently dismissed as minor or treated with inappropriate traditional remedies, allowing progression to sepsis
- Neonatal skin infections (omphalitis, SSSS) can be rapidly fatal
- Misidentification leads to delayed appropriate treatment
- Untreated impetigo can cause post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis — a preventable cause of childhood renal disease

**Common delays and barriers:**
- Patent medicine vendors (PMVs) commonly dispense antibiotics informally — often wrong agent, wrong duration
- Widespread use of toothpaste, engine oil, or herbal pastes on skin lesions
- Belief that skin conditions are spiritually caused delays healthcare seeking
- Poor water access limits wound hygiene
- Mothers may not recognise early cellulitis or systemic spread

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
- **Impetigo (non-bullous):** Honey-coloured crusted lesions on face (around nose and mouth), limbs; begin as small vesicles/pustules that rupture; child is well, afebrile, eating normally
- **Folliculitis:** Small pustules around hair follicles on scalp, back, buttocks; not tender to touch; no surrounding redness spreading
- **Tinea corporis:** Circular scaly patch with clearing centre and raised erythematous edge; mildly itchy; single or few lesions; no systemic features
- **Tinea capitis (mild):** Scaly patches on scalp with hair loss; minimal inflammation; no boggy swelling
- **Minor infected insect bite:** Single lesion, localised redness < 2cm, no streak, no swelling beyond immediate site; child afebrile

### Moderate Presentation
- **Bullous impetigo:** Larger fluid-filled bullae (often on trunk, axilla, nappy area in infants); bullae rupture leaving raw red base with collarette of skin; child may be mildly irritable; low-grade fever possible
- **Cellulitis (localised):** Area of skin warm, red, swollen, tender; defined but spreading erythema; no fluctuance (not yet abscess); child febrile (38–39°C); still drinking, alert
- **Kerion (tinea capitis with secondary infection):** Boggy, tender, inflamed scalp mass with pustules; cervical lymphadenopathy; low-grade fever; significant hair loss over affected area
- **Impetiginised scabies:** Multiple excoriated lesions with secondary crusting and pustules all over body including between fingers, wrists, groin; very itchy especially at night; other household members affected
- **Abscess (early/small):** Localised fluctuant swelling < 2cm, tender, erythematous; no systemic features or very mild fever

### Severe Presentation
- **Extensive cellulitis with systemic features:** Rapidly spreading erythema with defined edge advancing over hours; high fever (≥39°C); child toxic-looking, lethargic, refusing feeds
- **Necrotising fasciitis (rare but present in Nigerian context):** Skin may look deceptively normal early; exquisite pain out of proportion to appearance; crepitus on palpation; skin darkening/necrosis developing; child severely unwell — THIS IS A SURGICAL EMERGENCY
- **Staphylococcal Scalded Skin Syndrome (SSSS):** Predominantly in neonates and infants under 2; generalised erythema followed by large flaccid bullae and widespread skin peeling; Nikolsky sign positive; child febrile, irritable, very unwell; high risk of fluid loss and secondary sepsis
- **Omphalitis with spreading cellulitis:** Umbilical stump red, swollen, malodorous; erythema spreading onto abdominal wall; neonate unwell, febrile or hypothermic — EMERGENCY
- **Sepsis from skin source:** Any skin infection + child lethargic, not feeding, high fever, fast breathing, cold extremities, or altered consciousness — EMERGENCY regardless of skin appearance

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Impetigo vs eczema with secondary infection:**
- Impetigo: honey-coloured crust, starts as vesicle/pustule, highly contagious (other children affected), responds quickly to antibiotics
- Infected eczema: background of dry itchy skin, lichenification, personal/family atopy history, weeping eczema underneath the infection

**Cellulitis vs deep vein thrombosis / lymphoedema:**
- Cellulitis: acute onset, warm, tender, systemic features; DVT is rare in children; lymphoedema is chronic and non-tender

**Abscess vs cellulitis:**
- Abscess: fluctuant centre (feels like fluid under pressure when palpated), more localised; cellulitis is indurated without fluctuance
- Important: early abscess can look like cellulitis — reassess if not improving at 24–48 hours

**Tinea capitis vs seborrhoeic dermatitis vs bacterial scalp infection:**
- Tinea: hair loss, scaly, responds to antifungal not steroid; kerion is inflammatory but still fungal
- Seborrhoeic: greasy scale, no hair loss, eyebrows/face also affected, no lymphadenopathy
- Bacterial: pustules, no scale pattern, responds to antibiotics

**SSSS vs burns vs bullous impetigo:**
- SSSS: generalised, systemic illness, peeling across wide areas, Nikolsky positive
- Burns: history of heat exposure, clear demarcation
- Bullous impetigo: more localised, older child usually

**Necrotising fasciitis — the can't-miss diagnosis:**
- Pain far exceeding visible skin findings
- Rapid progression over hours
- Systemic toxicity out of proportion to local appearance
- Any skin infection in a very unwell child with crepitus = necrotising fasciitis until proven otherwise

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild — all of the following:
- Child afebrile or temperature < 38°C
- Lesions localised, not rapidly spreading
- Child alert, feeding well, not irritable beyond normal
- No involvement of face around eyes, genitals, or umbilicus
- No signs of systemic infection

### Moderate — any of the following:
- Fever 38–39°C with skin infection
- Cellulitis with spreading erythema but child still alert and drinking
- Bullous impetigo with multiple or large bullae
- Kerion on scalp
- Abscess requiring drainage
- Impetiginised scabies with widespread lesions
- Skin infection in a malnourished child (moderate-severe malnutrition = automatic upgrade)

### Severe — any ONE = emergency:
- Child lethargic, not feeding, toxic appearance
- Fever ≥ 39°C with skin infection and systemic features
- Rapidly spreading erythema advancing visibly over hours
- Crepitus, skin darkening, or necrosis — possible necrotising fasciitis
- SSSS pattern: generalised peeling, Nikolsky positive
- Omphalitis with abdominal wall spread in neonate
- Any skin infection in a neonate (< 4 weeks) with fever or hypothermia
- Periorbital or orbital cellulitis (eye swollen shut, pain on eye movement)

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild

**Clinical actions:**
- Identify and treat underlying cause (scabies, heat rash, insect bites)
- Impetigo: topical antiseptic cleaning (chlorhexidine solution if available), keep lesions clean and dry
- If multiple lesions or not responding to topical care: oral antibiotic appropriate for Staphylococcus and Streptococcus
- Tinea: topical antifungal (clotrimazole); tinea capitis requires oral antifungal (griseofulvin — available in Nigeria) — topical alone will not cure scalp ringworm
- Counsel mother on hygiene: handwashing, separate towels/clothing, cut fingernails
- Treat all household contacts for scabies if scabies identified
- Review in 3–5 days if not improving

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"Your child's skin infection looks mild right now. Keep the area clean with water and soap. Don't apply toothpaste, oil, or any paste on it — these can make it worse. I'll tell you which clinic to go to for the right cream or medicine. Make sure to wash hands often and keep your child's nails short so they don't scratch and spread the infection. Come back to me if it gets worse, spreads, or your child gets a fever."

---

### Moderate

**Clinical actions:**
- Same-day attendance at primary health centre or clinic
- Oral antibiotics required (amoxicillin-clavulanate or cloxacillin appropriate for skin organisms)
- Abscess: requires incision and drainage at clinic — do not delay
- Kerion: oral antifungal + consider short course of oral steroid to reduce scarring (clinic decision)
- Assess nutritional status — refer for SAM/MAM screening if indicated
- Ensure tetanus immunisation up to date if there is a wound entry point
- Review in 48–72 hours; if worsening, escalate

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"This skin infection needs to be seen at a clinic today — it's spreading and your child may need medicine by mouth, not just a cream. Please go to [nearest PHC] as soon as you can. Don't wait to see if it gets better on its own. If you notice the redness spreading quickly, your child becomes very sleepy or stops eating, go to the hospital straight away without waiting."

---

### Severe

**Clinical actions:**
- Immediate hospital referral — do not delay
- Necrotising fasciitis: surgical emergency, call ahead to hospital
- SSSS: IV antibiotics, fluid management, wound care — requires inpatient
- Omphalitis with spread: IV antibiotics in neonatal unit
- Periorbital cellulitis: ophthalmology-accessible hospital
- During transport: keep child warm, encourage breastfeeding if infant and able, do not apply anything to skin

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"This is a serious skin infection and your child needs to go to the hospital right now — not the chemist, not the local clinic, but a hospital with inpatient care. This infection can spread into the blood and become life-threatening very quickly. Please go immediately to [referral hospital]. If you have no transport, call [emergency contact / community health worker]. Do not put anything on the skin. I know it's hard to travel but your child's life depends on getting there fast."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Application of toothpaste, engine oil, groundnut oil, kerosene, or herbal pastes to skin infections — all delay healing and introduce new pathogens
- "Burning out" infection with hot objects — causes secondary burns and deeper infection
- Scarification over infected areas (traditional practice in some communities)
- PMVs dispensing tetracycline ointment (inappropriate, causes resistance, photosensitising in children)
- Use of potent topical steroids (betamethasone/clobetasol sold without prescription) on infected skin — masks infection and drives spread

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Griseofulvin is the appropriate oral antifungal for tinea capitis in children but stock-outs are common — counsel mother on alternative facilities if unavailable
- Incision and drainage requires sterile technique — advise against self-drainage or PMV drainage in unhygienic conditions
- IV antibiotics require hospital admission — transport barriers mean some patients arrive late; community health workers (CHEWs) at PHCs should be first contact for severe cases while transport arranged

**Cultural factors:**
- Skin conditions frequently attributed to spiritual causes (evil eye, jinn) leading to use of Quranic water (rubutu) as primary treatment — acknowledge the practice respectfully, emphasise that clinic treatment is needed alongside
- Stigma around skin infections — mothers may hide conditions or delay presentation to avoid being accused of poor hygiene
- In tsangaya (Quranic boarding schools), tinea capitis and scabies spread rapidly due to shared bedding/combs — recommend treating the entire group

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Severely malnourished children (SAM): impaired skin barrier, impaired immunity — any skin infection can become rapidly severe
- Neonates under 4 weeks: SSSS and omphalitis can cause death within hours
- Children with sickle cell disease: more susceptible to osteomyelitis if skin bacteria enter bloodstream
- HIV-exposed or HIV-infected children: atypical and more severe presentations; recurrent or unusual skin infections should prompt HIV testing
- Children with severe anaemia: reduced healing capacity
