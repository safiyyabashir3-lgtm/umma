# TUBERCULOSIS — CHILD
## Disease File for UMMA AI | Maternal and Child Health | Kano/Northwest Nigeria Context

---

## OVERVIEW

Tuberculosis (TB) in children under 5 is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in Kano and northwest Nigeria, where Nigeria carries one of the highest TB burdens globally. Children are infected primarily through household contact with an infectious adult — typically a parent, grandparent, or uncle with smear-positive pulmonary TB. Because young children cannot expectorate sputum reliably, diagnosis is clinical and epidemiological rather than bacteriological in most cases, creating significant risk of missed and delayed diagnosis.

Kano State has high TB notification rates and significant rates of undiagnosed TB in the community. Overcrowded housing, poor ventilation in compound dwellings, high rates of HIV and malnutrition (which dramatically worsen TB risk and severity), and low awareness of childhood TB contribute to the burden. Many families first present to patent medicine vendors or traditional healers, receiving antibiotics or antimalarials for weeks before a TB diagnosis is considered.

UMMA relevance: Children of mothers who are themselves TB-positive, or who live with known TB contacts, are at highest risk. Children with persistent cough, weight loss, or recurrent fever not responding to standard treatment must trigger TB consideration regardless of initial diagnosis.

Common delays:
- TB considered only after multiple courses of antimalarials and antibiotics fail
- Cough attributed to "chest cold" or teething for months
- Parents fear stigma of TB diagnosis — avoid healthcare
- District hospitals have GeneXpert but access from rural LGAs is difficult
- Nutritional rehabilitation and TB treatment rarely coordinated effectively

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
- Persistent dry or productive cough lasting more than 2 weeks with no clear cause
- Low-grade fever, often in the evenings — mother notices child is "always warm" at night
- Mild fatigue, child less active than usual but still eating and playing
- Mild weight loss or failure to gain weight over 1–2 months
- Night sweats (may not be volunteered — ask directly)
- History of household TB contact present
- BCG scar present (does not rule out TB but absence in a severely ill child is relevant)
- Child otherwise appears well between fever episodes

### Moderate Presentation
- Cough persisting beyond 3–4 weeks, sometimes with small amounts of mucoid or purulent sputum
- Documented or visible weight loss — clothes loose, ribs visible, loss of fat over buttocks
- Persistent low-grade or intermittent fever for more than 2 weeks despite antimalarials
- Reduced appetite, poor feeding — mother reports child "not eating"
- Moderate fatigue — child plays less, more irritable
- Peripheral lymphadenopathy — especially cervical nodes — firm, non-tender, may be matted
- Signs of mild respiratory compromise: slightly increased respiratory rate, mild chest retractions
- Child may have had one or two courses of antibiotics without improvement
- Malnutrition (moderate acute malnutrition) often coexisting

### Severe Presentation
- Severe respiratory distress: fast breathing, chest indrawing, grunting, cyanosis
- Signs of miliary TB: very ill child, high fever, diffuse respiratory signs, hepatosplenomegaly
- TB meningitis: altered consciousness, seizures, neck stiffness, bulging fontanelle in infants, photophobia
- Severe wasting — weight-for-height z-score below -3 (SAM) with no response to nutritional therapy
- Pleural effusion: dullness on percussion, reduced breath sounds unilaterally
- Spinal TB (Pott's disease): child refusing to walk, kyphotic deformity, back pain on palpation
- HIV-positive child with any TB features — automatically escalate severity
- Age under 2 with TB contact and any systemic features — high risk of dissemination

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

TB vs Pneumonia:
- Pneumonia: acute onset, responds to antibiotics within 48–72 hours, fever typically high and sudden
- TB: subacute/chronic course over weeks to months, no antibiotic response, weight loss prominent
- TB pneumonia can coexist — lack of antibiotic response in a sick child with known contact should trigger TB workup

TB vs Malaria:
- Malaria: fever with chills and rigors, responds to antimalarials, acute onset
- TB: persistent fever not responding to antimalarials, weight loss, cough, lymphadenopathy
- Both can coexist — treating malaria and assuming resolution when fever persists is dangerous

TB vs Malnutrition alone:
- Malnutrition without TB: weight loss without fever, cough, or lymphadenopathy; responds to nutritional rehabilitation
- TB with malnutrition: failure to respond to nutritional rehabilitation is a red flag for underlying TB
- Any malnourished child not improving on RUTF within 2–4 weeks must be assessed for TB

TB vs HIV-related illness:
- HIV in children: recurrent infections of different types, oral candidiasis, parotid swelling, developmental regression
- TB and HIV coexist commonly in Kano — if HIV suspected, both must be investigated simultaneously

TB vs Lymphoma:
- Lymphoma: rapidly growing, painless nodal masses, may have systemic B-symptoms
- TB adenitis: slower growing, firm, matted, may have overlying skin changes or fistulation
- Differentiation requires histology — both warrant urgent referral

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild Criteria
- Cough more than 2 weeks AND at least one of: TB contact history, night sweats, mild weight loss
- Child feeding and hydrated
- No respiratory distress
- No neurological signs
- No lymphadenopathy or small non-matted nodes only

### Moderate Criteria
- Visible weight loss or documented weight loss AND persistent cough AND fever
- Cervical or peripheral lymphadenopathy (firm, matted)
- Mild to moderate respiratory symptoms not meeting distress criteria
- Failure of two courses of antibiotics or antimalarials
- Moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) coexisting with respiratory and systemic symptoms
- Age under 2 with household TB contact and systemic features

### Severe Criteria — ANY ONE = EMERGENCY
- Respiratory distress (fast breathing for age, severe chest indrawing, grunting, cyanosis)
- Altered consciousness, seizures, or neck stiffness (TB meningitis)
- Miliary pattern suspected: very ill, diffuse signs, hepatosplenomegaly
- Severe acute malnutrition not responding to nutritional therapy with TB features
- Pleural effusion suspected
- Spinal deformity or neurological deficits in lower limbs
- Known HIV-positive child with any TB features
- Age under 1 year with household smear-positive contact and any systemic signs

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- This combination of symptoms (cough, fever, weight loss, household contact) needs proper assessment — not a pharmacy visit
- Child must be taken to a primary health centre or TB clinic for evaluation
- TB screening includes: clinical assessment, tuberculin skin test (TST) if available, chest X-ray referral, sputum or gastric aspirate if possible
- If TB contact confirmed and child is under 5 and symptomatic: TB preventive therapy (TPT) or diagnostic treatment may be initiated by a doctor
- Nutritional support should be started immediately regardless of TB outcome
- Do not give any herbal preparations — these can mask symptoms and harm the liver
- Return if symptoms worsen before appointment

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"Your child has had a cough and fever for a while and has also been losing weight. This combination — especially if someone in the home has TB — means we need to check for TB. Please take your child to a health centre or TB clinic as soon as you can. Do not give any herbal medicine or traditional treatment until after the doctor has seen your child. This is important."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Urgent same-day or next-day clinic visit — primary health centre with TB diagnostic capacity or secondary facility
- Describe symptoms clearly to healthcare worker: duration of cough, weight loss, household contact history, previous treatments that have not worked
- Child needs: clinical examination, weight measurement, chest X-ray, TB contact investigation
- If MAM coexisting: child may need nutritional rehabilitation alongside TB investigation — both must happen simultaneously
- Do not delay for transport barriers — ask community health workers or ward focal persons for support if needed

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"Your child needs to be seen by a doctor today or tomorrow — please do not wait. The cough and weight loss that has not improved with other medicines may be TB. This is treatable but it needs proper medicine from a doctor, not from a chemist. Please go to the health centre and tell them your child has been coughing for [X] weeks, losing weight, and that someone in your home has or had TB."

### Severe
UMMA should advise:
- This is an emergency — go to hospital immediately, do not wait
- If child has difficulty breathing, fits, unconsciousness, or is very limp — call for help now, go directly to emergency unit
- Do not stop at a patent medicine vendor
- At hospital: emergency stabilisation, TB workup (chest X-ray, LP if meningitis suspected, blood cultures), initiation of anti-TB therapy
- If HIV status unknown, HIV testing should be done simultaneously
- Nutritional support (including therapeutic feeding if SAM present) must be initiated alongside TB treatment

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"This is very serious. Your child needs to go to hospital right now. Please go to the emergency unit — do not stop at a chemist. Tell the doctor your child is having [describe symptom: difficulty breathing / fits / is unconscious / not responding]. This cannot wait."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Patent medicine vendors frequently prescribe cough mixtures, amoxicillin, or cotrimoxazole for weeks — masking TB symptoms without treating the disease
- Traditional healers may attribute cough and weight loss to spiritual causes — families may spend weeks in traditional treatment before seeking biomedical care
- Herbal preparations commonly used in Kano for "chest problems" can cause hepatotoxicity, which complicates TB treatment when eventually started
- Families sometimes discontinue treatment when child begins to feel better — this is especially dangerous with TB as it causes drug resistance

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- GeneXpert machines available at secondary and tertiary facilities but cartridge stockouts are common
- Chest X-ray often requires referral to secondary facility — this is a barrier for rural LGAs
- TB drugs (fixed-dose combinations for children) have had stockouts in Kano in past years — UMMA should advise families to confirm drug availability before starting long journey
- Nutritional rehabilitation (RUTF/RUSF) and TB treatment require coordination that does not always happen at PHC level

**Cultural factors:**
- TB stigma is significant in northern Nigeria — families may resist the diagnosis or hide it
- Purdah restrictions can limit mothers' ability to bring children for repeated clinic visits — UMMA should acknowledge this and help with problem-solving
- Male guardian permission may be required for healthcare decisions in some households — this can delay care in severe cases

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Children under 2 (highest risk of dissemination and meningitis)
- HIV-positive children
- Severely malnourished children (SAM/MAM)
- Children in households with smear-positive pulmonary TB contact
- Children who have not completed BCG vaccination
