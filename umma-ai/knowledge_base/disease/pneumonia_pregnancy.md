# DISEASE FILE: PNEUMONIA IN PREGNANCY
## Nigerian Context (Kano / Northwest Priority) — Awaiting Clinician Verification

---

## OVERVIEW
Pneumonia in pregnancy is a serious condition associated with increased
maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality. Pregnant women are at higher
risk of severe pneumonia than non-pregnant women due to immunological
changes, diaphragm elevation from the growing uterus reducing lung
reserve, and increased oxygen demand. The leading bacterial causes in
Nigeria include Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and
Klebsiella pneumoniae. Viral causes include influenza. In Nigeria, malaria
must always be actively excluded when a pregnant woman presents with fever
and respiratory symptoms — both can coexist. Tuberculosis — with high
prevalence in northern Nigeria — must be considered in any pregnant woman
with prolonged cough, weight loss, or night sweats. Pneumonia in pregnancy
is associated with preterm labour, fetal growth restriction, and preterm
delivery. Women in the third trimester are at highest risk of severe disease.

---

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PREGNANT WOMEN

### Mild Presentation
- Cough — productive or dry
- Fever — low to moderate
- Mild shortness of breath on exertion — not at rest
- Pleuritic chest pain — sharp pain worse with breathing or coughing
- General malaise and fatigue
- Reduced appetite
- Fetal movements normal

### Moderate Presentation
- Persistent cough — productive, may be rusty or yellow sputum
- Moderate to high fever — with chills
- Shortness of breath — now present at rest or with minimal activity
- Chest pain more prominent
- Fast breathing — above 20 breaths per minute at rest
- Reduced feeding and fluid intake
- Woman looks unwell

### Severe Presentation — Emergency
- Severe breathlessness — unable to complete a sentence
- Very fast breathing — above 30 breaths per minute
- Oxygen saturation low — lips or fingertips bluish — cyanosis
- Confusion or altered consciousness
- Unable to maintain upright position — extreme exhaustion
- High fever with rigors
- Signs of sepsis — fast weak pulse, cold extremities
- Reduced or no fetal movement
- Haemoptysis — coughing up blood

---

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES
- Cough + fever + fast breathing + chest pain in a pregnant woman
  — pneumonia until proven otherwise
- Malaria commonly coexists — exclude malaria with RDT alongside
  evaluating for pneumonia; treat both if both suspected
- Tuberculosis — consider if cough lasting more than 2 weeks,
  weight loss, night sweats, haemoptysis, contact with TB patient
- Pulmonary embolism — sudden onset breathlessness without fever,
  pleuritic chest pain, leg swelling; rare but important to exclude
- Anaemia causing breathlessness — pallor present, no cough,
  no fever or fever from other cause
- Cardiac failure in pregnancy — breathlessness lying flat,
  leg swelling, fast heart rate; different clinical picture

---

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild
- Cough, fever, mild symptoms only at exertion
- Alert, eating and drinking, fetal movements normal
- No fast breathing at rest

### Moderate
- Fast breathing at rest, productive cough, fever
- Reduced oral intake, woman looks unwell
- Fetal movements present

### Severe — Emergency
- Any of: cyanosis, confusion, fast breathing above 30 per minute,
  inability to speak in full sentences, shock signs, reduced fetal
  movement, haemoptysis

---

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
- Urgent clinic or hospital — same day
- Chest assessment essential — do not manage at home
- Malaria RDT essential before or alongside antibiotic decision
- Oral antibiotics — amoxicillin-clavulanate or macrolide depending
  on clinical assessment and local guidelines
- Paracetamol for fever — safe in pregnancy
- Oral fluids increased
- Rest completely
- Monitor fetal movements — count and report reduction immediately
- Return immediately if breathlessness worsens

Plain language for caregiver:
"The cough and fever you have may be a chest infection that needs
antibiotic medicine. In pregnancy this is more serious than usual
because your lungs have less space and your baby needs oxygen.
Please go to the clinic today for a proper assessment and treatment.
Watch your baby's movements carefully and come back immediately if
they reduce."

### Moderate
- Hospital today
- Injectable antibiotics likely needed
- Malaria RDT and treatment if positive
- IV or oral fluids
- Fetal monitoring — preterm contractions risk
- Possible admission

Plain language for caregiver:
"This chest infection is more serious and you need hospital treatment
today. You may need medicine through a drip. Your baby also needs to
be monitored. Please go now."

### Severe
- Emergency — hospital immediately
- Oxygen, IV antibiotics, close fetal and maternal monitoring
- Possible ICU-level care
- Delivery may need to be considered depending on gestation and
  maternal condition — senior obstetric involvement essential

Plain language for caregiver:
"This is a serious chest emergency for you and your baby. Please go
to hospital immediately. Do not wait."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS
- Malaria and pneumonia coexist in pregnant Nigerian women —
  always exclude malaria when managing respiratory illness in pregnancy
- Tuberculosis must be considered in any prolonged respiratory illness
  in pregnancy — TB in pregnancy is treatable and treatment is safer
  than leaving it untreated; many women in Kano avoid diagnosis due
  to stigma
- Indoor cooking smoke from biomass fuel — common in Kano households —
  worsens respiratory illness and is a risk factor for pneumonia
- Oxygen availability at primary health centre level in Kano is limited
  — any pregnant woman with significant breathlessness needs referral
  to hospital level
- Antibiotic choice must consider safety in pregnancy — tetracyclines,
  fluoroquinolones, and chloramphenicol are contraindicated in pregnancy
- Preterm labour triggered by pneumonia — assess for contractions in
  any febrile pregnant woman with respiratory illness
- Influenza in pregnancy causes severe disease — in outbreak periods
  consider influenza as a cause of rapidly worsening respiratory illness
- Pulse oximetry is rarely available at primary level in Kano —
  clinical signs of cyanosis and respiratory rate must guide severity
  assessment
