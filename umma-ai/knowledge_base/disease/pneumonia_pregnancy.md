# UMMA Disease File — Pneumonia in Pregnancy
# Category: Pregnancy illness | Subcategory: Respiratory illness
# Last reviewed: 2025

---

## OVERVIEW

Pneumonia in pregnancy is a serious condition with higher morbidity and mortality than in non-pregnant adults. Pregnancy causes significant physiological changes to the respiratory system — the diaphragm is elevated by the growing uterus, tidal volume increases, and functional residual capacity decreases — that impair a pregnant woman's ability to compensate for respiratory compromise. Immune modulation in pregnancy also alters susceptibility to certain infections.

In Kano and northwest Nigeria, pneumonia in pregnancy is caused by bacterial organisms (Streptococcus pneumoniae most commonly), viral pathogens (influenza, COVID-19), and tuberculosis — which in this context must always be considered in the differential. Aspiration pneumonia is also more common in pregnancy due to oesophageal sphincter relaxation and slowed gastric emptying, particularly in the third trimester.

**Why it matters for UMMA:** Pneumonia in pregnancy is underdiagnosed because cough and breathlessness are attributed to normal pregnancy changes. Severe pneumonia in pregnancy is associated with preterm labour, fetal distress, and maternal death. The threshold for clinical assessment must be low. In Kano, the coexistence of TB and pregnancy makes careful evaluation essential — a pregnant woman with cough lasting more than 2 weeks must be assessed for TB without delay.

**Common delays and barriers:**
- Breathlessness attributed to normal pregnancy — "it is because the baby is big"
- Cough treated with cough syrups from PMV
- Fear that X-ray or medications will harm the baby — delays care-seeking and investigation
- TB stigma causing non-disclosure of respiratory symptoms at ANC
- Distance and transport costs to facilities with oxygen and IV antibiotics

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
- Cough — productive or dry; may produce yellow, green, or rust-coloured sputum
- Fever — moderate; may be lower grade in some viral causes
- Breathlessness — may be difficult to distinguish from physiological breathlessness of late pregnancy; key distinguishing feature is breathlessness at rest or with minimal exertion in a woman who was not breathless before
- Pleuritic chest pain — sharp, worse on inspiration or cough; localises to one side
- Fatigue disproportionate to the stage of pregnancy
- Mild tachycardia
- Respiratory rate at upper normal or mildly elevated (>20/minute at rest)
- Woman can speak in full sentences
- Able to eat and drink
- No cyanosis

**Nigerian-specific note:** Physiological breathlessness of pregnancy is common from second trimester — it occurs with exertion, improves with rest, and does not worsen rapidly. Pneumonia breathlessness is at rest, progressive, and accompanied by fever and cough. UMMA should ask specifically: "Were you breathless before this week? Does the breathlessness get better when you sit still?"

### Moderate Presentation
- Fast breathing at rest: respiratory rate >24/minute (significant in pregnancy)
- Increasing breathlessness — unable to complete sentences without stopping
- High fever >38.5°C persisting despite paracetamol
- Productive cough with rust-coloured or blood-tinged sputum
- Reduced oxygen saturation (SpO2 90–94% where measured)
- Significant tachycardia (heart rate >100/minute)
- Decreased movement or feeding intake
- Fetal movements may be reduced — woman may note this (see fetal_distress_pregnancy.md)
- Pleuritic chest pain preventing deep breathing
- Signs of dehydration — dry mouth, reduced urine

### Severe Presentation — any ONE = emergency
- Respiratory rate >30/minute at rest
- Cyanosis — blue lips, fingertips, tongue
- SpO2 <90% (where measured)
- Inability to speak more than a few words without stopping
- Altered consciousness — confusion, agitation, drowsiness
- Systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg — septic shock
- Signs of preterm labour concurrent with pneumonia (regular contractions, rupture of membranes)
- Haemoptysis (coughing blood) — consider TB, pulmonary embolism
- Failure to improve or worsening after 48–72 hours of outpatient antibiotics

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Pneumonia vs Physiological breathlessness of pregnancy:**
- Physiological: gradual onset; exertional; no fever; no cough; no pleuritic pain; SpO2 normal; improves with rest
- Pneumonia: acute onset; at rest; fever; productive cough; pleuritic pain; does not improve with rest

**Pneumonia vs Pulmonary Tuberculosis:**
- The most important differential in Kano — TB prevalence is high in northwest Nigeria
- TB: cough >2 weeks; low-grade fever; night sweats; weight loss; haemoptysis; household TB contact; previous TB treatment; HIV-positive status
- Pneumonia: more acute onset; higher fever; purulent sputum; faster deterioration
- CRITICAL: Both require hospital assessment. TB in pregnancy is not a barrier to treatment — anti-TB treatment is safe in pregnancy and must not be delayed
- TB and bacterial pneumonia can coexist

**Pneumonia vs Pulmonary Embolism (PE):**
- PE: sudden onset breathlessness and pleuritic chest pain WITHOUT fever (or with only low-grade fever); leg swelling or calf pain (DVT); no productive cough; risk factors: immobility, previous DVT, caesarean section
- Pneumonia: fever prominent; productive cough; gradual rather than sudden onset
- Both are serious — hospital assessment required

**Pneumonia vs Cardiac failure (peripartum cardiomyopathy):**
- Cardiac failure: bilateral crackles at lung bases; ankle oedema; elevated JVP; orthopnoea; cardiomegaly on CXR; no fever initially
- Pneumonia: fever; productive cough; typically unilateral or focal signs
- UMMA note: peripartum cardiomyopathy presents in late pregnancy and first months postpartum — see separate file when available

**Pneumonia vs Pre-eclampsia with pulmonary oedema:**
- Pre-eclampsia: hypertension; proteinuria; headache; visual disturbance; oedema; no productive cough
- Pneumonia: normal BP (unless septic shock); productive cough; pleuritic pain
- Both can cause breathlessness and require emergency assessment

**Pneumonia vs Asthma exacerbation:**
- Asthma: wheeze prominent; known asthma history; responds to bronchodilator; no fever (unless infection triggering exacerbation)
- Pneumonia: focal signs; no wheeze; fever; purulent sputum
- See asthma_pregnancy.md

**Features making pneumonia more likely in pregnant Kano women:**
- Productive cough + fever + pleuritic chest pain
- Fast breathing persisting at rest
- Harmattan season (November–February) — peak bacterial and viral respiratory infection season
- Living in overcrowded conditions
- Household TB contact or HIV positive
- Not immunised against influenza (rare in this context) or pneumococcus

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Respiratory rate <24/minute
- SpO2 >95% (where measured)
- Can speak in full sentences
- Fever manageable with paracetamol
- Eating and drinking adequately
- No signs of preterm labour or fetal distress
- Ill for <48 hours

### Moderate criteria — hospital or secondary care same day
- Respiratory rate 24–30/minute
- SpO2 90–94%
- High fever not controlled by paracetamol
- Struggling to complete sentences
- Reduced oral intake
- Significant tachycardia
- Cough with haemoptysis (any blood in sputum — assess for TB)
- Worsening after 48 hours of oral antibiotics

### Severe criteria — emergency
- Respiratory rate >30/minute
- SpO2 <90%
- Cyanosis
- Altered consciousness
- Systolic BP <90 mmHg
- Signs of preterm labour
- Cannot speak more than a few words

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- Same-day ANC or clinic attendance — oral antibiotics require prescription and clinical assessment
- Amoxicillin or amoxicillin-clavulanate is appropriate first-line for community-acquired pneumonia in pregnancy — clinician to prescribe
- Paracetamol for fever — safe in pregnancy; avoid ibuprofen and aspirin
- Adequate oral hydration — increased fluid intake
- Rest in a well-ventilated room; avoid cooking smoke exposure
- Do not take cough suppressants — allow productive cough to clear secretions
- Complete full antibiotic course
- If TB is suspected at assessment: sputum assessment and TB testing before or alongside antibiotics
- Monitor fetal movements — if reduced, attend hospital
- Return immediately if breathlessness worsens, high fever persists beyond 48 hours, or coughing up blood

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"The cough and fever you have may be a chest infection. You need to see a doctor or nurse at the clinic today — chest infections in pregnancy need proper medicine, not just cough syrup. Paracetamol is safe for the fever. Drink plenty of water and rest somewhere with good air — not near cooking smoke. If your breathing gets worse, or you cough up blood, or you feel the baby moving less, go to the hospital straight away."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Hospital attendance today — requires clinical assessment, likely IV antibiotics, and oxygen monitoring
- Do not drive herself if breathless — needs escort
- If coughing blood or has chronic cough history: mention this clearly at the hospital — TB must be excluded
- Fetal monitoring will be needed — inform hospital she is pregnant and gestational age
- Do not take oral antibiotics from PMV while waiting — wait for hospital prescription

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your breathing is fast and your fever is high — you need to go to the hospital today, not the health centre. They need to check your oxygen level and may need to give you medicine through a drip. Tell them you are pregnant and how many months. If you have been coughing for a long time, tell the doctor — they will want to check you have no other infection in your lungs. Do not go alone — take someone with you."

### Severe
UMMA should advise:
- Emergency hospital immediately — AKTH or nearest general hospital with obstetric and medical care
- This is a life-threatening emergency for both mother and baby
- If possible: sit upright during transport; do not lie flat
- Call ahead if possible — state pregnant woman with severe breathing difficulty
- At hospital: mother needs oxygen, IV antibiotics, fetal monitoring — insist on obstetric involvement
- If signs of preterm labour alongside pneumonia: obstetric emergency

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"This is an emergency. You are having serious difficulty breathing and you are pregnant — both you and your baby are at risk. You need to go to the hospital right now. Try to sit up in the car — do not lie flat. Tell the doctors you are pregnant, how many months, and that you are having trouble breathing. They need to check the baby and give you oxygen."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Cough syrups from PMVs — no effect on bacterial pneumonia; some contain codeine derivatives or antihistamines with pregnancy safety concerns
- Belief that X-ray harms the baby — chest X-ray with abdominal shielding is safe in pregnancy and necessary for diagnosis of pneumonia, TB, and alternative diagnoses; fear of X-ray causes diagnostic delay
- Herbal steam inhalations for chest infections — no benefit; burn risk
- Avoiding antibiotics due to fear of harm to baby — delaying treatment of pneumonia is far more dangerous than safe antibiotics (amoxicillin, erythromycin, azithromycin are safe in pregnancy)
- Taking fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin) purchased from PMV — not first-line in pregnancy; avoid without specialist advice

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Pulse oximetry not reliably available at PHC level — clinical assessment essential
- Chest X-ray available only at secondary care and above
- IV antibiotics require secondary care — PHC cannot manage moderate or severe pneumonia
- TB diagnostic services: sputum microscopy available at designated TB centres in Kano; GeneXpert at select sites; results within 1–2 days
- Oxygen concentrators variably available — general hospitals more reliable than PHCs
- Anti-TB drugs: available free of charge at DOTS centres in Kano — all LGAs have at least one DOTS site; stigma is a barrier to uptake

**Cultural factors:**
- TB stigma: very high in Kano — women fear social and marital consequences of TB diagnosis; this causes delayed presentation and non-disclosure; UMMA should address this gently and emphasise that TB is treatable and the baby can be protected
- Fear that medication harms the baby: deeply embedded; UMMA should state clearly that the medicines prescribed for chest infections are safe in pregnancy, and that untreated infection is the real danger
- Praying and local treatments before hospital: UMMA should not dismiss religious practice but must communicate urgency in moderate and severe presentations
- Purdah: women may need male escort to attend hospital; UMMA should help problem-solve transport and escort without reinforcing barriers

**High-risk subgroups:**
- HIV-positive pregnant women — opportunistic pneumonias (PCP, atypical organisms); more rapid deterioration; co-trimoxazole prophylaxis should be in place; refer urgently
- Women with TB contact or previous TB — exclude active TB before or alongside treating for bacterial pneumonia
- Women with asthma — pneumonia triggers severe exacerbation; see asthma_pregnancy.md
- Women in third trimester — reduced respiratory reserve; faster deterioration; higher preterm labour risk
- Severely anaemic women — compensatory tachycardia and breathlessness may mask or be masked by pneumonia
- Malnourished women — impaired immune response; less physiological reserve
