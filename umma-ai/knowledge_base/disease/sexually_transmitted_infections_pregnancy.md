# SEXUALLY TRANSMITTED INFECTIONS IN PREGNANCY — DISEASE FILE
## For UMMA AI — Maternal & Child Health Companion (Kano/Northwest Nigeria Context)

---

## OVERVIEW

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) in pregnancy encompass a range of bacterial, viral, and parasitic infections transmitted sexually that can affect maternal health, pregnancy outcomes, and neonatal health through vertical (mother-to-child) transmission. The most clinically significant STIs in Kano/northwest Nigeria context are: syphilis, HIV (covered in principles here; full HIV file separate), gonorrhoea, chlamydia, trichomonas vaginalis, genital herpes (HSV-2), and HPV-related conditions. Bacterial vaginosis (BV), while not classically an STI, is included here due to its association with preterm labour and clinical overlap with STI presentations.

**Kano/Northwest Nigeria epidemiology:**
- Syphilis: antenatal syphilis seroprevalence in Nigeria estimated 1–4%; congenital syphilis causes stillbirth, neonatal death, and multisystem neonatal disease and is almost entirely preventable with antenatal screening and treatment
- HIV: prevalence in northwest Nigeria lower than south but significant — vertical transmission without intervention is 25–45%
- Gonorrhoea and chlamydia: high prevalence in reproductive-age women; significant cause of neonatal conjunctivitis (ophthalmia neonatorum) and neonatal blindness; maternal cervicitis associated with preterm labour
- Trichomonas: common; associated with preterm birth and low birth weight
- HSV-2: neonatal herpes from primary maternal genital herpes at delivery carries high neonatal mortality
- STIs are highly stigmatised in Kano context — disclosure, screening, and partner treatment are major barriers
- Syndromic management (treating on symptoms) is the standard of care at PHC level in Nigeria given laboratory limitations

**Why it matters for UMMA:**
- Congenital syphilis is a leading preventable cause of stillbirth in Nigeria — UMMA should promote syphilis testing at first ANC
- Neonatal conjunctivitis from gonorrhoea/chlamydia causes preventable blindness — eye prophylaxis at delivery is critical
- STI symptoms in pregnancy trigger decisions about facility attendance, partner involvement, and referral — UMMA navigates this sensitively
- HIV PMTCT (prevention of mother-to-child transmission) depends on knowing HIV status — UMMA should encourage HIV testing at ANC

**Common delays and barriers:**
- Stigma prevents disclosure of symptoms to spouse or health worker
- Symptoms attributed to "women's problems," heat, or hygiene issues rather than infection
- Partner treatment rarely completed even when woman is treated
- PHC staff stigmatising attitudes toward STI patients in conservative Kano context
- No laboratory testing available at PHC for most STIs — syndromic approach used
- Cultural and religious context makes STI discussion very sensitive — UMMA must approach with extreme sensitivity and non-judgment

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation (Asymptomatic or incidental STI / BV / Trichomonas)

**Asymptomatic STIs (very common):**
- No symptoms at all — detected only on screening (syphilis RPR, HIV, gonorrhoea/chlamydia NAAT where available)
- THIS IS THE MOST COMMON AND MOST IMPORTANT CATEGORY — asymptomatic syphilis and HIV cause severe neonatal/fetal harm without maternal symptoms
- UMMA role: promote ANC attendance and screening uptake

**Bacterial vaginosis:**
- Thin, grey-white vaginal discharge
- Fishy odour, worse after intercourse
- Minimal or no vulval irritation
- Often asymptomatic
- Associated with preterm labour risk

**Trichomonas vaginalis:**
- Frothy yellow-green vaginal discharge
- Vulval itching and irritation
- Dysuria (burning when urinating)
- Vaginal soreness
- May be asymptomatic

### Moderate Presentation (Symptomatic STI with local complications)

**Gonorrhoea/Chlamydia cervicitis:**
- Purulent or mucopurulent vaginal discharge (yellow-green)
- Intermenstrual bleeding or post-coital bleeding
- Dysuria
- Lower abdominal discomfort (mild)
- Cervical motion tenderness (on examination — not assessable by UMMA)
- Bartholin gland abscess: painful swelling at vaginal introitus (one side)

**Primary/secondary syphilis:**
- Primary: painless genital ulcer (chancre) — indurated, clean-based, may be missed as not painful; spontaneously resolves
- Secondary: widespread copper-coloured rash INCLUDING palms and soles (distinctive), generalised lymphadenopathy, condylomata lata (warty flat lesions in anogenital region), flu-like illness, mucous patches in mouth
- Syphilis in pregnancy: most women have latent syphilis (no symptoms) detected only on RPR testing

**Genital herpes (primary episode):**
- Multiple painful blistering ulcers on vulva, vagina, cervix
- Severe vulval pain — may be unable to urinate (urinary retention)
- Fever, malaise, inguinal lymphadenopathy
- First episode in pregnancy (especially third trimester) is HIGH RISK for neonatal herpes transmission

**HPV / Genital warts (condylomata acuminata):**
- Fleshy warts on vulva, vagina, perineum — may grow rapidly in pregnancy
- Usually painless
- May bleed on contact
- Large warts may obstruct vaginal delivery (rare)

### Severe Presentation (Systemic STI complications / Neonatal risk)

**Late/tertiary syphilis in pregnancy (uncommon but occurs):**
- Neurological symptoms — confusion, personality change, visual problems
- Cardiovascular symptoms
- More commonly: detected as high RPR titre on antenatal screen

**Severe pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) — rare in pregnancy due to mucous plug, but can occur early):**
- Severe lower abdominal pain, bilateral
- High fever
- Peritoneal signs (guarding, rigidity)
- Marked cervical motion tenderness
- Systemic sepsis signs

**Neonatal risk indicators (not maternal symptoms but UMMA should screen for these histories):**
- Untreated syphilis in current pregnancy = congenital syphilis risk (stillbirth, hydrops, multiorgan disease)
- Primary genital herpes in third trimester = neonatal herpes risk at delivery (encephalitis, death)
- Untreated gonorrhoea/chlamydia at delivery = ophthalmia neonatorum (neonatal blindness risk)
- Untreated HIV without ART = vertical transmission risk

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Vaginal discharge — distinguishing causes:**
- Normal pregnancy discharge: white/clear, non-offensive, non-irritating, increasing volume normal in pregnancy
- BV: grey-white, fishy smell, no itching
- Trichomonas: frothy yellow-green, itching, offensive
- Gonorrhoea/Chlamydia: purulent yellow, no strong odour, associated with dysuria and lower abdominal discomfort
- Candidiasis (thrush): thick white curd-like, intense vulval itching, no odour — very common in pregnancy (not STI but common differential); exacerbated by antibiotics and high blood sugar (diabetes/gestational diabetes)
- PPROM: watery, clear, continuous leaking, no smell — critical to distinguish from discharge

**Genital ulcers — distinguishing causes:**
- Syphilis chancre: painless, indurated, single, clean base
- Herpes: painful, multiple, blistering/vesicular, may have systemic symptoms
- Chancroid (Haemophilus ducreyi): painful, ragged, purulent base, common in Nigeria
- Donovanosis: slowly progressive, beefy-red granulomatous tissue, painless
- Behçet's disease: rare, recurrent, associated with oral ulcers and eye disease

**Lower abdominal pain in pregnancy — STI vs. other causes:**
- PID in pregnancy: rare after first trimester, fever, bilateral, cervical motion tenderness
- UTI/pyelonephritis: loin pain, dysuria, frequency, fever
- Preterm labour: uterine contractions, cervical change
- Appendicitis: right-sided, migrating pain, no vaginal discharge
- Round ligament pain: sharp, brief, lateral, no fever

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Asymptomatic — positive screening test only (syphilis, HIV)
- Vaginal discharge (BV, trichomonas, mild cervicitis) without systemic symptoms
- No fever, no abdominal pain, no ulceration
- Fetal movements normal
- Candidiasis symptoms (itching, thick white discharge)

### Moderate criteria
- Symptomatic gonorrhoea/chlamydia cervicitis (purulent discharge + dysuria + lower abdominal discomfort)
- Primary genital herpes episode (any trimester — refer urgently in third trimester)
- Symptomatic trichomonas with severe irritation or inability to sleep/function
- Secondary syphilis symptoms (rash on palms/soles, lymphadenopathy)
- Bartholin abscess
- Positive syphilis screen without evidence of prior treatment — requires immediate penicillin course
- Known HIV positive not yet on ART — PMTCT urgency

### Severe criteria — ANY ONE = emergency or urgent specialist referral
- Signs of sepsis: fever >38.5°C, tachycardia, rigidity, severe abdominal pain (PID/sepsis)
- Primary genital herpes at ≥34 weeks gestation (neonatal herpes prevention requires specialist plan)
- Untreated syphilis detected at >28 weeks (fetal risk of stillbirth — treat immediately)
- Urinary retention from severe genital herpes (herpes-related acute urinary retention)
- Systemic syphilis symptoms (neurological, cardiovascular)
- HIV positive with opportunistic infection signs (weight loss >10%, oral thrush, persistent cough, chronic diarrhoea)

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
**Clinical actions:**
- Promote ANC attendance for syphilis RPR and HIV testing — asymptomatic screening is the most impactful intervention
- BV: metronidazole — at facility, confirmed by clinician; advise that treatment reduces preterm labour risk
- Trichomonas: metronidazole — partner must also be treated; counsel sensitively
- Candidiasis: topical antifungal (clotrimazole pessary) — safe in pregnancy; advise completing course; control blood glucose if diabetic
- Normal discharge: reassurance with clear description of what is normal
- All STI diagnoses: partner notification and treatment counselling — must be handled very sensitively in Kano context

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"The discharge/itching you are describing may be an infection that is common in pregnancy. It is important to go to the health centre so a nurse can examine you and give you the right treatment. Some of these infections can affect your baby if not treated. You do not need to be ashamed — the nurse has seen this many times. Your husband may also need to be treated so you do not keep getting it back."

*For ANC screening:*
"At your next ANC visit, make sure to ask for a blood test for syphilis and HIV. These tests can protect your baby even if you feel completely well. Early treatment completely prevents harm to the baby."

### Moderate
**Clinical actions:**
- Same-day facility attendance
- Syphilis (positive RPR, untreated): benzathine penicillin — administered at facility; if penicillin allergy, specialist input needed — do not delay treatment
- Gonorrhoea/chlamydia cervicitis: dual therapy as per Nigerian national guidelines — ceftriaxone + azithromycin; at facility
- Genital herpes (primary, second or early third trimester): aciclovir — at facility; suppressive therapy from 36 weeks to reduce neonatal transmission risk
- Primary herpes third trimester: tertiary referral for delivery planning (caesarean section may be indicated to prevent neonatal herpes)
- Known HIV not on ART: same-day HIV clinic referral — every day of delay increases MTCT risk

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"This needs proper treatment today at the hospital or health centre. Please go today. The treatment is straightforward and will protect both you and your baby. Please go today — do not wait."

*For syphilis:*
"The blood test has shown syphilis. This can be completely cured with a simple injection of penicillin. Without treatment, it can cause the baby to be very sick or even be born dead. Please go to the hospital today for the injection. Your husband also needs to be tested and treated."

*Sensitively, on partner treatment:*
"For the treatment to work properly and protect your baby, your husband will also need to take medicine. I know this can be a difficult conversation. The health worker at the clinic can help explain this to him if you wish."

### Severe
**Clinical actions:**
- Sepsis from PID/STI: emergency referral — IV antibiotics, surgical input if abscess
- Herpes at ≥34 weeks (primary): tertiary referral for delivery planning — aciclovir suppression, plan caesarean section if active lesions at onset of labour
- Urinary retention from genital herpes: catheterisation at facility
- HIV with opportunistic infection: tertiary HIV/ID specialist referral; PMTCT continuation
- Syphilis detected late in pregnancy (>28 weeks): same day penicillin — do not wait for tertiary referral if injectable penicillin available at local facility

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"This is serious and you need hospital care today. Please go immediately. [Specific instruction per scenario.]"

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Self-treatment with ciprofloxacin or amoxicillin purchased from patent medicine vendors — subtherapeutic, wrong antibiotic for STIs, drives resistance
- Vaginal douching with soap, antiseptics, or herbal preparations — disrupts normal flora, worsens BV, masks symptoms, can cause chemical vaginitis
- Traditional healers treating STIs with incisions, vapour baths, or topical applications — delays effective treatment
- Applying hot poultices or herbal pastes to genital ulcers — secondary infection risk
- Using partner's leftover antibiotics for STI treatment — inadequate dose, wrong drug
- Sharing of bicycling/herbal pessaries from market — common practice, ineffective and potentially harmful

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- NAAT testing for gonorrhoea/chlamydia not available at PHC or most secondary facilities — syndromic management standard
- RPR for syphilis available at many ANC facilities but result turnaround can be delayed — point-of-care RPR tests being rolled out in Nigeria
- Penicillin allergy testing not available — anaphylaxis risk management in penicillin-sensitive patients requires specialist input
- Aciclovir may not be stocked at PHC — genital herpes management requires secondary facility
- HIV viral load testing for PMTCT monitoring not universally available outside teaching hospitals

**Cultural factors:**
- Extreme stigma around STIs in Kano context — women fear disclosure will imply infidelity
- Women may frame STI symptoms as "heat in the body," "smell from inside," or "something my husband gave me" without using STI language — UMMA must recognise symptomatic descriptions
- Men frequently resist STI diagnosis and treatment — "my wife is the one who is sick"
- In polygamous households, partner notification and treatment is complex — multiple co-wives may need treatment
- Religious context: unmarried women with STIs face additional stigma — UMMA must maintain absolute non-judgment
- HIV disclosure in pregnancy carries risk of domestic violence — UMMA should be aware that this is a sensitive safeguarding issue

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Women with multiple sexual partners or whose partners have multiple partners
- Women who are aware of partner STI history or infidelity
- Commercial sex workers (present in Kano — not openly acknowledged)
- HIV-positive women — increased susceptibility to all STIs
- Women with no or late ANC — missed screening opportunities
- Adolescent girls — biological susceptibility (ectropion) and power imbalance in relationships
- Women with prior STI history — high re-infection risk
