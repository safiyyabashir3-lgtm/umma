# DISEASE FILE: SICKLE CELL DISEASE — CRISES AND ACUTE COMPLICATIONS (CHILD)
**Category:** Child (0–5 years)
**Subcategory:** Haematological / Chronic disease acute presentations
**Version:** 1.0

---

## OVERVIEW

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is the most common severe genetic haemoglobin disorder in Nigeria and one of the most clinically significant chronic diseases affecting children in Kano and northwest Nigeria. Nigeria has the highest burden of SCD globally, with approximately 150,000–175,000 infants born with SCD annually. The most common and severe genotype in Nigeria is HbSS (homozygous sickle cell disease); clinically significant variants include HbSC and HbS/β-thalassaemia.

SCD is characterised by haemolytic anaemia and recurrent episodes of acute illness (crises) caused by vascular occlusion, haemolysis, and susceptibility to infection. In Kano, children with SCD face enormous disadvantages: neonatal screening is not routine, many children are not diagnosed until they present with a severe complication, families are often unaware of the diagnosis, and comprehensive SCD care facilities are limited.

**Epidemiology in Kano/northwest Nigeria:**
- Carrier frequency (HbAS — sickle cell trait) in Nigeria is 20–25% — approximately 1 in 4 Nigerians is a carrier
- HbSS prevalence: approximately 2–3% of all births in Nigeria have SCD; survival beyond 5 years without adequate care is poor
- In Kano and northern Nigeria, consanguinity patterns and lack of premarital genetic counselling perpetuate high rates of affected births
- Without prophylactic penicillin, pneumococcal vaccination, and folic acid, mortality in SCD under 5 is very high (estimated 50–80% of HbSS children die before age 5 in resource-poor settings without comprehensive care)
- Malaria is the most important precipitant of crises and mortality in SCD children in Kano — malaria chemoprophylaxis is critical but often not prescribed or not taken
- AKTH Kano has a haematology/SCD clinic that provides comprehensive care — a critical resource for UMMA to signpost families toward

**Types of acute crises relevant to UMMA:**
1. **Vaso-occlusive crisis (VOC) / painful crisis** — most common; microvascular occlusion causes severe pain
2. **Acute splenic sequestration crisis (ASSC)** — sudden massive splenic enlargement with pooling of red blood cells; potentially fatal within hours; most common under age 5
3. **Acute chest syndrome (ACS)** — vaso-occlusion in pulmonary vasculature; presents like severe pneumonia; life-threatening
4. **Aplastic crisis** — sudden fall in haemoglobin due to parvovirus B19 infection suppressing bone marrow; requires transfusion
5. **Haemolytic crisis** — accelerated haemolysis from infection or drugs; deepening jaundice and anaemia
6. **Stroke** — cerebral vascular occlusion; neurological emergency; occurs in 11% of HbSS children without transcranial Doppler screening
7. **Sepsis/overwhelming infection** — functional asplenia from early childhood; susceptible to encapsulated organisms (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Salmonella)
8. **Priapism** — painful prolonged erection in male children; rare under 5 but occurs

**Why it matters for UMMA:**
SCD crises are medical emergencies that require rapid recognition and immediate hospital referral. The window between crisis onset and death can be hours in ASSC and ACS. Many families manage crises at home with paracetamol and traditional remedies until the child is moribund. UMMA must be able to: identify when a known SCD child is in crisis, distinguish crisis types by symptoms, and convey life-threatening urgency clearly to mothers who may be fatigued by frequent crises and have barriers to hospital access. UMMA must also recognise undiagnosed SCD in a child presenting with chronic anaemia, recurrent pain, and swollen hands/feet.

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation (Uncomplicated Vaso-Occlusive Crisis — early)
- Pain in one or more body parts: commonly long bones (arms, legs), back, chest, or abdomen
- Pain may be dull to moderate, child is distressed but able to communicate
- Low-grade fever or no fever
- Child alert and interactive
- No respiratory distress
- Abdomen soft — no acute splenic enlargement detectable by mother
- Mild pallor at baseline (chronic anaemia)
- Mild jaundice at baseline (chronic haemolysis)
- Child is eating and drinking reasonably
- Previous episodes similar and resolved at home with analgesia and fluids
- No new neurological symptoms
- Crisis triggered by identifiable precipitant: cold weather (harmattan), dehydration, malaria, infection, fatigue

### Moderate Presentation (Severe VOC / Early Complications)
- Severe pain in multiple sites, not responding to paracetamol or ibuprofen at home
- Dactylitis in infant under 2: painful swelling of hands and feet (hand-foot syndrome) — often the first presentation of SCD in previously undiagnosed infants; symmetrical dorsal swelling, infant very irritable, will not let feet or hands be touched
- High fever (> 38.5°C) — any fever in SCD child is potentially serious (sepsis risk)
- Significant pallor beyond baseline — conjunctivae pale, lips pale
- Deepening jaundice beyond baseline
- Moderate tachycardia
- Child very distressed, crying continuously
- Poor feeding and oral intake
- Abdominal pain — spleen not yet clearly enlarged to mother's hand but child is tender
- Signs of evolving ACS: cough, breathing slightly faster than usual, but no marked respiratory distress yet
- Child unable to attend school or move about normally

### Severe Presentation (Life-Threatening Crises — any of the following)

**Acute Splenic Sequestration Crisis (ASSC):**
- Sudden rapid increase in spleen size — abdomen visibly distends, spleen palpable far below left costal margin, extending across midline
- Child becomes suddenly very pale (haemoglobin may fall from 7 to 2–3 g/dL within hours)
- Rapidly progressive weakness, lethargy, limpness
- Tachycardia — heart racing
- Restlessness, then collapse
- Distended, tense abdomen
- Little or no fever necessarily
- May have preceding upper respiratory infection
- MOST COMMON BETWEEN AGES 3 MONTHS AND 5 YEARS — UMMA'S TARGET AGE GROUP

**Acute Chest Syndrome (ACS):**
- Fever with new onset chest pain (child clutches chest, avoids deep breathing)
- Cough with increasing respiratory distress
- Rapid breathing (tachypnoea) — count: > 50 breaths/minute in infant, > 40 in toddler
- Oxygen desaturation — lips may appear bluish (central cyanosis)
- Looks very unwell with worsening pallor
- Cannot speak in full sentences (older children)
- May begin as "chest crisis" after bone pain — evolves rapidly

**Stroke:**
- Sudden onset of weakness or paralysis of one side (hemiplegia) — one arm or one leg not moving
- Sudden severe headache in older children (uncommon in under-5 to report)
- Facial drooping, difficulty speaking, slurred speech
- Seizures
- Loss of consciousness
- Sudden behavioural change, confusion, drowsiness
- Vision disturbance

**Overwhelming Sepsis:**
- High fever > 39°C or hypothermia in infant
- Child looking toxic — grey, mottled skin, sunken eyes, weak
- Rapid weak pulse
- Reduced consciousness
- In SCD: Salmonella species cause osteomyelitis and septicaemia; Streptococcus pneumoniae causes overwhelming sepsis and meningitis; both can kill within 12–24 hours
- Any fever in SCD child = assume sepsis until proven otherwise

**Aplastic Crisis:**
- Unusual pallor — much paler than the child's baseline
- Extreme fatigue, unable to walk or sit
- Heart racing (palpitations)
- Breathlessness even at rest
- Associated with rash illness (parvovirus B19 — slapped cheek disease); other family members may have had viral illness
- Little or no jaundice (unlike haemolytic crisis — haemolysis is reduced, not increased)

**Priapism (if older male child, occasionally relevant under 5):**
- Painful sustained erection > 4 hours
- Child distressed, cannot urinate
- Requires urgent urological management

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**SCD crisis vs Malaria in SCD child:**
- Malaria is the most common precipitant of crisis in SCD — RDT must be done for any febrile SCD child
- Malaria in SCD causes severe haemolysis, worsening anaemia, and can precipitate ASSC, ACS, and aplastic crisis
- These are not mutually exclusive — treat for both simultaneously at hospital
- UMMA rule: ANY FEVER in a known SCD child = refer to hospital immediately

**ASSC vs Gastroenteritis with abdominal distension:**
- Gastroenteritis: loose stools, vomiting, dehydration are the features; spleen not enlarged
- ASSC: sudden pallor, rapid abdominal distension, enlarged spleen on examination — no profuse diarrhoea as primary feature

**ACS vs Pneumonia:**
- Clinically very similar and often indistinguishable without imaging
- ACS: occurs in context of SCD, often follows bone crisis, chest pain prominent
- Pneumonia: infection in a non-SCD child is more likely plain pneumonia
- In SCD child: ACS and pneumonia are treated similarly in the acute phase — both require oxygen, IV antibiotics, analgesia, and hydration; both are emergencies

**Stroke vs Febrile seizures:**
- Febrile seizures: generalised, brief (< 5 minutes), with fever, child recovers fully within minutes, no focal deficit
- Stroke: focal neurological deficit (one-sided weakness), may have seizure but persistent deficit, may occur without fever

**SCD bone crisis vs Osteomyelitis:**
- Both cause bone pain and fever
- Osteomyelitis: fever more prominent, localised swelling, warmth, and tenderness over a specific bone; raised inflammatory markers; bone is infected
- SCD bone crisis: pain is from vascular occlusion in bone, may affect multiple sites, fever less prominent unless infected
- Salmonella osteomyelitis is specific to SCD children — multifocal, diaphyseal involvement, different from Staphylococcal osteomyelitis; diagnosis by blood culture and bone aspiration
- Clinically impossible to distinguish without investigation — treat as potential osteomyelitis with hospital referral if fever + bone pain in SCD child

**Dactylitis in undiagnosed infant:**
- Painful swollen hands and feet in an infant 6–24 months who has NOT been diagnosed with SCD should raise immediate suspicion
- Also consider septic arthritis, trauma, or lymphoedema — but SCD must be excluded urgently
- UMMA should recommend immediate clinic referral and haemoglobin electrophoresis for any infant with dactylitis

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria (home management with very close monitoring and low threshold to escalate)
- Known SCD child
- Single-site mild-to-moderate bone or limb pain
- Responsive to oral analgesia (paracetamol ± ibuprofen)
- No fever (temperature < 38°C)
- No pallor beyond baseline
- No abdominal distension or change in spleen size
- Eating, drinking, urinating normally
- No respiratory symptoms
- No neurological symptoms
- Previous similar crises managed successfully at home by family with medical guidance
- Family has emergency plan and can reach hospital within 1–2 hours if worsens

### Moderate criteria (needs hospital assessment same day)
- Pain not controlled by home analgesia within 2–4 hours
- Any fever ≥ 38°C in a known SCD child
- Dactylitis (hand-foot syndrome) in infant — especially first episode or if not tolerating fluids
- Deepening pallor or jaundice beyond the child's normal baseline
- Any new abdominal pain or spleen that feels larger than usual
- Moderate tachycardia
- Cough or mild chest symptoms in SCD child
- Child not drinking adequately (dehydration is a crisis precipitant and worsener)
- More than 2–3 days of persistent pain

### Severe criteria — ANY ONE = immediate emergency
- Rapidly enlarging spleen / sudden abdominal distension with pallor = ASSC (minutes count)
- Fever with toxic appearance, weak pulse, reduced consciousness = sepsis
- Chest pain with respiratory distress, fast breathing, low oxygen = ACS
- Any neurological symptom: weakness of limb, facial droop, speech change, seizure, loss of consciousness = stroke
- Extreme pallor far beyond baseline (aplastic crisis)
- Painful sustained erection > 4 hours
- Haemoglobin estimated very low: child extremely pale, breathless at rest, cannot sit up, heart racing

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild — UMMA advice
**Clinical approach:** Mild VOC in a known SCD child with an established care plan can be managed at home with strict instructions and very low threshold to escalate. UMMA must give clear escalation criteria.

- Increase oral fluids significantly — water, ORS, breast milk, soups. Dehydration worsens sickle crisis.
- Give paracetamol at the correct dose for weight — available at home if family is prepared
- Ibuprofen can also be used for pain if not otherwise contraindicated — advise taking with food
- Keep child warm — cold is a major trigger; avoid cold water baths, harmattan exposure, cold environments
- Rest the child — do not send to school or allow vigorous activity
- Do NOT give aspirin — aspirin is contraindicated in SCD children
- Ensure daily folic acid is continued — should always be on this if SCD is diagnosed
- Ensure the child is taking malaria chemoprophylaxis if prescribed (proguanil or chloroquine depending on clinic advice)
- Check temperature regularly — if fever develops (> 38°C), go to hospital immediately
- Check abdomen — if it feels swollen or hard, go to hospital immediately
- If pain not improving within 4 hours of analgesia, go to hospital

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child with sickle cell is having a mild pain crisis. Give paracetamol now and every 4–6 hours. Give lots of fluids — make sure they drink water or ORS often. Keep them warm and resting. Check their temperature — if it goes above 38°C, or if the tummy swells up, or the pain does not get better within 4 hours, go to the hospital immediately. Do not use aspirin. Keep giving folic acid and malaria prevention medicine as prescribed."

---

### Moderate — UMMA advice
**Clinical approach:** Fever, uncontrolled pain, dactylitis, or early ACS/ASSC features require same-day hospital assessment. Do not wait overnight.

- The child needs to go to a hospital TODAY — do not wait to see if things improve on their own.
- In Kano: ideally the SCD clinic at AKTH or the paediatric emergency unit of a general hospital.
- Bring the child's SCD records, medication list, and blood group card if available.
- Do not stop for patent medicine vendors — the child needs IV fluids, IV analgesia, and hospital assessment.
- Continue giving fluids on the way. Keep the child warm.
- If there is any fever: tell the hospital staff immediately that the child has sickle cell disease — this changes the urgency and management completely.
- If child is vomiting: small sips of fluid, keep upright.

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child needs to go to the hospital today — do not wait. Any fever in a sickle cell child is an emergency. The hospital needs to check the blood count, test for malaria, and give proper pain treatment. Bring any medicines and hospital records you have. Keep giving fluids on the way. Tell the doctor or nurse at the desk that your child has sickle cell disease as soon as you arrive."

---

### Severe — UMMA advice
**Clinical approach:** Any of the severe crisis types (ASSC, ACS, stroke, sepsis, aplastic crisis) are life-threatening emergencies with death possible within hours. Immediate tertiary hospital referral with maximum urgency.

- This is a life-threatening emergency. Go to AKTH or the nearest hospital with emergency paediatric services RIGHT NOW.
- Do not stop anywhere. Do not wait for morning. Do not try home treatment.
- **If the abdomen is suddenly swelling and the child is becoming very pale and weak**: this may be splenic sequestration — the child can die within hours. Call ahead to the hospital if possible: "My child has sickle cell disease and the spleen is suddenly enlarged — we are coming now."
- **If the child has a sudden weakness of one side, facial drooping, or is unconscious**: this may be a stroke — go immediately to the emergency unit. Time matters — every minute of delay means more brain damage.
- **If the child is struggling to breathe with chest pain**: oxygen and emergency treatment are needed now.
- **If the child has very high fever and looks collapsed**: sepsis in SCD kills within hours — the child needs IV antibiotics and fluids immediately.
- Keep the child warm during transport. If the child is unconscious, place on their side.
- If you are far from the hospital: call the hospital or any emergency contact you have. Do not delay.

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child is in serious danger right now. This is an emergency — you must go to the hospital emergency room immediately. Do not stop anywhere. If the belly is suddenly swelling and your child is very pale, or if your child cannot move one side of the body, or cannot breathe properly, or collapses — these are life-threatening emergencies. Call ahead if you can: tell them your child has sickle cell disease. Go now. Every minute matters."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous local practices:**
- Attributing SCD crises to spiritual causes: bone pain, pallor, jaundice, and swollen abdomen are frequently attributed to witchcraft, evil eye (malocchio / iska), or ancestral curse — especially if the child has repeated hospitalisations. Families may seek spiritual healers first. UMMA should respectfully acknowledge family distress while making medical urgency clear.
- Herbal treatments for SCD: numerous traditional preparations are used in Nigeria for SCD, some marketed specifically for "sickle cell" (e.g., Niprisan/Nicosan has some evidence but others do not). Potentially nephrotoxic or hepatotoxic herbs delay access to emergency care.
- Stopping prescribed medications: folic acid, penicillin prophylaxis, and antimalarial prophylaxis are frequently stopped when the child "seems well" — UMMA should reinforce daily compliance
- Cold water bathing (a cultural norm): applying cold water baths for fever in SCD children precipitates and worsens crises through vasoconstriction. Tepid water only.
- Aspirin use: aspirin is used for fever in some Nigerian households — absolutely contraindicated in SCD (reduces platelet function and increases sickling complications)
- Hydroxyurea non-uptake: hydroxyurea significantly reduces crisis frequency and severity — available at AKTH but severely underused due to cost, fear of side effects, and lack of awareness

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Blood transfusion: ASSC and aplastic crisis require urgent blood transfusion — blood bank availability at AKTH is variable; families may need to donate blood or source family donors urgently. UMMA should advise families to know their child's blood group and identify family donors in advance.
- Transcranial Doppler screening for stroke risk: available only at AKTH and selected centres; many at-risk children are not screened
- Neonatal screening for SCD: not routine in Kano — most children are diagnosed after a crisis event (first dactylitis, severe anaemia, or sepsis). UMMA should note when a child has clinical features suggesting undiagnosed SCD.
- Exchange transfusion for stroke: limited availability even at AKTH; simple top-up transfusion is often the only option
- Hydroxyurea supply and monitoring: available at AKTH SCD clinic but requires regular full blood count monitoring — access challenges for families outside Kano city
- 24-hour paediatric emergency services: inconsistent at general hospital level; AKTH emergency is the most reliable option

**Cultural factors:**
- Consanguinity and family reputation: parents of SCD children often feel shame or blame — mothers particularly blamed. UMMA should approach SCD topics with sensitivity and non-judgemental language.
- Genetic counselling uptake: very low in Kano. Many families have multiple SCD-affected children without understanding carrier status. UMMA should gently introduce the concept of genetic carrier status and encourage carrier testing for parents and older siblings.
- Gender and decision-making: fathers may minimise the child's pain ("children always cry") and resist repeated hospital visits due to cost. UMMA may need to frame urgency in terms of permanent disability or death to overcome dismissal.
- Fatalism regarding SCD: some families believe SCD children are "not meant to live long" — an Abiku or Ogbanje belief (spirit child who returns to die repeatedly). UMMA should challenge this compassionately with evidence that SCD children with good care live long lives.

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Infants 3–18 months with SCD: highest risk period for ASSC and first bacterial sepsis — often not yet diagnosed; UMMA should flag any infant with pallor, jaundice, recurrent swollen hands/feet, or recurrent severe illness for haemoglobin electrophoresis
- Children not on penicillin prophylaxis: until age 5 years, daily phenoxymethylpenicillin (penicillin V) dramatically reduces mortality from pneumococcal sepsis — many SCD children in Kano are not on prophylaxis
- Children not vaccinated: pneumococcal vaccine (PCV13) and Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccine are critical for SCD children; immunisation gaps are common
- Children during harmattan: cold, dry, dusty conditions are a major crisis trigger; UMMA should pre-empt increased crisis frequency with counselling
- Children with concurrent malaria: malaria is the dominant precipitant of severe crises including ASSC — malaria chemoprophylaxis and prompt testing and treatment of fever is the single most impactful preventive intervention in this context
- Children in families with low SCD literacy: parents who do not understand the disease, its complications, or the danger signs cannot act quickly enough in emergencies — UMMA's educational role is critical
