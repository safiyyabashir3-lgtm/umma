# DISEASE FILE: MALNUTRITION (ACUTE AND CHRONIC) — CHILD
**Category:** Child (0–5 years)
**Subcategory:** Nutritional / Systemic
**Version:** 1.0
**Context:** Kano and northwest Nigeria

---

## OVERVIEW

Malnutrition remains one of the leading causes of under-5 mortality in Nigeria. Northwest Nigeria — including Kano, Zamfara, Sokoto, and Kebbi states — has among the highest rates of acute malnutrition in the country and globally. SMART surveys have consistently placed GAM (Global Acute Malnutrition) rates in northwest Nigeria above the 15% emergency threshold.

Key forms relevant to UMMA:
- **Severe Acute Malnutrition (SAM):** Life-threatening; two forms: kwashiorkor (oedematous) and marasmus (wasting); requires CMAM (Community-based Management of Acute Malnutrition) or inpatient stabilisation
- **Moderate Acute Malnutrition (MAM):** Significant wasting without bilateral pitting oedema; managed with RUSF/supplementary feeding
- **Chronic malnutrition / stunting:** Low height-for-age; widespread; indicates long-term deficiency but not acute emergency
- **Micronutrient deficiency:** Often coexists — especially vitamin A, iron, zinc, iodine

UMMA's population is particularly at risk because: early cessation of breastfeeding; inappropriate complementary feeding (thin gruels with low nutrient density); high infectious disease burden; food insecurity; and poor maternal nutritional knowledge. Malnutrition and infection are mutually reinforcing — malnutrition increases infection susceptibility, and infection worsens nutritional status.

Common delays and barriers:
- Early signs normalised ("children are always thin")
- Kwashiorkor attributed to "pregnancy disease" (mother's next pregnancy causing illness in existing child)
- Traditional foods for ill children are often low-calorie
- CMAM sites have variable coverage; some families travel long distances
- Families discharged from CMAM may not complete full course

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation (MAM / Early SAM)
- Visibly thin child; prominent ribs; reduced muscle bulk
- Weight-for-height Z-score -2 to -3 (MAM); or MUAC 11.5–12.5 cm
- Still alert, responsive, and able to eat
- Hair may be slightly sparse or discoloured
- Child may have mild irritability
- Growth faltering — mother notices child not gaining weight or losing weight
- No bilateral pitting oedema
- No significant appetite loss

### Moderate Presentation (SAM — marasmus)
- Severe wasting: "old man" appearance; skin hanging loosely; visible wasting of buttocks ("baggy pants" sign)
- MUAC <11.5 cm
- Weight-for-height Z-score <-3
- Prominent bones throughout
- Irritable or apathetic
- Hair thin, sparse, easily plucked
- Reduced appetite but still able to take RUTF if offered
- May have complications developing: secondary infections, hypothermia, hypoglycaemia

### Severe Presentation (SAM with complications — marasmic-kwashiorkor / kwashiorkor / SAM with medical complication)
- **Kwashiorkor:** Bilateral pitting oedema of feet (press thumb to dorsum of foot — pit remains ≥3 seconds); may extend to legs, hands, face ("moon face"); skin changes (hyperpigmentation, desquamation, "flaky paint" skin); hair depigmentation (flag sign — bands of pale and dark hair); miserable affect; distended abdomen
- **Medical complications of SAM:**
  - Anorexia (child refuses RUTF — key sign of complicated SAM)
  - Pneumonia (fast breathing, chest indrawing — presents silently in SAM due to impaired immune/inflammatory response)
  - Severe anaemia (pallor of palms, conjunctivae, lips)
  - Hypoglycaemia (lethargy, cold, sweating, seizures — often without classic signs in SAM)
  - Hypothermia (axillary temperature <35°C — common in Kano evenings/harmattan)
  - Sepsis (often without fever — hypothermia or normothermia is the pattern)
  - Severe dehydration (difficult to assess in SAM — skin turgor unreliable; use history of diarrhoea/vomiting)
  - Eyes: bilateral corneal ulceration from vitamin A deficiency — Bitot's spots, xerophthalmia, corneal clouding
  - Altered consciousness, seizures

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**From normal variation in weight:**
- Normal thin child: appropriate weight-for-height, active, feeding well, growing on curve
- MAM/SAM: below anthropometric thresholds; growth faltering documented over time; reduced activity

**From oedema of other causes:**
- Kwashiorkor oedema: bilateral, pitting, starts in feet, associated with wasting and skin/hair changes
- Nephrotic syndrome: oedema may be more widespread; periorbital oedema prominent; frothy urine; less severe wasting
- Cardiac failure: rare under 5 in this context; signs of heart disease; hepatomegaly; elevated JVP

**From HIV wasting:**
- HIV wasting: chronic progressive; often with recurrent infections, oral candida, chronic diarrhoea; lymphadenopathy
- All SAM children should be considered for HIV testing — coexistence common and prognosis worse

**From intestinal malabsorption (e.g. persistent diarrhoea syndrome):**
- Persistent diarrhoea >14 days with failure to thrive
- EAEC, Giardia, Cryptosporidium important in Nigerian context
- Can cause and worsen SAM — treat both

**Tuberculosis:**
- TB causes weight loss, failure to thrive, chronic cough, night sweats
- TB must be excluded in any child with SAM not responding to CMAM treatment
- Northwest Nigeria has significant TB burden; TB-malnutrition coexistence common

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria (MAM)
- MUAC 11.5–12.5 cm
- Weight-for-height Z-score -2 to -3
- No bilateral pitting oedema
- Alert, able to eat
- No medical complications

### Moderate criteria (uncomplicated SAM)
- MUAC <11.5 cm OR weight-for-height Z-score <-3
- No bilateral pitting oedema
- Alert and able to eat RUTF (appetite test passed)
- No medical complications (no pneumonia, no dehydration, no severe anaemia, no altered consciousness)
- Eligible for outpatient CMAM (OTP) management

### Severe criteria — any ONE = inpatient/emergency referral
- Bilateral pitting oedema (any grade — including +1)
- Anorexia: child refuses RUTF/food (failed appetite test)
- Any of: fast breathing, chest indrawing, severe pallor, altered consciousness, hypothermia, hypoglycaemia signs, severe dehydration
- Convulsions
- Corneal clouding or ulceration (vitamin A deficiency emergency)
- Child not improving after 2 weeks in OTP
- Any medical complication requiring IV treatment

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild (MAM)
UMMA should advise:
- Refer to nearest CMAM supplementary feeding programme (SFP) or nutrition facility for MUAC measurement and enrolment
- Advise on age-appropriate complementary feeding — energy-dense foods available locally (groundnuts, bean flour, eggs, fish, full-fat milk/fura)
- Continue breastfeeding if under 2 years
- Address feeding frequency — young children need 5–6 small meals per day
- Treat intercurrent infections promptly
- Follow up monthly for MUAC monitoring
- Vitamin A supplementation if not received in last 6 months

**Plain language for UMMA:** "Your child's arm measurement shows they are not getting enough food and nutrition. You should take them to the nearest nutrition clinic or health centre to be registered for a feeding programme. While you are waiting, try to give small amounts of food more often through the day — foods like groundnut paste, eggs, bean porridge, or small fish are very good. Keep breastfeeding if your child is still young enough. This will not get better on its own."

### Moderate (Uncomplicated SAM — eligible for OTP)
UMMA should advise:
- Register immediately at nearest OTP (Outpatient Therapeutic Programme) — RUTF (Plumpy'Nut or equivalent) is the treatment; it is provided free at CMAM sites
- Weekly or bi-weekly clinic visits required
- Give RUTF as prescribed — do not share with other family members or dilute
- Continue breastfeeding alongside RUTF
- Give clean water alongside RUTF
- Return immediately if child refuses RUTF, develops swollen feet, has difficulty breathing, or deteriorates
- Complete the full course — many families stop when child "looks better"

**Plain language for UMMA:** "Your child has serious malnutrition and needs treatment called RUTF — a special peanut-based food in a sachet that will help them recover. You need to register at the nutrition clinic immediately. This treatment is free. The clinic will see you every week. Give the RUTF exactly as told, keep breastfeeding, and give clean water. If the child refuses to eat the RUTF, their feet swell, or they have difficulty breathing, take them to the hospital the same day."

### Severe (SAM with complications)
UMMA should advise:
- This child needs inpatient hospital care immediately — do not go to OTP
- Go directly to a stabilisation centre (SC) or hospital with a paediatric ward
- In Kano: AKTH, Murtala Mohammed Specialist Hospital, or the nearest hospital with a nutrition stabilisation centre
- SAM with complications can be fatal within hours — hypothermia, hypoglycaemia, and sepsis are silent killers
- Do not attempt to feed child at home if unconscious or very weak
- Keep child warm during transport — wrap in cloth, skin-to-skin if infant

**Plain language for UMMA:** "Your child is critically ill with malnutrition and needs hospital treatment immediately. Please go to the hospital now — not the local clinic, but a proper hospital with a children's ward. Do not try to treat this at home. Wrap your child warmly during the journey. This is an emergency."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Thin gruel (kunu, pap) as main complementary food — low calorie density; giving large volumes of water with gruel worsens malnutrition
- "Removing" oedema by restricting fluids — dangerous; kwashiorkor management requires careful fluid balance
- Herbal purgatives given to "cleanse" malnourished children — cause electrolyte disturbances and diarrhoea
- Attributing kwashiorkor to "ogbanje" or pregnancy of mother ("pregnancy disease") rather than nutritional deficiency
- Sharing RUTF within the household — reduces therapeutic dose to child
- Stopping CMAM treatment when child "looks better" at 4 weeks — relapse common if full course not completed

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- CMAM OTP sites have variable coverage — some communities have none within 10 km
- RUTF supply disruptions are common — families may arrive at clinic to find RUTF out of stock
- Stabilisation centres (inpatient) limited to tertiary and some secondary facilities
- Vitamin A supplementation campaigns have gaps in coverage
- Zinc and iron supplementation for SAM management protocols not always followed at PHC level

**Cultural factors:**
- Malnutrition in children often normalised or attributed to sibling jealousy when new pregnancy begins (the "disease of the deposed child" — Hausa: "kurna")
- MUAC measurement may be refused by some families as a "bad omen"
- Fathers often control food purchase decisions — mother's nutritional advice may not translate to household behaviour without paternal engagement
- During Ramadan, complementary feeding schedules may be disrupted
- Purdah (seclusion) limits mothers' ability to attend nutrition clinics — male escort required by some families

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Children 6–24 months (peak vulnerability — transitioning from exclusive breastfeeding to complementary foods)
- Children in polygamous households (resource competition)
- Twins and higher-order multiples
- Children of adolescent mothers
- Children with frequent diarrhoeal illness (common in Kano rainy season)
- Displaced populations and households affected by insecurity (northwest Nigeria)
- Children with HIV, TB, or SCD (all worsen nutritional status)
- Post-measles children (measles causes profound nutritional depletion)
