# CHORIOAMNIONITIS — PREGNANCY DISEASE FILE
## For UMMA AI — Maternal & Child Health Companion (Kano/Northwest Nigeria Context)

---

## OVERVIEW

Chorioamnionitis (also called intraamniotic infection or IAI) is infection of the chorion, amnion, amniotic fluid, placenta, and/or decidua during pregnancy. It is predominantly a bacterial ascending infection from the lower genital tract, most commonly after prolonged rupture of membranes. In Nigerian context, chorioamnionitis is a major but underrecognised cause of maternal sepsis and neonatal sepsis/death, frequently occurring in the context of prolonged labour, preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), and repeated vaginal examinations by non-sterile hands.

Chorioamnionitis is clinically difficult to diagnose — it is often presumed and treated on clinical criteria. The classic triad (fever, uterine tenderness, foul-smelling liquor) may be incomplete, and many cases in Nigerian facilities are diagnosed late or post-delivery when maternal or neonatal sepsis declares.

**Kano/Northwest Nigeria epidemiology:**
- Chorioamnionitis complicates 1–4% of all pregnancies globally but rates are significantly higher in Nigerian context due to:
  - High rates of PPROM
  - Prolonged labour, particularly with obstructed labour
  - Multiple vaginal examinations by TBAs and healthcare workers with non-sterile technique
  - Undetected and untreated lower genital tract infections (BV, trichomonas, gonorrhoea, GBS)
  - Malnutrition and immunosuppression (HIV, malaria-related immune dysfunction)
- Group B Streptococcus (GBS) carriage in Nigerian pregnant women: approximately 20–30%
- Gram-negative organisms (E. coli, Klebsiella) prominent in Nigerian IAI — reflect local flora and resistance patterns
- Neonatal early-onset sepsis from chorioamnionitis is a leading cause of neonatal death in Nigeria
- Maternal sepsis from chorioamnionitis carries significant mortality when antibiotics are delayed

**Why it matters for UMMA:**
- UMMA must recognise fever in labour or after membrane rupture as potentially chorioamnionitis — not normalise it
- Women with prolonged rupture of membranes (>18 hours) need urgent facility assessment regardless of symptoms
- Fever in postpartum period may represent untreated chorioamnionitis progressing to endometritis or sepsis
- Neonatal consequences are severe — recognising maternal chorioamnionitis helps ensure neonate is assessed and treated after birth
- Foul-smelling vaginal discharge or liquor is a key danger sign UMMA should screen for

**Common delays and barriers:**
- Fever in labour attributed to "too much heat," dehydration, or hard work of labour — not recognised as infection
- Foul-smelling discharge normalised or attributed to hygiene
- TBA deliveries: chorioamnionitis often goes unrecognised until maternal collapse or sick neonate
- Antibiotic therapy delayed at facility due to cost, stock-outs, or late diagnosis
- Transfer from PHC to secondary facility delayed — often occurs after maternal deterioration
- Postpartum recognition: women discharged after delivery without evaluation of intrapartum fever; neonatal sepsis may be the presenting manifestation

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation (Early/subclinical chorioamnionitis)
- Prolonged rupture of membranes (PROM: rupture at term >18 hours before delivery; PPROM: rupture before 37 weeks)
- Mild low-grade fever (37.8–38.0°C)
- Mild uterine discomfort or tenderness beyond normal labour
- Vaginal discharge with slightly unpleasant odour but not frankly foul
- Fetal tachycardia >160 bpm (if monitored) — often the earliest sign
- Maternal mild tachycardia (>100 bpm)
- Liquor clear to slightly cloudy but draining
- No signs of maternal systemic compromise
- Often subclinical — woman may not report symptoms, discovered on examination

### Moderate Presentation (Clinical chorioamnionitis)
- Temperature ≥38.0°C in labour or after membrane rupture — measured OR reported as feeling very hot
- Uterine tenderness on palpation — pain between contractions, uterus tender to touch
- Maternal tachycardia >100 bpm
- Foul-smelling vaginal discharge or amniotic fluid — woman, birth attendant, or family notices bad smell "down there"
- Purulent or cloudy amniotic fluid draining (green, brown, or turbid)
- Fetal tachycardia >160 bpm sustained
- Abdominal pain not consistent with contractions — constant rather than intermittent
- Maternal malaise, rigors, headache
- Labour may be dysfunctional — contractions present but no progress (chorioamnionitis impairs myometrial function)
- Signs of dehydration (dry mouth, reduced urine output)

### Severe Presentation (Severe chorioamnionitis / Maternal sepsis)
- High fever ≥39°C or temperature instability (hypothermia can occur in severe sepsis)
- Signs of sepsis: cold clammy extremities, rapid weak pulse, hypotension, confusion
- Severe uterine tenderness — abdomen rigid or guarded
- Foul purulent offensive liquor
- Rigors
- Altered consciousness, confusion, extreme lethargy
- Rapidly progressing labour dysfunction — uterus not contracting despite prolonged membrane rupture
- Peritonitis signs if uterine rupture or uterine necrosis (rare but catastrophic)
- Fetal: absent fetal movements, fetal tachycardia >180 bpm or bradycardia (late sign of fetal distress)
- Postpartum: fever within 24 hours of delivery, uterine tenderness, offensive lochia, failure to contract uterus (endometritis/sepsis)
- Neonatal indicators: neonate born flat, requiring resuscitation, or develops fever, poor feeding, respiratory distress within first 24–48 hours

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Chorioamnionitis vs. Urinary tract infection/Pyelonephritis:**
- Pyelonephritis: loin/costovertebral angle tenderness, dysuria, urine offensive — uterine tenderness less prominent
- Chorioamnionitis: uterine tenderness prominent, timing related to membrane rupture or prolonged labour
- Both cause fever in pregnancy — can co-exist; UTI is a risk factor for chorioamnionitis
- Urine and vaginal examination findings help distinguish — facility assessment needed

**Chorioamnionitis vs. Normal labour:**
- Normal labour: afebrile, uterus relaxes between contractions, liquor clear and odourless
- Chorioamnionitis: fever, persistent uterine tenderness, offensive liquor, fetal or maternal tachycardia

**Chorioamnionitis vs. Malaria:**
- Malaria: fever with rigors, headache, no uterine tenderness, positive RDT
- Chorioamnionitis: temporal relationship to membrane rupture or prolonged labour, uterine tenderness, offensive discharge
- CRITICAL: Both can co-exist — malaria increases susceptibility to infection; fever in labour or near labour with membrane rupture should prompt consideration of both

**Chorioamnionitis vs. Preeclampsia/HELLP:**
- Preeclampsia: hypertension, proteinuria, no fever (unless postictal)
- Chorioamnionitis: fever dominant, uterine tenderness, no hypertension from infection alone
- HELLP: epigastric pain, jaundice, thrombocytopenic bleeding — distinct from chorioamnionitis though both cause severe illness

**Chorioamnionitis vs. Appendicitis in pregnancy:**
- Appendicitis: right-sided pain (may be displaced upward in third trimester), no fever from membrane rupture history, no offensive discharge
- Chorioamnionitis: bilateral or generalised uterine tenderness, related to membrane rupture, offensive liquor

**Postpartum endometritis vs. Chorioamnionitis:**
- Postpartum endometritis is the continuation/consequence of untreated intrapartum chorioamnionitis
- Features: fever within 24–48 hours postpartum, uterine subinvolution (uterus not shrinking), offensive lochia, uterine tenderness
- Distinguished from normal postpartum lochia: normal lochia is blood-stained to pink and odourless or faintly iron-smelling; endometritis lochia is offensive, purulent

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Prolonged ROM (>18 hours) without fever or offensive discharge
- Low-grade temperature 37.8–38.0°C with no other features
- Mild uterine discomfort only
- Normal maternal heart rate and fetal heart rate
- Clear (not offensive) liquor draining

### Moderate criteria
- Fever ≥38.0°C in context of membrane rupture or prolonged labour
- Uterine tenderness beyond normal contractions
- Foul-smelling vaginal discharge or liquor
- Maternal tachycardia >100 bpm
- Fetal tachycardia >160 bpm
- Any fever in PPROM at <34 weeks (urgency of preterm delivery management + infection)
- Labour dysfunction with offensive signs

### Severe criteria — ANY ONE = emergency
- Fever ≥39°C with membrane rupture or in labour
- Signs of maternal sepsis: confusion, cold extremities, hypotension, very rapid weak pulse
- Rigors with abdominal tenderness
- Offensive purulent liquor with maternal systemic compromise
- Absent fetal movements or abnormal fetal heart rate
- Postpartum fever with confusion, abdominal rigidity, or haemodynamic compromise
- Neonate delivered showing signs of sepsis (born flat, respiratory distress, fever)

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
**Clinical actions:**
- PROM at term without fever: facility assessment same day — labour induction at or after 18 hours of rupture is standard
- PPROM: tertiary/secondary facility immediately — preterm delivery and infection management required
- Mild temperature: facility attendance urgently — do not manage at home
- Advise: no vaginal intercourse after membrane rupture, no vaginal douching, keep perineum clean with clean water only
- Count fetal movements regularly

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your waters have broken [or: you have a slight fever after your waters broke] — this means you need to go to the health centre or hospital today. When the bag of waters breaks, there is a risk of infection that can harm you and your baby. Even if you feel well, go now. Keep yourself clean but do not put anything inside your vagina. Count your baby's movements. If you develop a fever, your discharge smells bad, or your baby stops moving — go to the hospital immediately."

### Moderate
**Clinical actions:**
- Immediate facility admission — secondary level minimum
- IV antibiotics required (ampicillin + gentamicin is standard; metronidazole added for anaerobic cover where available)
- Delivery required — antibiotic treatment without delivery does not cure chorioamnionitis
- Fetal monitoring continuous where available
- Paracetamol for fever — does not mask infection signs adequately but reduces hyperthermia
- Neonatal sepsis team alerted for delivery — neonate will require sepsis workup and empirical antibiotics
- Do not delay delivery for antibiotic loading — both proceed simultaneously

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"You have signs of infection around your baby. This is serious and your baby needs to be delivered and you both need antibiotic treatment in hospital. Please go now — do not wait. Tell the hospital that you have a fever and your waters have broken and there is a bad smell. They will give you antibiotics by drip and help deliver your baby safely. Your baby will also be checked for infection after birth."

### Severe
**Clinical actions:**
- Life-threatening emergency — maternal sepsis
- Immediate tertiary referral (AKTH or FMC Kano)
- IV antibiotics at first available facility — do not delay for transport
- IV fluid resuscitation for septic shock en route if IV access available
- Delivery of fetus required urgently — caesarean section may be necessary
- Left lateral positioning for unconscious patient
- Airway management for confused/unconscious patient
- Postpartum: same sepsis management applies — new-onset fever, offensive lochia, confusion in postpartum woman
- Neonatal resuscitation team should be prepared at receiving facility

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"This is a life-threatening emergency. The infection has spread and she is very sick. Go to AKTH or a big hospital right now. Do not stop anywhere else for treatment — go directly to the big hospital. If she is confused or cannot walk, carry her on her left side. Call the hospital ahead if you can to say you are coming with a woman with infection in labour. Go immediately."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Multiple vaginal examinations by TBAs and multiple birth attendants with unsterile technique — most important risk factor for chorioamnionitis in Nigerian context; UMMA should advise against multiple VEs by non-sterile hands
- Vaginal herbal preparations and vaginal steam baths used to "clean" or "prepare" the vagina for labour — directly introduce infection
- Tight binding of abdomen after membrane rupture — traditional practice to "keep baby in" — does not prevent preterm delivery and delays recognition of complications
- Oral or rectal herbal uterotonic preparations — contain potential pathogens and cause gut flora disruption
- Delaying facility transfer because "the baby has not come yet" — chorioamnionitis with prolonged rupture is the scenario where this delay is most lethal
- Withholding water from febrile labouring women — worsens sepsis physiology

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Blood cultures not available at PHC or most secondary facilities — diagnosis is clinical
- IV antibiotics (ampicillin, gentamicin) may not be stocked at PHC — PHC care is supportive only; secondary facility required
- Neonatal ICU: neonates with early-onset sepsis from chorioamnionitis require NICU — only AKTH and FMC in Kano
- Caesarean section: required for many severe chorioamnionitis cases — only available at General Hospital level and above
- Continuous fetal monitoring (CTG): not available at most facilities in Kano; clinical fetal heart rate auscultation is the standard
- Laboratory: WBC, CRP, blood cultures — variable availability; clinical criteria must guide management

**Cultural factors:**
- Offensive vaginal discharge and odour are sources of extreme shame — women may hide these symptoms from family and health workers
- Fever in labour attributed to "too much effort" or "the struggle of labour" — not recognised as a warning sign
- Postpartum fever in first 40 days: women confined at home, not expected to return to facility; maternal sepsis can present as "weakness" or "not recovering well" — UMMA must actively screen postpartum women for fever and offensive lochia
- TBA practice of multiple vaginal examinations: deeply embedded practice; families trust TBA assessment; UMMA cannot simply condemn TBA — must redirect to facility while respecting community relationships
- Neonatal illness attributed to spiritual causes ("evil eye," "jinn") in first days of life — delays recognition of neonatal sepsis from chorioamnionitis

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Women with PPROM at any gestation — infection universal if membranes ruptured >24 hours
- Women in prolonged obstructed labour
- Women with prior or current lower genital tract infections (BV, trichomonas, gonorrhoea)
- HIV-positive women — impaired immune response to ascending infection
- Women with multiple vaginal examinations by multiple attendants (TBA + community + PHC)
- Malnourished women — impaired immune function
- Grand multiparae with prior pelvic infections
- Adolescent girls — cervical ectropion increases susceptibility to ascending infection
- Women with sickle cell disease — increased infection susceptibility and severity
