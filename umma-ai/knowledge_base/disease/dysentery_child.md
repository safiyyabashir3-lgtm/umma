# DYSENTERY — CHILD
## Disease File for UMMA AI | Maternal and Child Health | Kano/Northwest Nigeria Context

---

## OVERVIEW

Dysentery — defined as diarrhoea with visible blood and/or mucus in the stool — is common in children under 5 in Kano and northwest Nigeria. The predominant cause in this population is Shigella species (bacterial dysentery), particularly Shigella flexneri and Shigella sonnei. Entamoeba histolytica (amoebic dysentery) also occurs but is less common in under-5s than Shigella. Other causes include Campylobacter and Salmonella non-typhi.

In Kano, bacterial dysentery peaks in the hot dry season (April–June) and early rains when contamination of water supplies and food is high. Children are exposed through contaminated water, unwashed hands, contaminated weaning foods, and communal eating practices. Shigella dysentery can cause severe systemic illness including haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS), severe hyponatraemia, seizures, and toxic megacolon — these life-threatening complications are often not recognised at community level.

UMMA relevance: Bloody diarrhoea in a child is never a minor symptom — it always requires clinical assessment. Families frequently attribute bloody stool to teething, worms, or "heat in the body" and delay seeking care. Early antibiotic treatment of Shigella significantly reduces complications and death.

Common delays:
- Blood in stool attributed to "worm" (tsutsaye) — herbal worm treatments given first
- Antidiarrhoeals given from PMV — dangerous in dysentery
- Families wait to see if it resolves — Shigella can deteriorate rapidly
- Amoebic and bacterial dysentery require different antibiotics — incorrect treatment is common

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
- Small amounts of blood or blood-streaked mucus mixed with loose stool
- 3–5 stools per day
- Low-grade fever or no fever
- Mild crampy lower abdominal pain
- Child is alert, feeding (though appetite reduced), and hydrated
- No tenesmus (straining) in young children — older children may complain of pain with stooling
- No systemic signs of toxicity
- Child can drink and retain fluids

### Moderate Presentation
- Frequent bloody/mucoid stools — 6 or more per day
- Visible blood in stool — not just streaking but frank blood
- Fever (38–39°C), child appears unwell and irritable
- Abdominal cramps visible — child draws up legs, cries with stool passage
- Tenesmus in older children (straining and pain at end of stooling)
- Mild to moderate dehydration: sunken eyes, dry mouth, reduced urine output
- Reduced appetite and oral intake
- Child consolable but clearly sick

### Severe Presentation
- Very frequent bloody stools — continuous or more than 10 per day
- High fever (above 39°C) or hypothermia (below 36°C — sign of sepsis)
- Severe dehydration
- Altered mental status: lethargy, confusion, reduced responsiveness
- Seizures (Shigella toxin can cause encephalopathy/febrile seizures — "Ekiri syndrome" in severe cases)
- Signs of septicaemia: cold peripheries, rapid weak pulse, pallor, mottled skin
- Abdominal distension with reduced or absent bowel sounds (toxic megacolon)
- Severe pallor — haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS): pallor + bloody diarrhoea + reduced urine = emergency
- Rectal prolapse (especially in malnourished children with repeated straining)
- Child refusing all oral intake and too weak to cry properly

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

Shigella vs Amoebic dysentery:
- Shigella: acute onset, high fever, systemic toxicity, mucoid-bloody stool, tenesmus, more common in under-5s, epidemics
- Amoeba: more gradual onset, less fever, stool with "anchovy paste" appearance or bloody mucus, less systemic toxicity initially, more common in older children and adults, travel/rural water exposure
- In practice, both can look similar — clinical differentiation difficult without stool microscopy; treatment differs (Shigella: ciprofloxacin or azithromycin; Amoeba: metronidazole)

Dysentery vs Intussusception:
- Intussusception: "redcurrant jelly" stool — blood and mucus but without faecal matter, colicky severe abdominal pain, child draws up legs and screams then becomes limp between episodes, palpable abdominal mass, no fever initially
- Dysentery: faecal matter mixed with blood, fever prominent, continuous symptoms
- CRITICAL: intussusception is a surgical emergency — must not be mistaken for dysentery

Dysentery vs Cholera:
- Cholera: rice-water watery stool without blood, profuse volumes, rapid dehydration, no fever, no abdominal cramps
- Dysentery: bloody/mucoid stool, fever, cramping
- Mixed picture: some children in endemic areas have mixed infections

Dysentery vs Salmonella non-typhi:
- Similar presentation to Shigella — bloody diarrhoea, fever, systemic illness
- Cannot be distinguished clinically — both require antibiotics, stool culture differentiates

Dysentery vs Haemolytic Uraemic Syndrome (HUS) — complication of Shigella:
- HUS triad: bloody diarrhoea + pallor (haemolytic anaemia) + reduced urine output (renal failure)
- Child looks severely pale beyond what dehydration explains
- Requires urgent tertiary care

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild Criteria
- Blood-streaked stool, fewer than 5 stools per day
- No or low-grade fever
- Child alert, drinking, and hydrated
- No abdominal distension
- No systemic signs of toxicity

### Moderate Criteria
- Frankly bloody stools, 6 or more per day
- Fever 38–39°C
- Mild to moderate dehydration
- Abdominal cramps and tenesmus
- Child unwell but conscious and can drink
- Appetite significantly reduced

### Severe Criteria — ANY ONE = EMERGENCY
- High fever (above 39°C) or hypothermia
- Altered consciousness, seizures, or encephalopathy
- Signs of septicaemia or shock
- Severe dehydration
- Abdominal distension with absent bowel sounds (toxic megacolon)
- Severe pallor + bloody diarrhoea + reduced urine (HUS)
- Rectal prolapse
- Child unable to drink at all
- Age under 6 months with any bloody diarrhoea
- Known or suspected intussusception (immediate surgical referral)

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- Child needs to be seen at a health centre — bloody stool always needs clinical assessment
- ORS should be started immediately to maintain hydration
- Do not give antidiarrhoeals (Flagyl alone is not appropriate for Shigella — ciprofloxacin or azithromycin is needed, prescribed by a clinician)
- Do not give herbal remedies for "worm" — this delays treatment
- Continue breastfeeding
- Zinc supplementation if available
- If assigned an antibiotic, complete the full course even if stool improves in 2 days — incomplete treatment causes resistance

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"Blood in your child's stool is always something a doctor or nurse needs to check. Please take your child to the health centre today. In the meantime, give ORS (oral rehydration salts) to make sure your child does not get dry. Do not give Flagyl or any herbal medicine yet — the right medicine depends on what is causing this, and the nurse will decide. This is not from worms."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Urgent same-day health centre or hospital visit
- Continue ORS during transport
- At facility: clinical assessment, stool examination where possible, antibiotic prescription (expect ciprofloxacin or azithromycin depending on age and local protocol)
- Rehydration with ORS or IV if needed
- Monitor for signs of deterioration: seizures, worsening pallor, abdominal distension
- Zinc for 10–14 days
- If no improvement within 48 hours of antibiotics, return to facility — may indicate amoebic dysentery or resistant organism

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"Your child is having many stools with blood and has a fever. This needs treatment today. Please go to the health centre or hospital now. Keep giving ORS on the way. Tell the doctor or nurse about the blood in the stool and the fever. Your child needs the right antibiotic — please do not buy medicine from a chemist before seeing the doctor."

### Severe
UMMA should advise:
- Emergency — go to hospital immediately
- If child is having fits or is unconscious — do not wait
- At hospital: IV access, fluid resuscitation, IV antibiotics, neurological monitoring, blood tests for HUS if pallor + reduced urine
- Surgical review if intussusception or toxic megacolon suspected
- Do not give anything by mouth to an unconscious or seizing child

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"Your child is very seriously ill and needs to go to hospital right now. Please do not go to a chemist. If your child is having fits or cannot wake up, this is an emergency. Go to the hospital emergency unit immediately."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Metronidazole (Flagyl) is the most commonly dispensed drug for dysentery at PMVs — effective against amoeba but NOT against Shigella, which is the more common and dangerous cause in under-5s; this delays effective treatment
- Antidiarrhoeals (loperamide) are dangerous in dysentery — they can cause toxic megacolon in Shigella infection
- "Blood in stool = worms" is a widespread belief — antihelminthics are given first, delaying antibiotic treatment
- Herbal purgatives given for bloody stool in some communities worsen dehydration and intestinal damage

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Stool microscopy is available at secondary facilities but turnaround can be slow — clinical diagnosis drives most management decisions
- Stool culture and sensitivity (for antibiotic resistance) is rarely available in Kano outside of AKTH — ciprofloxacin resistance in Shigella is emerging and may cause treatment failures
- IV ciprofloxacin for severe cases may not be available at all PHCs
- Surgical capacity for intussusception is only at secondary and tertiary level — transfer must not be delayed

**Cultural factors:**
- Blood in a child's stool is frightening and some families attribute it to spiritual attack or evil eye — traditional healer consultation may precede or replace clinic visit
- Communal weaning food preparation and shared eating utensils in compound households can lead to multiple children being affected simultaneously
- Water from uncovered containers, hawked water sachets, and street food are major transmission sources in Kano urban areas

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Malnourished children (SAM/MAM) — much higher risk of severe Shigella disease and complications
- Children recently weaned onto solid foods (6–18 months) — high exposure risk
- Children in households with open defecation or pit latrines near water sources
- Children who have recently received antibiotics for other reasons — gut flora disruption and possible C. difficile risk (less common but relevant)
