# PREECLAMPSIA & ECLAMPSIA — PREGNANCY DISEASE FILE
## For UMMA AI — Maternal & Child Health Companion (Kano/Northwest Nigeria Context)

---

## OVERVIEW

Preeclampsia is a hypertensive disorder of pregnancy defined by new-onset hypertension (≥140/90 mmHg) after 20 weeks gestation combined with proteinuria or end-organ dysfunction. Eclampsia is the occurrence of grand mal seizures in a woman with preeclampsia, in the absence of other neurological causes. In Nigeria, this disease complex is the leading or co-leading direct cause of maternal death, accounting for approximately 20–30% of maternal mortality nationally, with disproportionate burden in the northwest.

**Kano/Northwest Nigeria epidemiology:**
- High prevalence driven by early and closely-spaced pregnancies, poor ANC attendance, and late presentation
- Grand multiparous women (≥5 pregnancies) are heavily represented — parity is protective in Western populations but less so here due to chronic hypertension accumulation
- Adolescent primigravidae are extremely high-risk — early marriage is common in Kano state
- Many women present in eclampsia without any prior blood pressure check in pregnancy
- Sickle cell disease, chronic kidney disease from recurrent UTIs/nephrotic syndrome, and diabetes co-exist frequently and worsen outcomes

**Why it matters for UMMA:**
- Most deaths occur outside facility or on arrival — UMMA's role is to trigger early facility referral before convulsion
- Blood pressure measurement may not be available at home — UMMA must identify symptom-based danger signs
- Magnesium sulfate is available at secondary and tertiary facilities in Kano but often not at PHCs — this shapes referral urgency

**Common delays and barriers to care:**
- Delay 1 (recognition): Headache and swelling attributed to heat, stress, or spiritual causes — seizure may be first recognized emergency
- Delay 2 (decision): Family permission required for referral; male decision-makers may be absent or refuse
- Delay 3 (transport): Distance from LGAs to AKTH or specialist hospitals; no ambulance system
- Patent medicine vendors may give antihypertensives inappropriately or refer too late
- Traditional birth attendants may manage eclampsia with restraint and traditional remedies

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation (Preeclampsia without severe features)
- Blood pressure 140–159/90–109 mmHg on two readings taken at least 4 hours apart
- Mild pitting oedema of ankles and lower legs (NOTE: bilateral leg oedema is extremely common in normal pregnancy in hot climate — mild oedema alone is non-specific)
- Mild headache, not persistent
- No visual symptoms
- Fetal movement perceived as normal
- Proteinuria on dipstick (1+ or 2+) if testable — often not tested in community
- Woman feels generally unwell but functional

### Moderate Presentation (Preeclampsia with evolving features)
- Blood pressure 150–159/100–109 mmHg persistently
- Persistent frontal or occipital headache not relieved by paracetamol
- Facial oedema appearing in addition to leg oedema — woman or family notices face "swollen"
- Upper abdominal pain or epigastric discomfort (right upper quadrant = hepatic capsule stretching)
- Nausea and vomiting in third trimester (a warning sign, not normal)
- Visual blurring — "my eyes are not clear" or "I am seeing like fog"
- Reduced urine output noticed by woman
- Fetal movement may be subjectively reduced
- Generalised irritability and photophobia

### Severe Presentation (Severe preeclampsia / impending eclampsia / eclampsia)
- Blood pressure ≥160/110 mmHg (any single reading)
- Severe unrelenting headache — "worst headache of my life," not relieved by anything
- Visual disturbance: scotomata, flashing lights ("I am seeing stars"), transient blindness
- Epigastric or right upper quadrant pain — severe, may mimic acute abdomen
- Facial and generalised oedema
- Hyperreflexia, clonus (3+ beats or more at ankle)
- Oliguria: urine output <500mL in 24 hours or woman reports not passing urine
- Confusion, altered consciousness, extreme agitation
- ECLAMPSIA: tonic-clonic seizure, frothing at mouth, loss of consciousness, tongue bite, postictal confusion
- HELLP syndrome features: jaundice, bleeding from gums or IV sites, extreme malaise
- Fetal: reduced or absent fetal movements, abnormal CTG if available
- Pulmonary oedema: sudden breathlessness, pink frothy sputum

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Preeclampsia vs. Chronic hypertension in pregnancy:**
- Chronic hypertension: BP elevated BEFORE 20 weeks or known hypertension pre-pregnancy
- Preeclampsia: onset after 20 weeks; proteinuria or end-organ signs
- Superimposed preeclampsia on chronic hypertension is common in Nigeria and carries highest risk — sudden worsening of controlled BP with new proteinuria

**Preeclampsia vs. Gestational hypertension:**
- Gestational hypertension: elevated BP after 20 weeks WITHOUT proteinuria or end-organ dysfunction
- UMMA should treat gestational hypertension with same referral urgency as mild preeclampsia — cannot be distinguished without lab work

**Eclampsia vs. Epilepsy:**
- Eclampsia: first seizure in pregnancy or up to 6 weeks postpartum, usually with hypertension
- Epilepsy: known prior history; may not have hypertension
- In Nigeria, epilepsy is stigmatised — family may deny history; UMMA should treat ALL seizures in pregnancy as eclampsia until proven otherwise
- Malaria cerebral: fever, parasitemia, younger age, not necessarily hypertensive — but can co-exist

**Eclampsia vs. Cerebral malaria:**
- Malaria: fever prominent, history of fever/chills, positive RDT if available, more likely in first/second trimester
- Eclampsia: hypertension dominant, third trimester more common, fever may occur post-ictal
- Both can co-exist and both are medical emergencies — treat as both simultaneously

**HELLP syndrome:**
- May present WITHOUT severe hypertension or proteinuria initially
- Triad: haemolysis (jaundice), elevated liver enzymes (epigastric pain), low platelets (bleeding)
- Often misdiagnosed as hepatitis, typhoid, or acute abdomen in Nigerian settings
- Any pregnant woman with jaundice + abdominal pain = refer immediately

**Preeclampsia vs. Malaria in pregnancy:**
- Malaria: fever, rigors, anaemia, splenomegaly — headache and vomiting present in both
- Measure BP in ALL women presenting with headache in pregnancy regardless of other features

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- BP 140–159/90–109 mmHg
- Proteinuria present (if tested)
- No headache, visual symptoms, epigastric pain, or oedema beyond ankles
- Normal fetal movement
- No laboratory abnormalities (if available)

### Moderate criteria
- BP consistently in 150–159/100–109 range with symptoms
- Persistent headache or visual blurring
- Facial oedema
- Epigastric discomfort (not severe pain)
- Nausea/vomiting in third trimester
- Reduced fetal movement (subjective)
- Proteinuria 2+ or more on dipstick

### Severe criteria — ANY ONE = emergency, refer NOW
- BP ≥160/110 mmHg (even one reading)
- Severe persistent headache unresponsive to paracetamol
- Visual changes: scotomata, flashing lights, blindness
- Epigastric or RUQ pain severe
- Seizure (eclampsia) — active or reported
- Altered consciousness or confusion
- Oliguria or anuria
- Pulmonary oedema signs (breathlessness, pink sputum)
- Facial and generalised oedema with any hypertension
- Jaundice + thrombocytopenic bleeding (HELLP)
- Absent fetal movements
- Postpartum hypertension with any of above (up to 6 weeks)

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
**Clinical actions:**
- Same-day ANC attendance for BP measurement, urine dipstick, fetal assessment
- Increase ANC frequency — at least weekly monitoring
- Advise rest (reduce strenuous activity) but strict bed rest not evidence-based
- Monitor for worsening symptoms — give woman specific warning signs to watch for
- Do not start antihypertensives without facility assessment and confirmation
- Ensure delivery planning — mild preeclampsia requires delivery at or near term in a facility

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your blood pressure may be high and we need a nurse or doctor to check you today. Please go to your nearest health centre or clinic as soon as possible — do not wait. You are not in danger right now but things can change quickly. You need to be checked regularly. If you get a bad headache, your face swells, you see blurred or flashing things, or your baby stops moving — go immediately."

### Moderate
**Clinical actions:**
- Same-day referral to secondary facility (General Hospital or FMC minimum)
- Do not manage at home or PHC level
- Advise on danger signs requiring emergency escalation
- Ensure escort and transport arranged — do not travel alone
- If woman declines referral: document warning signs clearly, advise return at first symptom change

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your symptoms are a serious warning. You need to go to the hospital today — not tomorrow, today. This is not something that can be treated at home or with medicine from a chemist. Please ask someone to take you. If you feel a sudden bad headache, see flashing lights, have pain under your ribs, or feel confused — this is an emergency. Go immediately to the nearest big hospital."

### Severe
**Clinical actions:**
- Emergency referral to tertiary care (AKTH, FMC Kano, or equivalent) — this is a life-threatening emergency
- Magnesium sulfate should be given at the referring facility if available before transport — UMMA should advise caller to request this
- Antihypertensive treatment to prevent stroke (labetalol, hydralazine, nifedipine) — at facility only
- Airway protection if seizing — left lateral position, do not restrain, clear airway, do not put anything in mouth
- Immediate delivery may be required regardless of gestational age
- Postpartum eclampsia is equally dangerous — referral rules apply for up to 6 weeks post-delivery

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"This is a life-threatening emergency. Go to the hospital RIGHT NOW — AKTH or a big hospital. Do not wait. If she is having a fit: lay her on her side, do not hold her down, do not put anything in her mouth, turn her head so she does not choke. Call someone to drive you immediately. Tell the hospital she had a seizure in pregnancy. She needs an injection called magnesium sulfate as soon as she arrives."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Herbal remedies ("agbo") administered for headache and swelling — may delay presentation and some hepatotoxic herbs can worsen HELLP syndrome
- Self-medication with antihypertensives from patent medicine vendors — incorrect drug, dose, or duration; sudden BP drop can cause fetal distress
- Restricting fluid intake to reduce swelling — exacerbates intravascular depletion
- Restraining a seizing woman — causes fractures, aspiration; common traditional response
- Attributing seizure to spiritual attack — delays emergency care critically
- Herbal enemas and vaginal preparations used to "clean" the womb in convulsing women — extremely dangerous

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- BP machines not available at many PHCs or available but not calibrated
- Magnesium sulfate not stocked at many PHCs — UMMA should direct to secondary or tertiary facility
- Urine dipstick often unavailable at PHC level
- No functioning ambulance system in most LGAs — transport must be privately arranged
- AKTH is the main referral centre for Kano but can be overwhelmed; FMC and private facilities (Murtala Muhammad Specialist Hospital) are alternatives

**Cultural factors:**
- Purdah restrictions may limit woman's ability to seek care independently — male permission required
- Postpartum period (40 days) treated as confinement — postpartum eclampsia may be missed as woman is not expected to present to facility
- Swelling and headache normalised as "pregnancy complaints" by community and some traditional attendants
- First pregnancy in adolescent girls — in-laws and mother-in-law may be primary decision-makers, not the woman herself

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Adolescent primigravidae (married before 18) — highest risk of eclampsia
- Grand multiparae (parity ≥5) with interval hypertension
- Women with chronic kidney disease (from recurrent UTI, nephrotic syndrome, diabetes)
- Sickle cell disease patients (SS or SC genotype)
- Twin or higher-order pregnancies
- Women with no ANC attendance — presenting in labour with unknown BP history
- Postpartum women (up to 6 weeks) — often not considered "pregnant patients" by family or community
