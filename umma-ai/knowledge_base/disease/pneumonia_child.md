# DISEASE FILE: PNEUMONIA IN A CHILD UNDER 5
## Nigerian Context (Kano / Northwest Priority) — Awaiting Clinician Verification

---

## OVERVIEW
Pneumonia is the second leading cause of death in Nigerian children under 5
after malaria, accounting for approximately 12% of under-5 mortality.
In Kano, acute respiratory infection affects approximately 38% of under-5
children. The leading bacterial causes are Streptococcus pneumoniae and
Haemophilus influenzae. Viral pneumonia including RSV is common in infants.
Malnutrition — present in 62% of Kano under-5 children — dramatically
worsens pneumonia outcomes. Malaria and pneumonia frequently coexist.
Indoor cooking fires using biomass fuel in northern Nigerian homes are a
significant risk factor for childhood respiratory disease.

---

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN CHILDREN UNDER 5

### Mild Presentation
- Cough — wet or dry
- Fast breathing — persistent even when child is calm
- Fever — low to moderate
- Mild reduction in feeding
- Child still alert and responsive
- No chest indrawing
- Able to drink

Fast breathing thresholds (WHO IMCI):
- Under 2 months — 60 or more breaths per minute
- 2 to 11 months — 50 or more breaths per minute
- 1 to 5 years — 40 or more breaths per minute

### Moderate Presentation
- Persistent cough
- Fast breathing at rest
- Chest indrawing — skin pulling in between ribs or below breastbone
- Moderate fever
- Reduced feeding — child drinking less than half normal
- Child looks tired and unwell
- Nasal flaring

### Severe Presentation — Any ONE = Severe Pneumonia, Emergency
- Central cyanosis — blue or grey lips, tongue, or fingertips
- Inability to drink or breastfeed at all
- Severe chest indrawing — marked, involving the whole chest
- Grunting with each breath — especially in infants
- Stridor at rest — harsh crowing noise breathing in
- Altered consciousness — very drowsy or unresponsive
- Severe respiratory distress — child cannot complete a sentence or feed
- Head nodding with each breath — exhaustion sign in infant

---

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES
- Fast breathing at rest is the most reliable sign of pneumonia in children
- Chest indrawing distinguishes moderate to severe from mild pneumonia
- Cough may be absent in young infants — fast breathing may be the only sign
- In malnourished children — fever may be absent even in severe pneumonia
- Malaria anaemia also causes fast breathing — pallor with fast breathing
  and no cough suggests anaemia, not pneumonia
- Both can coexist — if both fever patterns and chest signs present, treat
  as both and escalate

---

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild — Non-severe pneumonia
- Cough with fast breathing
- No chest indrawing
- Child alert and able to drink
- No central cyanosis

### Moderate — Pneumonia with chest indrawing
- Fast breathing AND chest indrawing
- Feeding reduced but child still drinking some
- No cyanosis, no altered consciousness
- Child looks unwell but not critically so

### Severe — Very severe pneumonia, emergency
- Any of: cyanosis, inability to drink, severe chest indrawing, grunting,
  stridor at rest, altered consciousness, head nodding
- Child in evident respiratory distress
- Any severe sign in child under 2 months — emergency

---

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
- Urgent clinic visit — same day
- Oral antibiotics needed — prescribed by clinician
- Continue breastfeeding and oral fluids
- Paracetamol for fever
- Return immediately if breathing worsens or child cannot drink

Plain language for caregiver:
"Your child has a chest problem that needs antibiotic medicine from the
clinic. Please go today. Keep giving fluids and watch the breathing carefully.
Come back immediately if the breathing gets faster or harder."

### Moderate
- Hospital today — not just a clinic
- Needs proper assessment and likely injectable antibiotics
- May need oxygen if available
- Do not delay — chest indrawing means the lungs are working very hard

Plain language for caregiver:
"The chest pulling in when your child breathes means this is more serious.
Your child needs to go to hospital today for proper treatment. Please go now."

### Severe
- Emergency — hospital immediately
- Needs oxygen, injectable antibiotics, close monitoring
- Life-threatening without hospital treatment
- Do not wait or attempt home care

Plain language for caregiver:
"This is a serious chest emergency. Your child needs hospital care right now.
Please go immediately — do not wait for any reason."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS
- Many caregivers in Kano treat cough and fast breathing with herbal steam
  inhalations — this does not treat pneumonia and delays care
- Antibiotics from patent medicine vendors without diagnosis are common —
  ask what has been given already
- Oxygen availability is limited in primary health centres in Kano —
  hospital-level care needed for severe cases
- Indoor cooking smoke is a major risk factor — counsel on ventilation
  where possible
- Malnourished child with pneumonia — deteriorates faster, escalate sooner
- Malaria and pneumonia coexist — both conditions must be treated
- HIV-positive children are at high risk of severe pneumonia — Pneumocystis
  jirovecii pneumonia must be considered in HIV-exposed infants
- Pulse oximetry is rarely available at primary level in Kano — clinical
  signs must guide management decisions
