# UMMA Disease File — Pneumonia (Child, Under 5)
# Category: Child illness | Subcategory: Respiratory illness
# Last reviewed: 2025

---

## OVERVIEW

Pneumonia is the leading infectious cause of death in children under 5 globally and remains a major killer in Kano and northwest Nigeria. It is caused by bacterial pathogens (most importantly Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae type b), viral pathogens (RSV, influenza, measles), and atypical organisms. In Nigerian children under 5, bacterial pneumonia predominates clinically and requires antibiotic treatment.

In Kano, pneumonia burden peaks during harmattan season (November–February) when dry cold air, dust, and indoor smoke exposure are highest, but occurs year-round. It is the most important cause of respiratory distress and fast breathing in children over 2 months.

**Why it matters for UMMA:** Pneumonia kills because it is under-recognised and under-treated. Fast breathing is the cardinal sign but is often attributed to fever alone. Mothers frequently present to PMVs and receive subtherapeutic antibiotics or cough syrups only. Children with severe pneumonia need hospital-level antibiotics and supportive care.

**Common delays and barriers:**
- Fast breathing not identified as dangerous — attributed to fever or "normal" child breathing
- Cough treated symptomatically with cough syrups from PMVs — delays antibiotic treatment
- Nocturnal worsening not recognised until morning
- Pneumonia concurrent with malaria — one diagnosis delays recognition of the other
- Malnutrition significantly worsens pneumonia outcomes and is very common in Kano under-5s
- Indoor air pollution (wood smoke, kerosene) both causes and worsens pneumonia — ubiquitous in Kano households

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
Non-severe pneumonia (WHO classification):
- Cough — may be dry or productive (child cannot expectorate; swallows sputum)
- Fast breathing at rest (after fever has been treated with paracetamol for at least 30 minutes):
  - Age 2–11 months: ≥50 breaths/minute
  - Age 12 months–5 years: ≥40 breaths/minute
- Fever — usually present but not always
- Reduced feeding and activity
- No chest indrawing
- No grunting or nasal flaring
- Child is alert and can be comforted
- No cyanosis

**Nigerian-specific note:** Always count respiratory rate after fever is reduced — fever alone raises respiratory rate and can cause false positive. Count for a full 60 seconds with the child calm. If fast breathing persists after paracetamol, the fast breathing is likely due to pneumonia.

### Moderate Presentation
- All features of mild, PLUS:
- Chest indrawing: lower chest wall draws IN during inspiration (distinguish from normal abdominal breathing in infants)
- Nasal flaring
- Increased work of breathing visible — use of neck muscles, subcostal recession
- Child is anxious or agitated due to breathing difficulty
- Feeding is significantly reduced — child cannot feed well while breathing fast
- Mild cyanosis may be developing (lips or fingertips)
- Grunting may be present — particularly in infants
- In infants: head bobbing with each breath

### Severe Presentation
- Any ONE of the following indicates severe pneumonia:
  - Central cyanosis — bluish discolouration of lips, tongue, fingers
  - Severe respiratory distress: grunting at rest, severe indrawing, child cannot complete a sentence or cry without stopping to breathe
  - Inability to feed or drink (too breathless to suck)
  - Altered consciousness, severe agitation or drowsiness from hypoxia
  - Convulsions
  - Stridor at rest (indicates upper airway obstruction — different management pathway)

**Very severe pneumonia features (immediate life threat):**
- Cyanosis
- Child cannot be roused
- Severe grunting with every breath
- Absent breath sounds or very reduced on one side (empyema, effusion)

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Pneumonia vs Malaria (very common coexistence):**
- Count respiratory rate AFTER giving paracetamol — malaria fever causes tachypnoea that resolves with fever reduction; pneumonia tachypnoea persists
- Pneumonia: cough prominent; focal chest signs; fast breathing persists after fever control
- Malaria: fever-dominant; cough may be absent; tachypnoea resolves with fever
- CRITICAL: Both can and frequently do coexist in Kano children — if RDT positive AND fast breathing persists after paracetamol, treat BOTH

**Pneumonia vs Bronchiolitis (important in infants):**
- Bronchiolitis: age typically under 12 months, usually 2–6 months; wheeze prominent; widespread crackles; RSV season (harmattan); no focal consolidation
- Pneumonia: any age under 5; focal signs more common; less wheeze; bacterial more likely
- See bronchiolitis_child.md for detailed comparison

**Pneumonia vs Cardiac failure:**
- Cardiac failure: bilateral basal crackles; hepatomegaly; oedema; history of congenital heart disease; gallop rhythm
- Pneumonia: focal signs; fever; cough
- Rheumatic heart disease (older children) can cause pulmonary oedema mimicking pneumonia

**Pneumonia vs Inhaled foreign body:**
- Foreign body: sudden onset, typically no fever at onset; unilateral reduced air entry; wheeze unresponsive to salbutamol; history of possible ingestion (peanuts, beans — common in Nigerian households)
- Pneumonia: gradual onset with fever; bilateral but may be asymmetric

**Pneumonia vs TB:**
- TB: chronic course >2 weeks; weight loss; night sweats; contact with known TB case; no or partial response to standard antibiotics
- Pneumonia: acute onset; responds to antibiotics
- Consider TB if pneumonia not improving after 48–72 hours of treatment

**Pneumonia vs Pertussis:**
- Pertussis: paroxysmal cough with whooping or cyanosis; apnoea in infants; post-tussive vomiting; prolonged course
- Immunisation history relevant — pertussis more likely in unimmunised or partially immunised children (common in Kano)

**Features making pneumonia more likely:**
- Fast breathing persisting after fever control
- Chest indrawing
- Cough + fever in child aged 2 months to 5 years
- Harmattan season
- Household smoke exposure
- Unimmunised child (PCV13 and Hib vaccination protect against common causative organisms)
- Preceding measles (measles pneumonia)

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria — Non-severe pneumonia
- Fast breathing at rest (age-appropriate cutoffs above)
- Cough and fever
- NO chest indrawing
- NO grunting
- NO cyanosis
- Child alert and can feed (though reduced)
- Can be managed with oral amoxicillin at PHC level

### Moderate criteria — Chest indrawing pneumonia (WHO "severe pneumonia" — hospital recommended)
- Chest indrawing (lower chest wall recedes on inspiration)
- Nasal flaring
- Grunting (in infants)
- Child anxious, feeding significantly reduced
- No cyanosis
- No altered consciousness
- Requires IV or IM antibiotics and hospital assessment; oral amoxicillin alone insufficient

**Note on WHO classification:** WHO currently classifies chest indrawing pneumonia as "severe pneumonia" requiring hospitalisation. In Nigerian context with limited hospital capacity, some guidelines manage chest indrawing pneumonia with high-dose oral amoxicillin at home if oxygen saturation >90% and adequate follow-up is assured. UMMA should recommend PHC/hospital assessment and let clinicians decide disposition.

### Severe criteria — Very severe pneumonia — ANY ONE = emergency
- Central cyanosis
- Inability to feed or drink (too breathless)
- Severe respiratory distress (severe indrawing, grunting, nasal flaring all together)
- Altered consciousness / not rousable
- Convulsions
- SpO2 <90% (where measured)
- Stridor at rest

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- Same-day attendance at PHC or clinic — child needs clinical assessment and prescription
- Oral amoxicillin is first-line at PHC — ensure full course (5 days minimum) even when child improves
- Paracetamol for fever and comfort
- Continue breastfeeding; encourage fluids
- Child should sleep in a well-ventilated room — reduce indoor smoke exposure (cook outside if possible, or improve ventilation)
- Count breaths again at home that night — if breathing becomes faster or chest starts to draw in, go to hospital that night
- Return for review in 48 hours or sooner if worsening
- Do not give cough syrups — they do not treat pneumonia and some contain harmful ingredients
- If child is unimmunised, discuss PCV and Hib vaccination at this visit

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child's fast breathing is a sign of pneumonia. She needs to go to the health centre today for medicine — not a cough syrup from the chemist, but a proper antibiotic. Give paracetamol for the fever and keep giving her breast milk. Tonight, watch her breathing — count the breaths for one minute when she is calm. If her chest starts to pull inward when she breathes, or she turns bluish, go to the hospital straight away. Do not wait until morning."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Hospital or secondary care attendance today — chest indrawing requires clinical assessment beyond PHC capacity in most cases
- Child may need IV antibiotics and oxygen — this cannot be managed at home
- Do not give food or anything that might cause choking if child is very breathless — small sips of fluid only if child can swallow safely
- Position child upright or semi-upright — helps breathing
- If travel to hospital will take >1 hour, go to nearest PHC first for stabilisation while arranging transport
- Monitor continuously during transport — watch for cyanosis developing

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child's chest is pulling inward when she breathes — this means she is working very hard to get air. She needs to go to the general hospital today, not just the health centre. They may need to give her medicine through a drip and give her oxygen. Try to keep her sitting up on the way there. If her lips or fingers start turning blue at any point, that is an emergency — go as fast as you can."

### Severe
UMMA should advise:
- Immediate emergency hospital — AKTH or nearest general hospital with oxygen and IV capacity
- Call ahead if possible
- Keep child upright during transport
- Do not give food or drink during transport
- Do not delay for any reason
- At hospital: emphasise child has fast breathing, chest indrawing, and [specify presenting feature — cyanosis/altered consciousness/not feeding]

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"This is an emergency. Your child needs oxygen and medicine through a drip — only the hospital can give this. Go to Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital or the nearest general hospital right now. Keep her sitting up. Do not give her anything to eat or drink on the way. Tell the doctors she has severe breathing difficulty. Please go now."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Cough syrup from PMV as sole treatment — delays antibiotic therapy; many Nigerian OTC cough syrups contain antihistamines, codeine derivatives, or dextromethorphan; none treat bacterial pneumonia
- Incomplete antibiotic courses — family stops amoxicillin when child improves after 2–3 days; ensures relapse and resistance
- Herbal steam inhalations — can cause burns to airway; no evidence of benefit
- Chest rubbing with camphor, Robb, or Mentholatum — not harmful in mild illness but does not treat pneumonia and may delay care
- Antibiotics from PMV (ampicillin capsules, septrin) without dose verification or duration — common in Kano; septrin (co-trimoxazole) resistance is high for pneumococcus
- Not immunising with PCV13 and Hib — these vaccines dramatically reduce pneumonia severity; low coverage in parts of Kano remains a problem

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Pulse oximetry unavailable at most PHCs in Kano — severity assessment is clinical
- Oxygen concentrators variably available — some PHCs in Kano have them; general hospitals more reliably stocked; reliability depends on electricity
- Chest X-ray available only at general hospitals and above
- Blood cultures not routinely done — empirical antibiotic treatment is standard
- Amoxicillin stock-outs occur at PHC level — UMMA should advise families to ask specifically for amoxicillin; if unavailable, seek care at next level

**Cultural factors:**
- Indoor air pollution: three-stone open fire cooking and kerosene lamps are major risk factors for pneumonia in children in Kano households — culturally and economically entrenched; UMMA should mention improved ventilation as a harm reduction step
- Harmattan and "cold": cold air is culturally attributed as a cause of respiratory illness — this is partially accurate (cold dry air + dust increases respiratory irritation); UMMA can validate this belief while directing to treatment
- Male child preference may occasionally mean girls with pneumonia are assessed as less urgent — UMMA should not reinforce this
- Measles: measles causes severe pneumonia in unimmunised children; outbreaks occur in Kano; UMMA should flag unimmunised status as high risk

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Severely malnourished children (SAM/MAM) — impaired immunity, impaired respiratory muscle function, no physiological reserve; any pneumonia in SAM child = treat as severe
- HIV-positive children — Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP) possible; atypical presentation; severe course
- Children with sickle cell disease — acute chest syndrome can mimic and overlap with pneumonia; requires hospital assessment
- Very young infants (under 2 months) — bacterial pneumonia in this age group is part of neonatal sepsis spectrum; refer to under-2-month sepsis protocols; Group B Strep and gram-negatives predominate; requires IV antibiotics at hospital
- Children with congenital heart disease — decompensate rapidly with any respiratory illness
- Unimmunised or partially immunised children — highest risk for severe pneumococcal and Hib pneumonia
