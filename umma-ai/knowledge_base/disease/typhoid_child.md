# DISEASE FILE: TYPHOID FEVER (CHILD)
**Category:** Child (0–5 years)
**Subcategory:** Febrile illness / Gastrointestinal
**Version:** 1.0

---

## OVERVIEW

Typhoid fever (enteric fever) is caused by Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi (and less commonly Paratyphi A/B/C). It is endemic in Kano and throughout northwest Nigeria, driven by poor water and sanitation infrastructure, open defaecation, and contaminated street food. Nigeria has one of the highest burdens of typhoid fever in sub-Saharan Africa. In children under 5, the clinical presentation is frequently atypical and overlaps heavily with malaria, making diagnosis difficult in resource-limited settings.

**Epidemiology in Kano/northwest Nigeria:**
- Highly endemic — year-round transmission with peak in the rainy and post-rainy season when faecal contamination of water sources is highest
- Street food (kilishi, suya, masa, gwaza, fura da nono) and contaminated sachet water are major transmission vectors in urban Kano
- Multidrug-resistant (MDR) and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) Salmonella Typhi strains are increasingly prevalent in Nigeria — treatment failure with ampicillin, chloramphenicol, and co-trimoxazole is common; fluoroquinolones and third-generation cephalosporins are current mainstays
- Blood culture confirmation is the gold standard but rarely available at PHC level; Widal test is widely used but poorly specific and frequently misinterpreted
- Children under 5 have less specific symptoms than older children and adults — high index of suspicion required

**Why it matters for UMMA:**
Typhoid is one of the most over-diagnosed and under-treated conditions in Nigerian paediatric practice simultaneously. Widal testing is used loosely and many children are given "typhoid treatment" for simple viral fever. Conversely, genuine typhoid causing complications (intestinal perforation, encephalopathy) is missed when assumed to be malaria. UMMA must recognise the possibility of typhoid in a febrile child with prolonged illness, help distinguish it from malaria and other differentials, and ensure appropriate referral. Deaths occur from delayed diagnosis of perforation and from inappropriate home treatment.

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
- Persistent low-to-moderate grade fever for 3–7 days, often described as not "very high" but not resolving with paracetamol
- Fever may be intermittent initially — mothers may not recognise persistence
- Mild headache (in children old enough to report it)
- Reduced appetite and general malaise
- Mild loose stools or constipation (constipation is more common early in typhoid than diarrhoea)
- Mild abdominal discomfort — diffuse, not focal
- Child less active than usual but still interactive
- No localising features
- May have mild cough (10–15% have respiratory symptoms)
- Mild hepatomegaly may be present on examination

### Moderate Presentation
- Fever persisting for 7–14 days, progressive — stepladder pattern (rising each day) in classic presentation, but often not clearly stepladder in young children
- Fever higher at night than in the morning
- Relative bradycardia — pulse rate lower than expected for degree of fever (a useful clinical sign when present)
- Significant anorexia — child refusing food
- Abdominal distension becoming apparent
- Loose stools or "pea-soup" diarrhoea in some; constipation in others
- Vomiting — may be prominent
- Child increasingly lethargic — spending more time lying down
- Hepatosplenomegaly (common in Nigerian children — enlarged spleen is an important physical finding)
- Mild jaundice in some
- Rose spots (salmon-coloured blanching macules on trunk): rarely identified in dark-skinned patients but occasionally visible on careful examination
- Cough and mild respiratory distress in some (typhoid pneumonitis)
- Coated tongue

### Severe Presentation (Complications)
- Prolonged fever > 14 days without improvement
- Acute abdomen: sudden onset severe abdominal pain and rigidity — intestinal perforation (surgical emergency, high mortality)
- Peritonitis features: rebound tenderness, board-like rigidity, absent bowel sounds
- Typhoid encephalopathy: confusion, altered consciousness, delirium, seizures, coma — "typhoid state"
- Severe anaemia (haemolysis or bone marrow suppression)
- Myocarditis: weak thready pulse, hypotension, cardiac failure
- Cholecystitis: right upper quadrant tenderness
- Osteomyelitis: bone pain, swelling — particularly in sickle cell disease children (Salmonella osteomyelitis)
- Haemorrhage: blood per rectum from intestinal ulceration
- Septic shock: hypotension, cold clammy extremities, reduced consciousness

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Typhoid vs Malaria:**
- The most critical differential in Kano — both cause prolonged fever in children
- Malaria: typically abrupt onset high fever with rigidity/chills, cyclical pattern (every 24–72 hours depending on species), splenomegaly, anaemia, positive RDT/blood film
- Typhoid: gradual onset, fever more persistent and progressive (not cyclical), abdominal symptoms more prominent, relative bradycardia, negative malaria RDT
- Critical point: malaria and typhoid frequently COEXIST in Nigerian children — a positive malaria RDT does not exclude typhoid. If a child has been treated for malaria and fever persists beyond 5–7 days, typhoid must be actively considered.
- UMMA rule: persistent fever beyond 5–7 days despite malaria treatment = refer for typhoid evaluation

**Typhoid vs Gastroenteritis:**
- Gastroenteritis: acute onset, vomiting prominent early, profuse watery diarrhoea, rapid dehydration, shorter duration (3–7 days), fever less prominent or absent
- Typhoid: gradual onset, fever is the dominant feature, diarrhoea is variable and less profuse, illness lasts weeks without treatment

**Typhoid vs Urinary Tract Infection:**
- UTI: dysuria, frequency, suprapubic tenderness, back pain — fever without obvious source; urine dipstick/culture positive
- Typhoid: abdominal and systemic features dominate, urinary symptoms not prominent unless concurrent UTI

**Typhoid vs Brucellosis:**
- Brucellosis is underdiagnosed in Kano given livestock exposure and consumption of unpasteurised fura da nono
- Brucellosis: undulant fever, sweats, joint pains, lymphadenopathy, sacroiliitis; blood culture and serology needed to differentiate

**Typhoid vs Viral febrile illness:**
- Viral illness: short duration (< 7 days), upper respiratory symptoms, no abdominal distension, well-looking child after fever defervesces

**Widal test — important caution for Nigerian context:**
- Widal is non-specific in endemic settings — background titres are elevated in many people who have had prior exposure or vaccination
- A positive Widal alone does not confirm typhoid and a negative Widal does not exclude it
- UMMA should not advise treatment based on Widal result alone — clinical assessment and blood culture (where available) are needed
- Widal is frequently used by patent medicine vendors and small clinics to justify "typhoid injections" — this is poor practice

**Common coexistences:**
- Typhoid + malaria (very common)
- Typhoid + anaemia (especially in malnourished children)
- Typhoid + Salmonella osteomyelitis in sickle cell disease children
- Typhoid + intestinal worm burden (facilitates bacterial translocation)

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Fever present for fewer than 7 days
- Child alert, interactive, distressed but rousable
- Eating and drinking reduced but not completely refusing
- No abdominal distension or focal tenderness
- No altered mental status
- No signs of dehydration
- Able to take oral medications

### Moderate criteria
- Fever for 7–14 days, progressive
- Significant lethargy — child spending most time lying down
- Anorexia — substantially reduced intake
- Abdominal distension present
- Vomiting that limits oral intake
- Hepatosplenomegaly on examination
- Mild jaundice
- No peritonism, no altered consciousness
- Widal or clinical picture strongly suggestive
- Child can still take oral fluids and medications

### Severe criteria — ANY ONE = severe/emergency
- Fever > 14 days without improvement or new deterioration after initial improvement
- Signs of intestinal perforation: sudden onset severe abdominal pain, abdominal rigidity, rebound tenderness, absent bowel sounds
- Blood per rectum
- Altered consciousness, confusion, seizures, or coma (typhoid encephalopathy)
- Hypotension or shock
- Severe anaemia with cardiac compromise
- Complete refusal of food and fluids with deteriorating clinical status
- Signs of peritonitis (surgical emergency)

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild — UMMA advice
**Clinical approach:** Child with fewer than 7 days of persistent fever and mild symptoms should still be assessed at a PHC or clinic — typhoid cannot be reliably diagnosed or treated without clinical examination and basic investigation.

- Child needs to be seen at a clinic — today if possible, or within the next 24 hours. Persistent fever for more than 3 days in a child always needs evaluation.
- At the clinic: malaria RDT should be done first. If negative and fever pattern is consistent with typhoid, further evaluation is needed.
- Blood culture (if available at facility) is the gold standard.
- Typhoid treatment requires a specific antibiotic — advise mother not to start any antibiotic from a patent medicine vendor before the clinic assessment. Wrong antibiotics worsen outcomes and contribute to resistance.
- Paracetamol can be given at home for fever and comfort while waiting to be seen.
- Ensure the child is drinking fluids — ORS, clean water, breast milk.
- Soft foods as tolerated.
- Do not give aspirin or ibuprofen to febrile children without medical advice.

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child has had a fever for several days and this needs to be checked at a clinic. It could be malaria, typhoid, or another infection — only a clinic can tell. Please go today or tomorrow. Do not buy antibiotics or typhoid injections before the clinic visit — the wrong medicine can make things worse. Give paracetamol for the fever and make sure your child keeps drinking fluids. If your child becomes very weak, confused, stops eating and drinking, or the fever suddenly gets much worse, go to the clinic immediately."

---

### Moderate — UMMA advice
**Clinical approach:** Child with more than 7 days of progressive fever, lethargy, and abdominal symptoms needs same-day facility care. Requires blood culture (if available), blood film/RDT, full blood count, and appropriate antibiotic therapy. Should be seen at a general hospital level where lab support is available.

- Child must be seen at a general hospital or a clinic with laboratory facilities TODAY. PHC-only facilities may not have appropriate diagnostics.
- This is not something to manage at home — the child needs a doctor's assessment, blood tests, and prescription antibiotics.
- On the way to the facility: continue giving fluids. If vomiting is severe, small frequent sips. Do not give any antibiotics before arrival.
- At hospital: will likely need blood culture, full blood count, malaria test, and liver function. Treatment will be prescribed — likely a cephalosporin or fluoroquinolone depending on availability and age.
- Advise mother to tell the doctor: exactly how many days the fever has been present, any previous treatment given, whether malaria was tested and the result.
- If the child cannot keep fluids down: IV fluids and IV antibiotics may be needed — inpatient admission.

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child has had a fever for [X] days and is getting worse — this needs proper treatment from a hospital today. This is likely a serious infection like typhoid that needs the right antibiotic, and only a doctor can prescribe that after tests. Please do not buy medicines before going. Take your child to a general hospital, not just a patent medicine store. Tell the doctor exactly how long the fever has been there and what you have given so far. Keep giving fluids on the way."

---

### Severe — UMMA advice
**Clinical approach:** Emergency. Intestinal perforation, typhoid encephalopathy, and septic shock are life-threatening. Requires immediate referral to a tertiary/surgical facility.

- This is a medical emergency. The child needs to go to a hospital with a surgeon and emergency unit immediately.
- In Kano: Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital (AKTH) or the nearest general hospital with surgical capability.
- If the child has sudden severe abdominal pain, rigid abdomen, or is unconscious: this may be intestinal perforation or typhoid encephalopathy — both are surgical/intensive care emergencies with high mortality if not treated within hours.
- Do not give anything by mouth if the child has abdominal rigidity or reduced consciousness.
- Do not stop at a chemist or attempt home treatment.
- If the child is having seizures: place on their side, protect from injury, do not put anything in the mouth, go immediately to hospital.
- UMMA should convey extreme urgency clearly — delay is the most common cause of death in typhoid perforation.

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child is very seriously ill and needs emergency hospital care right now. This cannot wait and cannot be treated at home or by a chemist. Please go to [AKTH / nearest general hospital] immediately. If your child's stomach has become hard and painful, or if they are confused or unconscious, this is a surgical emergency. Go now."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous local practices:**
- "Typhoid injections" at patent medicine vendors: chloramphenicol and ampicillin injections are widely given by PMVs and unregistered practitioners without diagnosis. MDR strains mean these drugs often fail. Injections give a false sense of adequate treatment while the disease progresses.
- Widal test overuse and misinterpretation: almost every PMV and small clinic performs Widal; a titre of 1:80 or 1:160 is considered "positive typhoid" and treated accordingly — even in children who simply have endemic background titres. This leads to over-treatment with wrong drugs and under-investigation of other causes of fever.
- Herbal treatments for "typhoid": dawo, agbo, and other preparations are used — some with hepatotoxic ingredients that worsen the hepatitis component of typhoid
- Self-medication with ciprofloxacin: fluoroquinolones are available without prescription; parents buy courses they cannot complete, contributing to resistance
- Delayed recognition of perforation: sudden improvement followed by severe abdominal pain is misread as "the fever broke" — perforation causes a transient temperature drop followed by peritonitis

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Blood culture is unavailable at PHC level and uncommon even at general hospitals in Kano — clinical diagnosis is the norm
- XDR typhoid requires azithromycin or carbapenems — not reliably available in Kano; early referral to AKTH is critical for complicated cases
- Surgical capacity for perforation repair is available at AKTH but mortality remains high (30–50%) due to delayed presentation
- Unreliable drug supply chains mean prescribed antibiotics may not be available at the hospital pharmacy — mothers may need to source from external pharmacies

**Cultural factors:**
- Extended illness seen as "spiritual attack" or consequence of the evil eye (iska, evil spirit) in some communities — families may seek religious/spiritual healing before medical care for prolonged fevers
- Food restriction during illness: some families severely restrict food intake during typhoid — this worsens malnutrition and impairs recovery
- Decision-making by fathers or in-laws may delay female health workers at PHC being consulted for a sick child

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Children with sickle cell disease: at very high risk for Salmonella osteomyelitis and bacteraemia; any fever in a SCD child must exclude Salmonella — see sickle_cell_crises_child.md
- Malnourished children: impaired immune response, higher risk of bacteraemia and complications, poorer outcomes
- Children with prior incomplete typhoid treatment: risk of relapse and selection of resistant strains
- Children consuming raw fura da nono, unboiled well water, or street food regularly — highest exposure risk
