# DISEASE FILE: INTESTINAL OBSTRUCTION — CHILD
**Category:** Child (0–5 years)
**Subcategory:** Surgical emergency / Acute abdomen
**Version:** 1.0
**Context:** Kano and northwest Nigeria

---

## OVERVIEW

Intestinal obstruction in children under 5 encompasses several distinct conditions, each with different age peaks, presentations, and urgency levels. In Kano and northwest Nigeria, the most clinically important are:

- **Intussusception** (peak age 3–18 months) — most common cause of acute intestinal obstruction in infants
- **Hirschsprung's disease** (congenital; presents in neonates and infants, but delayed diagnosis common in Nigeria)
- **Adhesive obstruction** (post-surgical; less common in under-5s without prior surgery)
- **Incarcerated inguinal hernia** — common surgical emergency, especially in male infants; frequently missed at primary level
- **Intestinal volvulus** — rare but rapidly fatal
- **Ascariasis-related obstruction** — specifically important in Nigerian context; heavy worm burden can cause mechanical obstruction

This is a high-mortality condition in Nigeria because: most primary health centres cannot manage surgical emergencies; families delay presentation due to transport and cost barriers; obstruction is frequently misdiagnosed as gastroenteritis; and herbal enemas (gwangwani) given at home can worsen perforation risk.

Intestinal obstruction is a surgical emergency. UMMA's role is to identify red flags early and direct urgently to hospital with surgical capacity.

---

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild / Early Presentation
- **Intussusception (early):** Intermittent colicky abdominal pain — infant draws up legs and cries intensely for 1–3 minutes, then becomes quiet and appears normal; episodes repeat every 15–20 minutes
- Vomiting (may be bilious in intussusception)
- Reduced stool output or no stool
- Child feeding poorly between episodes
- Abdomen soft between pain episodes early on
- **Inguinal hernia (reducible):** Visible lump in groin or scrotum that appears with crying; disappears when infant relaxes — may be noticed by mother during bathing

### Moderate Presentation
- Persistent vomiting, becoming bilious (green vomit = strong red flag)
- Abdominal distension developing
- Child continuously irritable or lethargic between pain episodes
- No passage of stool or flatus for >12 hours
- **Intussusception (subacute):** "Redcurrant jelly" stool (blood and mucus) — pathognomonic but may not appear until 12–24 hours; do not wait for this sign
- Palpable sausage-shaped mass in right side of abdomen (intussusception)
- **Incarcerated hernia:** Lump in groin becomes hard, tender, non-reducible; infant inconsolable; overlying skin may be red

### Severe Presentation
- **Peritonitis / perforation:** Rigid board-like abdomen; severe tenderness; child does not allow abdomen to be touched; fever; shock
- **Intestinal gangrene:** Toxic appearance; high fever or hypothermia; circulatory collapse
- **Strangulated hernia:** As above with necrotic bowel — irreversible within hours without surgery
- **Volvulus:** Sudden severe abdominal pain, rapid distension, bilious vomiting, rapid shock
- Grossly bloody stool with haemodynamic instability
- Altered consciousness (shock)
- Child completely refuses feeds and is floppy

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**From acute gastroenteritis:**
- Gastroenteritis: watery diarrhoea, vomiting, fever — child passes stool frequently
- Obstruction: no stool or decreased stool, progressive distension, bilious vomiting, pain out of proportion to examination (early intussusception)
- Key feature: bilious (green) vomiting almost never occurs in simple gastroenteritis — treat as obstruction until proven otherwise
- Gastroenteritis does not produce intermittent episodic inconsolable crying with normal intervals (classic intussusception pattern)

**From malaria:**
- Malaria causes fever, vomiting, irritability — but NOT progressive abdominal distension or bilious vomiting
- Can coexist: malaria can trigger intussusception (lymphoid hyperplasia post-viral/parasitic illness is a risk factor)

**From simple colic:**
- Colic: typically <3 months; no vomiting; normal stool; no abdominal distension; child feeds well between episodes
- Intussusception: sudden onset in a previously well infant; progressive; vomiting; stool changes

**From appendicitis:**
- Appendicitis rare under 2 years but does occur
- Right iliac fossa tenderness localised + fever + anorexia
- Difficult to distinguish from intussusception without imaging

**From worm infestation (non-obstructive):**
- Ascaris commonly causes colicky pain, vomiting, pot-bellied appearance without obstruction
- Ascaris obstruction (bolus): diffuse tenderness, mass palpable (worm bolus), no flatus/stool — surgical emergency

**From mesenteric adenitis:**
- Central abdominal pain; often follows viral illness; no distension; normal stool output; child not toxic

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Intermittent colicky pain with pain-free intervals in an alert child
- No bilious vomiting
- Stool passed in last 12 hours
- No abdominal distension
- Reducible inguinal hernia (no incarceration)
- Child able to feed between episodes

### Moderate criteria
- Persistent vomiting (especially bilious)
- No stool or flatus >12 hours
- Mild-moderate abdominal distension
- Blood or mucus in stool
- Non-reducible inguinal hernia without overlying skin changes
- Child irritable/lethargic between episodes
- Feeding refused

### Severe criteria — any ONE = surgical emergency
- Bilious (green) vomiting at any age
- Board-like rigid abdomen
- Signs of shock (rapid weak pulse, cold peripheries, altered consciousness)
- Non-reducible tender inguinal hernia with red/purple skin changes
- Grossly bloody stool with deteriorating condition
- High fever with abdominal rigidity (perforation)
- Rapid progressive abdominal distension with acute pain

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- Any child with recurrent episodic inconsolable crying + vomiting should be seen at a health facility the same day
- Do NOT give laxatives, enemas, or herbal treatments — this is critical
- Do NOT give anything by mouth if obstruction suspected
- A reducible hernia without signs of incarceration should be reviewed by a clinician today — hernias can incarcerate suddenly
- Monitor closely and go to hospital immediately if vomiting becomes green, abdomen swells, or child deteriorates

**Plain language for UMMA:** "When a baby has episodes of severe crying where they pull their legs up, then go quiet, especially with vomiting — this needs to be checked at a clinic today. Do not give any medicines, herbal treatments, or enemas. If you notice green vomiting, a swollen belly, or the baby gets worse, go to the hospital immediately."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Go to a hospital with surgical capacity now — this cannot wait
- Do not give anything by mouth — no food, water, or traditional remedies
- Bilious vomiting in any child = go to hospital immediately without delay
- Bring child's vaccination card and any previous medical records
- The child may need IV fluids, X-ray, ultrasound, or surgery — only a hospital can assess this

**Plain language for UMMA:** "Your child needs to go to a hospital right now. Green or yellow vomiting, no stool, or a swollen belly in a baby that won't stop crying are signs of a possible blockage in the intestines. This needs surgery at a proper hospital. Do not wait and do not give anything by mouth. Go directly to a hospital with a surgery department."

### Severe
UMMA should advise:
- Life-threatening surgical emergency — go to hospital with surgical theatre immediately
- AKTH or Murtala Mohammed Specialist Hospital for surgical emergencies in Kano
- Do not stop at a primary health centre — they cannot manage this
- Keep child warm and positioned comfortably; do not give anything by mouth
- Call ahead if possible to alert surgical team
- Every minute matters — bowel gangrene progresses rapidly

**Plain language for UMMA:** "This is a life-threatening emergency. Please go immediately to AKTH or the nearest hospital with a surgery theatre. Do not stop anywhere else first. A rigid belly, green vomiting, signs of shock, or a hard painful lump in the groin that will not go back in means your child may need emergency surgery right now. Do not give any food, water, or medicines. Go immediately."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Herbal enemas (gwangwani) are widely used in Kano for abdominal complaints — can cause perforation, electrolyte imbalance, and rectal burns in infants
- Applying abdominal pressure or massage to "push back" hernias — causes gangrene
- Patent medicine vendor treatment of bilious vomiting as "ordinary vomiting" with antiemetics — delays surgical referral
- Assumption that bloody stool in infant = dysentery → treatment with antibiotics rather than surgical referral
- Traditional healers performing scarification on abdominal wall

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Paediatric surgical capacity concentrated at AKTH and a few specialist hospitals — primary and secondary centres cannot manage obstruction
- Ultrasound for intussusception diagnosis not available at most facilities outside tertiary level
- Air enema reduction for intussusception not universally available — many children proceed directly to surgery
- Blood products needed for surgical emergencies may be unavailable — families should be prepared for directed donation
- Anaesthetic paediatric capacity limited at secondary facilities

**Cultural factors:**
- Abdominal distension often attributed to "iba inu" (internal fever) or spiritual causes — traditional healer may be consulted first
- In Hausa culture, herbal preparations for abdominal complaints in infants are normalised — mothers may not volunteer this history
- Financial barrier to surgical care is significant — families may delay seeking surgical care due to anticipated cost; UMMA should emphasise that delay increases risk of death and cost

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Male infants (inguinal hernia far more common)
- Infants 3–12 months (intussusception peak)
- Children with heavy Ascaris burden (recurrent abdominal pain + pot belly)
- Post-viral illness infants (intussusception triggered by lymphoid hyperplasia)
- Children with previous abdominal surgery (adhesive obstruction)
- Neonates with delayed first meconium (Hirschsprung's disease)
