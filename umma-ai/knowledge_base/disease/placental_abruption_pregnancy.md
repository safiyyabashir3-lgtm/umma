# PLACENTAL ABRUPTION
*UMMA Disease File | Pregnancy Category*

---

## OVERVIEW

Placental abruption is the premature separation of a normally situated placenta from the uterine wall before delivery of the fetus. Blood collects between the placenta and uterine wall (retroplacental haematoma), causing uteroplacental insufficiency, foetal distress, and in severe cases, maternal haemorrhagic shock and foetal death. It is a leading direct cause of maternal and perinatal mortality in Nigeria.

**Presentation is highly variable:**
- *Revealed abruption*: external vaginal bleeding — visible and may alert the woman/family
- *Concealed abruption*: bleeding entirely trapped behind placenta — NO vaginal bleeding; may be misdiagnosed or missed entirely until patient collapses
- *Mixed*: both internal and external bleeding

**The critical concept for UMMA:** Severity of vaginal bleeding does NOT correlate with severity of abruption. Concealed abruption with massive internal haemorrhage may present with minimal or no vaginal bleeding. Abdominal pain and uterine tenderness are more reliable danger signs.

**Epidemiology (Kano/Northwest Nigeria):**
- Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (pre-eclampsia/eclampsia) are the strongest risk factor — both extremely prevalent in northern Nigeria
- Anaemia is near-universal in pregnant women in this population — dramatically worsens haemorrhagic outcomes
- Trauma (including abdominal trauma from falls, road traffic accidents, and domestic violence) is an important cause — domestic violence is under-reported
- Short inter-pregnancy interval (common in this population) is a risk factor
- High parity (5+ births) increases risk — common in Kano
- Grand multiparity (≥5 births): uterine wall changes increase abruption risk
- Traditional abdominal massage by traditional birth attendants (TBAs) can precipitate abruption
- Contributes significantly to the high perinatal mortality rate in northwest Nigeria

**Why it matters for UMMA:**
- Placental abruption is a third-trimester emergency requiring immediate hospital delivery
- Concealed abruption is particularly dangerous because neither patient nor family may appreciate severity
- Foetal death can occur rapidly — foetal movement changes are an important early warning
- Coagulopathy (DIC — disseminated intravascular coagulation) is a severe complication requiring blood products
- Many women in Kano plan home delivery with TBAs — placental abruption must be diverted to hospital regardless of birth preferences

**Common delays and barriers:**
- Concealed abruption not recognised as an emergency — woman has no visible bleeding
- Pain attributed to normal pregnancy discomfort or Braxton Hicks contractions
- Traditional birth attendants not recognising the condition
- Decision to travel requiring husband's permission or family consensus
- Transport to tertiary facility — AKTH or MMSH Kano — may take 1–3 hours from rural areas
- Financial barriers to emergency caesarean section and blood transfusion

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation (Small/partial abruption — grade 1)
- Small amount of dark red vaginal bleeding or spotting
- Mild lower abdominal discomfort or backache — may be described as "tightening"
- Uterus may be mildly tender or contracting more than usual
- Foetal movement may be perceived as normal
- Maternal vital signs normal
- Cervix may or may not be dilated
- Clinically easy to confuse with placenta praevia or "show" of labour

*This presentation must not be managed at home — all antepartum haemorrhage in third trimester requires hospital assessment.*

### Moderate Presentation (Moderate abruption — grade 2)
- Moderate vaginal bleeding (or absent bleeding with concealed abruption — important)
- Moderate-to-severe constant abdominal pain — the pain does not come and go like contractions; it is persistent
- Uterine tenderness on palpation — uterus feels "woody" or very firm between contractions; may be hypertonic (continuous contraction)
- Uterus may feel larger than expected for gestational age (due to concealed blood accumulation)
- Foetal movements: may be decreased or changed in character
- Foetal heart: may show abnormalities (bradycardia, late decelerations) — indicates foetal distress
- Maternal pulse elevated (90–110), blood pressure may be normal or elevated (if hypertensive aetiology)
- Patient in significant pain, anxious

### Severe Presentation (Major abruption — grade 3)
- Massive vaginal bleeding (or completely concealed with shock)
- Severe constant abdominal pain — patient distressed and unable to cooperate with examination
- Woody, board-like uterus — extremely tender on palpation
- Uterus grossly enlarged from concealed blood
- Absent foetal movements — foetal death may have occurred or be imminent
- Absent foetal heart tones on auscultation — foetal death
- Maternal haemorrhagic shock: pallor (conjunctivae, palms), cold clammy skin, rapid weak thready pulse, hypotension, fast breathing, confusion
- Coagulopathy (DIC) signs: prolonged bleeding from IV sites or wounds, bruising, petechiae — indicates consumption of clotting factors
- Oliguria or anuria
- Signs of hypertensive disease (if abruption secondary to pre-eclampsia): hypertension, headache, visual disturbance, epigastric pain

*This is an obstetric catastrophe — maternal and foetal survival depends on immediate delivery (often emergency caesarean section) and blood product replacement.*

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Placental abruption vs placenta praevia:**
- Both cause antepartum haemorrhage in third trimester
- Abruption: PAINFUL — constant abdominal pain, tender uterus, hypertonic uterus; bleeding may be dark; foetal distress; concealed form has no bleeding
- Placenta praevia: PAINLESS bright red vaginal bleeding; soft non-tender uterus; foetal presentation may be abnormal (transverse, oblique); bleeding often recurrent
- **Never do vaginal examination in third trimester bleeding without ultrasound confirmation of placental site — digital examination with praevia can cause fatal haemorrhage**
- In practice, both require immediate emergency hospital referral — do not attempt to differentiate at home

**Placental abruption vs labour:**
- Labour: contractions are intermittent and rhythmic; between contractions uterus is soft; cervix dilating progressively; vaginal bleeding is "show" (pink mucoid, not frank blood); foetus well
- Abruption: constant abdominal pain that does not relax; uterus hard and tender between contractions; foetal distress

**Placental abruption vs uterine rupture:**
- Uterine rupture: previous uterine scar (caesarean, myomectomy); sudden tearing pain; foetus may be palpable outside uterus; contractions cease; foetal parts very easily palpable; maternal shock
- Abruption: no scar required; uterus remains intact (usually); foetal parts less easily palpable through hypertonic uterus
- Both are catastrophic emergencies — differentiation by surgeon at theatre

**Placental abruption vs pre-eclampsia/HELLP without abruption:**
- Abruption may be triggered BY pre-eclampsia — both can coexist
- Pre-eclampsia without abruption: epigastric pain, headache, visual disturbance, oedema, hypertension — but uterus is NOT woody/tender and no retroplacental bleeding
- The combination of pre-eclampsia features + abdominal pain + uterine tenderness = abruption until proven otherwise

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild — all of the following:
- Small amount of vaginal bleeding
- Mild or no abdominal pain
- Uterus soft and not tender
- Foetal movements perceived as normal
- Maternal vital signs normal
- *Still requires same-day hospital assessment — mild abruption can extend rapidly*

### Moderate — any of the following:
- Moderate vaginal bleeding OR concealed abruption suspected (constant pain, tender uterus, NO bleeding)
- Constant abdominal pain (not intermittent)
- Uterine tenderness or hypertonicity
- Decreased or changed foetal movements
- Mild maternal tachycardia (pulse 90–110)
- Signs of foetal distress

### Severe — any ONE = obstetric emergency:
- Heavy vaginal bleeding or clinical evidence of major concealed haemorrhage
- Maternal haemodynamic compromise: tachycardia > 110, hypotension, pallor, cold extremities
- Absent foetal movements or absent foetal heart
- Board-like tender uterus
- Maternal altered consciousness or seizure
- Signs of DIC: abnormal bleeding from multiple sites
- Signs of pre-eclampsia/eclampsia coexisting with abruption

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild

**Clinical actions:**
- Same-day hospital referral without exception — no antepartum haemorrhage in third trimester can be managed at home
- Advise: no vaginal examination at home; do not insert anything vaginally
- Patient should not be left alone during transport
- Monitor foetal movement during travel — if movements stop, escalate urgency
- Nil by mouth in case emergency delivery needed

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"Any bleeding in the last months of pregnancy is serious and needs to be checked at the hospital today — not tomorrow. Even if the bleeding has stopped and you feel okay, please go now. Do not let anyone examine you vaginally at home or at a chemist. Keep track of whether your baby is still moving. Go with someone who can stay with you. If the pain gets worse or the bleeding gets heavier on the way, go to the nearest hospital — do not continue travelling long distance."

---

### Moderate

**Clinical actions:**
- Immediate hospital referral — emergency
- Nil by mouth
- Lie patient on her left side during transport (reduces aortocaval compression, improves uteroplacental flow)
- IV access if available at community level
- Count foetal movements during transport and report to hospital team
- Call ahead to hospital: "pregnant woman coming in, suspected placental abruption, foetal distress possible"
- Do not administer tocolytics (medicines to stop contractions) — this is not the appropriate treatment and will delay delivery

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"This is a pregnancy emergency and you need to go to the hospital right now. The pain you are feeling and the bleeding suggest that the placenta may be separating from the womb — this is dangerous for both you and your baby. Lie on your left side in the vehicle. Do not eat or drink anything. Count whether you feel your baby moving and tell the doctors when you arrive. Tell the hospital it may be placental abruption."

---

### Severe

**Clinical actions:**
- Immediate life-threatening emergency — minutes matter
- Lie patient on left side, keep warm
- Do NOT attempt any treatment at home level beyond positioning
- Call emergency services, community ambulance, or NEMA if available
- Rush to nearest hospital with surgical and blood transfusion capacity — tertiary centre preferred (AKTH Kano, MMSH Kano)
- Inform receiving hospital en route: maternal haemorrhagic shock, suspected major abruption, possibly no foetal heartbeat
- Blood group and crossmatch will be needed immediately on arrival
- Do not delay transport for any home measures

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"This is a life-threatening emergency for the mother. She is losing large amounts of blood — inside and possibly outside. She needs emergency surgery and blood transfusion right now. Call for an ambulance or any available vehicle immediately. Take her directly to [AKTH / MMSH Kano] — do not go to a smaller clinic as they will not have the resources she needs. Lay her on her left side. Do not give her anything to eat or drink. Do not leave her. Tell the hospital team immediately when you arrive that she is in shock from placental abruption."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Traditional birth attendants performing abdominal massage (TBA "turning the baby") in third trimester — can precipitate or worsen abruption; UMMA must advise against all abdominal manipulation by TBAs
- Herbal uterine "tonics" or abortifacients: can trigger uterine contractions and worsen abruption
- Waiting for "morning" or for husband to return before travelling — hours lost at this stage can be fatal
- Attempting home delivery with a TBA once abruption is occurring — TBAs have no capacity to manage haemorrhagic shock or perform emergency delivery

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Blood transfusion capacity is limited even at secondary level — direct referral to AKTH or MMSH Kano for severe cases
- Emergency caesarean section requires anaesthesia, theatre, and surgical team — not available at PHC level
- Transportation: Kano state ambulance service is limited; community health workers (CHEWs) and ward focal persons should be first contact for arranging transport
- Many women do not have blood group known — early ANC registration and blood grouping is important preventive advice

**Cultural factors:**
- Maternal death in pregnancy attributed to spiritual causes in some communities — delaying biomedical care; UMMA should acknowledge beliefs but strongly emphasise the need for immediate hospital care
- Purdah (seclusion): women in purdah may not be able to travel without male guardian permission — husband must be involved in transport decision immediately; in some communities, medical emergency has religious sanction to travel without full purdah restrictions
- Home delivery preference: very strong in many rural Kano communities — UMMA must explain that this particular emergency cannot be managed at home without endangering the mother's life
- Domestic violence: where abruption follows trauma, UMMA should handle sensitively — focus on medical urgency; do not pressure disclosure if it would delay transport

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Women with hypertensive disease of pregnancy (pre-eclampsia, chronic hypertension) — highest risk group; abruption complicates up to 2–5% of pre-eclamptic pregnancies
- Grand multiparas (5+ previous births): uterine wall changes, higher incidence
- Women with sickle cell disease: vaso-occlusive crises can involve uterine vessels
- Women with severe anaemia (Hb < 7g/dL): extremely poor tolerance of even moderate haemorrhage; haemorrhagic shock occurs at smaller blood loss volumes
- Women who have experienced previous abruption: recurrence risk 10–15%
- Adolescent mothers: both hypertensive disease and anaemia more common
- Women with short inter-pregnancy interval (< 18 months)
