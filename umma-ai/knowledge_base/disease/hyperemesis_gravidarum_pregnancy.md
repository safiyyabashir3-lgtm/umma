# UMMA Disease File — Hyperemesis Gravidarum in Pregnancy
# Category: Pregnancy illness | Subcategory: Gastrointestinal / metabolic
# Last reviewed: 2025

---

## OVERVIEW

Hyperemesis gravidarum (HG) is severe, persistent nausea and vomiting in pregnancy that causes dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, weight loss, and nutritional deficiency. It is distinguished from the normal nausea and vomiting of pregnancy (NVP, "morning sickness") by its severity, persistence, and impact on maternal nutritional status.

HG typically peaks at 8–12 weeks gestation and resolves in most women by 20 weeks, though a minority experience it throughout pregnancy. In its severe form, it causes Wernicke's encephalopathy (from thiamine/B1 deficiency), liver dysfunction, and fetal growth restriction from maternal starvation.

In Kano and northwest Nigeria, HG is underdiagnosed — severe vomiting in pregnancy is frequently managed at home with herbal remedies and home remedies, and women present to hospital only when severely dehydrated or collapsed. Multiple pregnancy (twins), which has a higher prevalence with increasing use of fertility medicines, and molar pregnancy are associated with more severe HG due to higher hCG levels.

**Why it matters for UMMA:** HG causes preventable maternal harm when managed inadequately at home. Many women stop eating, lose significant weight, and become severely dehydrated before seeking care. Women who cannot keep any food or fluid down for more than 24–48 hours need IV rehydration — this cannot be managed at home. UMMA must distinguish normal NVP from true HG, and HG from other causes of vomiting in pregnancy (appendicitis, UTI, hepatitis, malaria).

**Common delays and barriers:**
- Vomiting normalised as inevitable in pregnancy — "all pregnant women vomit"
- Herbal remedies used first — many worsen vomiting or cause hepatotoxicity
- Shame about inability to eat — women hide the extent of their illness
- Not knowing that IV fluids are available and safe in pregnancy
- Fear that hospital medications will harm the baby
- Dehydration causing weakness that makes reaching the hospital harder
- Husbands or family minimising the severity — "it is normal pregnancy"

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation — Normal nausea and vomiting of pregnancy (NVP) vs early HG

**Normal NVP (not HG — for reference):**
- Nausea with or without vomiting; typically worst in the morning but can occur any time
- Usually manageable with dietary adjustment — small frequent meals, ginger, dry crackers
- Woman can keep some food and fluid down
- Weight maintained or minimal loss (<5% body weight)
- No dehydration
- Begins around 4–6 weeks; usually improves by 12–14 weeks

**Early / Mild HG:**
- Vomiting multiple times daily (more than 3–4 episodes per day)
- Cannot complete a normal meal without vomiting
- Nausea is constant and severe — not just in the morning
- Reduced oral intake but still managing some fluids
- Mild weight loss — woman notices clothes fitting differently
- Mild dehydration — slightly dry mouth, slightly reduced urine but still passing some
- Still able to perform basic daily activities
- No ketonuria (if tested)

### Moderate Presentation
- Vomiting almost everything eaten and drunk — retching even when stomach is empty
- Cannot keep oral fluids down for several hours at a time
- Significant weight loss — >5% body weight (noticeable thinning of face, arms)
- Moderate dehydration: dry mouth, concentrated dark urine, reduced urine frequency, dizziness on standing
- Weakness — reduced ability to perform household tasks
- Ketonuria present (if urine dipstick available) — indicates starvation ketosis
- Psychological distress — tearfulness, hopelessness, anxiety about the pregnancy
- Heartburn and acid taste from repeated retching
- Difficulty sleeping due to nausea
- Constipation from reduced intake and dehydration

**Nigerian-specific note:** Women with moderate HG often look thin and unwell. Family members may describe the woman as "fading" or "becoming too weak." This clinical picture warrants hospital assessment and likely IV fluids.

### Severe Presentation — life-threatening HG
- Complete inability to keep any fluid down — even sips of water trigger vomiting
- Severe dehydration: sunken eyes, very dry mucous membranes, no urine output for >8 hours, skin tenting
- Severe weight loss — >10% body weight; severe weakness
- Collapse or inability to stand without help
- Altered consciousness or confusion — URGENT: may indicate Wernicke's encephalopathy from thiamine deficiency
- Wernicke's encephalopathy features: confusion, inability to coordinate eye movements (ophthalmoplegia), unsteady gait — rare but devastating if missed; requires urgent IV thiamine
- Haematemesis — vomiting blood (from Mallory-Weiss oesophageal tear or severe gastritis from retching)
- Jaundice — HG-associated liver dysfunction (transient elevation of liver enzymes)
- Severe tachycardia from dehydration
- Severe electrolyte disturbance: muscle cramps or weakness, palpitations, tingling (hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia)

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**HG vs Normal NVP:**
- NVP: manageable; keeps some fluid down; no weight loss; no dehydration; resolves with dietary adjustment
- HG: cannot keep fluid down; weight loss; dehydration; does not respond to dietary changes alone

**HG vs Malaria:**
- Malaria: fever (usually); headache; chills; RDT positive; vomiting often a feature but not the dominant symptom early
- HG: no fever (unless concurrent infection); no headache; vomiting dominant from early pregnancy
- CRITICAL: Malaria and HG can coexist — malaria exacerbates vomiting in an already nauseous pregnant woman; always consider malaria in Kano regardless of other diagnoses

**HG vs Gastroenteritis:**
- Gastroenteritis: diarrhoea usually prominent; other household members affected; acute onset; often with fever; not necessarily related to gestational timing
- HG: no diarrhoea; onset correlated with pregnancy (6–12 weeks); other household members well

**HG vs Urinary tract infection / pyelonephritis:**
- UTI: dysuria, frequency, loin pain; vomiting may occur with pyelonephritis but not the dominant feature
- HG: no urinary symptoms; vomiting dominant

**HG vs Appendicitis:**
- Appendicitis: right iliac fossa or periumbilical pain migrating to right; fever; guarding; can occur in pregnancy
- HG: no localised abdominal pain; no fever; gestational timing

**HG vs Molar pregnancy:**
- Molar pregnancy: HG may be more severe than expected for gestational age; disproportionately large uterus; no fetal heart on ultrasound; grape-like tissue
- Any woman with unusually severe HG very early in pregnancy — exclude molar pregnancy at hospital

**HG vs Hepatitis:**
- Hepatitis: jaundice; right upper quadrant pain; liver tenderness; contact history; fever may be present
- HG can cause mild transient jaundice — but if jaundice is prominent, exclude hepatitis

**HG vs Hyperthyroidism of pregnancy (gestational transient thyrotoxicosis):**
- GTT: associated with severe HG; tachycardia; tremor; high hCG driving TSH suppression; typically resolves when HG resolves
- Cannot be distinguished from HG at community level — hospital assessment required

**Features making HG more likely:**
- Gestational age 6–14 weeks
- Multiple pregnancy (twins — much higher hCG)
- Previous HG in prior pregnancy (strong predictor of recurrence)
- First pregnancy
- Molar pregnancy (excluded at hospital)

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria — NVP or early HG; manage with dietary modification first
- Vomiting ≤3 times daily
- Keeping some fluids and food down
- No significant dehydration
- No weight loss or minimal
- Managing daily activities

### Moderate criteria — HG requiring clinical assessment and likely IV fluids
- Vomiting >3–4 times daily; cannot complete meals
- Cannot keep fluids down consistently
- Visible weight loss
- Dizziness on standing; reduced urine output
- Weakness limiting daily function
- Not improving after 24–48 hours of home dietary management

### Severe criteria — emergency
ANY ONE:
- Cannot keep any fluid down — sips trigger vomiting
- No urine output >8 hours
- Collapsed or unable to stand
- Altered consciousness or confusion — Wernicke's risk
- Haematemesis (blood in vomit)
- Signs of severe electrolyte disturbance: muscle cramps, palpitations, tingling
- Jaundice
- Severe weight loss — clearly visibly wasted in early pregnancy

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- Dietary modification: small, frequent meals every 2 hours; dry, bland foods (plain rice, bread, crackers); cold foods tolerated better than hot (less smell); avoid fatty, spicy, or strongly scented foods
- Ginger: safe in pregnancy; effective for mild nausea — ginger tea, ginger biscuits
- Avoid triggers: cooking smells are a major trigger; if possible, have someone else cook or cook outdoors; cold foods reduce smell
- Hydration: small sips of cold water or light ORS frequently — not large amounts at once; sucking ice chips if available
- Rest: fatigue worsens nausea; rest between meals
- Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine): safe in pregnancy; available at PHC; effective for mild-moderate NVP — mention to ANC provider
- If not improving after 2–3 days or weight loss is noticed: attend clinic

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Nausea in pregnancy is very common and usually gets better by about 12 weeks. To help, eat small amounts every two hours instead of three large meals. Dry foods like plain rice or bread are easier to keep down. Ginger tea can help. Try to stay away from cooking smells if you can — cold foods smell less. Sip water slowly rather than drinking a lot at once. If after two or three days you still cannot eat anything or you are getting weaker, you need to go to the clinic."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Clinic or hospital attendance today — IV fluids and antiemetic injection very likely needed
- Anti-emetic medications are safe in pregnancy — metoclopramide, promethazine, ondansetron (by prescription); she does not need to suffer without medication
- Do not take any PMV medications for vomiting without prescription — some antiemetics and herbal preparations are harmful in pregnancy
- If she cannot get to clinic today: continue sipping ORS or cold water; rest; if no improvement in 24 hours or unable to take any fluid, she must go to hospital
- Vitamin B1 (thiamine) supplementation important if prolonged HG — clinician to advise
- Assess for concurrent malaria — fever or any suggestive features, add RDT

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"What you are describing is more than normal morning sickness — you are losing weight and cannot keep fluids down properly. You need to go to the clinic or hospital today. They can give you medicine by injection to stop the vomiting and fluids through a drip to rehydrate you. These medicines are safe for the baby. You do not have to just suffer through this. Please go today."

### Severe
UMMA should advise:
- Emergency hospital immediately — IV fluids, IV antiemetics, electrolyte correction, and thiamine are all needed
- If confused or unable to walk straight: state this clearly when arriving at hospital — Wernicke's encephalopathy requires immediate IV thiamine before any glucose infusion
- Do not give sugary drinks or glucose before thiamine in a woman with confusion and HG — this can precipitate Wernicke's
- If blood in vomit: state this at hospital — Mallory-Weiss tear or gastric erosion needs assessment
- Fetal wellbeing assessment should be part of the hospital admission

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"This is serious — you need to go to the hospital right now. You cannot keep any water down, you have not passed urine for hours, and your body is becoming very depleted. The hospital needs to give you fluids and medicine through a drip. If you are feeling confused or your vision is strange, tell the doctors immediately — that is very important for them to know. Do not try to drink sugary drinks first. Go now."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Herbal preparations for vomiting in pregnancy — many Nigerian herbal remedies for nausea (agbo, various "stomach medicines") contain hepatotoxic alkaloids; some cause severe liver damage; some are teratogenic; none are safe in pregnancy without proper evaluation
- Forcing food or fluid in severely vomiting woman — causes further vomiting, aspiration risk
- Stopping all food entirely and fasting to "rest the stomach" — worsens starvation ketosis and nutritional depletion
- Antihistamines from PMV (chlorphenamine, promethazine) — some are used for vomiting; promethazine is actually appropriate but should be prescribed not self-administered; chlorphenamine in high doses is not appropriate
- Metoclopramide from PMV without dose guidance — can cause extrapyramidal side effects, especially at higher doses
- IV glucose given to a confused HG patient before thiamine — precipitates Wernicke's encephalopathy; UMMA should flag confusion + HG = urgent thiamine before glucose to any hospital

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- IV fluids and antiemetics: available at general hospitals and most PHCs with IV capacity in Kano; Hartmann's solution or normal saline for rehydration; metoclopramide IV available widely
- Ondansetron: available at AKTH and private facilities; not routinely at PHC level
- Thiamine injection: should be available at general hospitals — important to request specifically in prolonged HG
- Urine dipstick for ketones: available at some PHCs; ketonuria confirms starvation state
- Ultrasound to exclude molar pregnancy: general hospital level; important if HG is very severe very early in pregnancy
- Electrolyte measurement (potassium): general hospitals and above; hypokalaemia from HG is dangerous and requires monitored replacement

**Cultural factors:**
- Normalisation of severe vomiting: family members — especially mothers-in-law — often dismiss HG as normal and expected; this is a major barrier to women seeking care; UMMA should validate the woman's distress and communicate that severe HG is a medical condition, not a sign of weakness
- Pica in HG: some women eat clay or non-food substances during HG; this both reflects nutritional deficiency and worsens it; ask sensitively
- Cooking duties: in many Kano households, the pregnant woman is still expected to cook even when severely nauseated; cooking smells are the strongest trigger; UMMA should help the woman communicate to her family why this is harmful and negotiate alternative arrangements
- Ramadan fasting during HG: fasting is extremely dangerous in a woman with HG — complete dehydration and ketosis can develop rapidly; sick and pregnant women are exempt from fasting in Islamic jurisprudence; UMMA can gently confirm this Islamic position for women who are distressed about being unable to fast
- Multiple pregnancy: the Kano context includes increasing use of fertility medicines (clomiphene widely available at PMVs); twin pregnancy is associated with more severe HG; UMMA should ask about possible multiple pregnancy in any woman with unusually severe HG

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Women with previous HG — very high recurrence risk; should have antiemetics prescribed early in subsequent pregnancy
- Multiple pregnancy (twins) — higher hCG; more severe HG
- First pregnancy — higher NVP and HG risk
- Women with molar pregnancy — severe HG; must be excluded at hospital
- Women with pre-existing diabetes — ketoacidosis risk from HG and starvation
- Women with pre-existing thyroid disease — HG can trigger transient thyrotoxicosis
- Severely malnourished women — less nutritional reserve; deplete faster; higher Wernicke's risk
- Women who cannot access hospital easily — dehydration will worsen at home without IV fluids; UMMA should assess transport options and escalate urgency accordingly
