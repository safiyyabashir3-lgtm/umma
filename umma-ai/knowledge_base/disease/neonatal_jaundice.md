# DISEASE FILE: NEONATAL JAUNDICE
## Nigerian Context (Kano / Northwest Priority) — Awaiting Clinician Verification

---

## OVERVIEW
Neonatal jaundice — yellowing of the skin and eyes in the newborn — is one
of the leading causes of neonatal death and permanent brain damage in Nigeria.
It is responsible for a significant proportion of neonatal admissions across
Kano hospitals. The condition results from elevated bilirubin in the newborn's
blood. While physiological jaundice is common and usually harmless, pathological
jaundice — particularly when bilirubin rises to dangerous levels — causes
kernicterus, a form of permanent brain damage resulting in cerebral palsy,
deafness, and intellectual disability. In Kano, many deliveries occur at home
or in poorly equipped facilities. Jaundiced newborns are frequently brought
to hospital late, often after days of home management with herbal remedies,
sunlight exposure, or sugar water. G6PD deficiency is highly prevalent in
Nigeria and is a major cause of severe sudden-onset neonatal jaundice,
frequently triggered by household substances including camphor balls.

## HOW IT PRESENTS

### Physiological Jaundice — Common, Usually Safe
- Appears day 2 to 3 of life — never in first 24 hours
- Mild yellowing of face and upper chest only
- Does not extend below the navel
- Baby alert, feeding well, active, normal cry
- Resolves by day 7 in term babies, day 14 in preterm babies
- No fever
- Not worsening

### Pathological Jaundice — Requires Urgent Assessment
- Appears within first 24 hours of life — always pathological
- OR appears after 24 hours but progresses rapidly
- Yellowing spreading below the navel to abdomen, legs, or palms and soles
- Baby becoming increasingly sleepy — hard to wake for feeds
- Baby feeding poorly or refusing feeds
- High-pitched or abnormal cry
- Pale or white stools alongside jaundice — suggests liver or bile duct problem
- Dark urine

### Severe — Kernicterus Risk, Emergency
- Baby very difficult to rouse — floppy or limp
- Arching of the back and neck — opisthotonus — late and dangerous sign
- High-pitched shrill cry
- Seizure
- Eyes deviated — looking upward (sunset sign)
- Baby no longer feeding at all

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES BY CAUSE

### ABO or Rhesus incompatibility (haemolytic)
- Jaundice appearing in first 24 hours
- Rapidly rising bilirubin
- Pallor alongside jaundice
- Mother blood group O, baby blood group A or B — ABO incompatibility
- Mother Rhesus negative — Rhesus disease
- Previous affected sibling

### G6PD deficiency (haemolytic)
- Sudden dramatic worsening of jaundice
- Exposure to triggering substance — camphor balls (very common in Kano
  households), menthol, henna, naphthalene, certain antibiotics
- Family history of severe jaundice in previous children
- Male baby more commonly affected (X-linked)
- Can occur even in apparently physiological jaundice that suddenly worsens

### Neonatal sepsis (infective)
- Fever or hypothermia alongside jaundice
- Baby looks unwell beyond just yellow
- Umbilical cord red, swollen, or discharging
- Baby lethargic, poor feeding, abnormal cry
- Late-onset — after day 3

### Breast milk jaundice
- Appears after day 4 to 5
- Mild and prolonged — can last several weeks
- Baby feeding well, gaining weight, alert and active
- No other symptoms
- Diagnosis of exclusion — only after pathological causes excluded

### Prolonged jaundice with pale stools — biliary obstruction
- Jaundice persisting beyond 2 weeks in term baby or 3 weeks in preterm
- Stools pale or white — clay coloured
- Urine dark
- This is a surgical emergency — biliary atresia until proven otherwise

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Low risk — monitor closely
- Day 2 to 3 onset, face and chest only, baby feeding well and alert,
  not worsening, term baby, no risk factors

### Moderate risk — urgent clinic same day
- Spreading below navel, OR baby feeding less well, OR bilirubin level
  not known, OR any uncertainty about whether it is worsening

### High risk — emergency
- Jaundice in first 24 hours
- Jaundice spreading rapidly — reaching legs, palms, soles
- Baby lethargic, not waking for feeds
- Abnormal cry
- Any G6PD trigger identified
- Suspected sepsis

### Extreme emergency — kernicterus risk
- Baby floppy or unresponsive
- Arching of back and neck
- Seizure
- Baby cannot feed at all

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Low risk physiological jaundice
- Clinic review within 24 hours to assess visually
- Frequent breastfeeding — 8 to 12 feeds per day
- Do NOT stop breastfeeding
- Do NOT use sunlight as treatment — not evidence-based and can cause
  sunburn and dehydration
- Do NOT give sugar water, herbal medicines, or gripe water
- Return immediately if yellowing spreads or baby becomes sleepier

Plain language for caregiver:
"The yellowing in your baby may be the normal type that many babies get.
But it still needs to be checked at the clinic today or tomorrow to be sure.
Keep breastfeeding often. Do not put the baby in direct sun — this does
not treat the yellow colour and can harm the baby. Come back immediately
if the yellowing spreads further or the baby becomes harder to wake."

### Moderate risk
- Urgent hospital today — bilirubin measurement and phototherapy assessment
- Phototherapy (blue light treatment) is the treatment — hospital only
- Do NOT delay

Plain language for caregiver:
"Your baby's yellowing needs to be checked at hospital today. There is
a light treatment that can bring the yellow level down safely — but it
is only available at hospital. Please go now."

### High risk and extreme emergency
- Emergency — hospital immediately
- Phototherapy or exchange transfusion needed — hospital only
- Every hour of delay increases risk of permanent brain damage

Plain language for caregiver:
"This is serious and your baby needs emergency hospital care right now.
The yellow colour has reached a level that can damage the brain if not
treated immediately. Please go to hospital now — do not stop anywhere."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS
- Camphor balls (mothballs) are extremely commonly used in Kano homes
  for clothing storage — they trigger severe G6PD haemolysis and
  must never be placed near a newborn or in the same room
- Home sunlight treatment is widely practiced in northern Nigeria and
  widely believed to be effective — it is not effective and can cause
  dehydration and sunburn; must be addressed sensitively but firmly
- Herbal medicines and gripe water given to jaundiced newborns in Kano
  can worsen jaundice — always ask and advise to stop
- Exclusive breastfeeding continuation is important — stopping
  breastfeeding does not help and causes other harms
- Many home deliveries in Kano mean jaundice is first noticed late at
  home with no access to bilirubin measurement
- Phototherapy equipment availability varies across Kano health facilities
  — Murtala Muhammad Specialist Hospital and AKTH have reliable access
- Exchange transfusion — for severe kernicterus risk — available only
  at tertiary centres
- G6PD screening is not routinely done in most Kano hospitals at birth —
  clinicians treat on clinical grounds
- Male infants are at higher risk of G6PD-related jaundice but all
  infants should be considered at risk given prevalence in Kano
