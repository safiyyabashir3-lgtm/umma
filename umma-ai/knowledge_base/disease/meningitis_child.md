# DISEASE FILE: MENINGITIS IN A CHILD UNDER 5
## Nigerian Context (Kano / Northwest Priority) — Awaiting Clinician Verification

---

## OVERVIEW
Meningitis is inflammation of the membranes covering the brain and spinal cord,
caused by bacteria, viruses, or other organisms. Northern Nigeria — including
Kano — sits within the African meningitis belt, a region stretching across
sub-Saharan Africa with the world's highest meningitis burden. Bacterial
meningitis in this region is predominantly caused by Neisseria meningitidis,
Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Haemophilus influenzae. Meningococcal outbreaks
occur periodically across northern Nigerian states. Without prompt hospital
treatment, bacterial meningitis kills within hours to days. Survivors frequently
suffer permanent disability — deafness, brain damage, limb loss. Many children
in Kano present late due to delayed care-seeking and prior home treatment.
Meningitis cannot be distinguished from cerebral malaria without hospital
investigations — both are emergencies requiring immediate care.

---

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN CHILDREN UNDER 5

### Early Presentation
- High fever — sudden onset
- Severe headache in older children — may be reported as head pain or
  the child holding their head
- Irritability — unusual, inconsolable, high-pitched cry in infants
- Poor feeding
- Vomiting
- Child looks very unwell — beyond typical fever illness
- Photophobia — crying or turning away from light in older infants

### Moderate Presentation
- All of the above worsening
- Neck stiffness — child resists or cries when neck is gently bent forward
- Bulging fontanelle in infants — soft spot on top of head pushed outward
- Altered consciousness — increasingly drowsy, confused, difficult to rouse
- Seizure occurring
- Rash beginning — initially flat pink spots

### Severe Presentation — Emergency
- Deeply unconscious or unresponsive
- Repeated or prolonged seizures
- Non-blanching rash — purple or dark red spots that do not fade when
  a glass is pressed against them — meningococcal septicaemia
- Shock — very fast weak pulse, cold extremities, grey appearance
- Opisthotonus — back arching with neck retraction
- Respiratory irregularity
- Rapid deterioration over hours

---

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES
- Neck stiffness combined with fever — highly suspicious for meningitis
  in any age child
- Bulging fontanelle in infant with fever — highly suspicious
- Non-blanching purpuric rash — meningococcal disease, immediate emergency
- Kernig sign — child cannot straighten knee when hip is flexed —
  meningeal irritation
- Brudzinski sign — flexing the neck causes the knees to draw up —
  meningeal irritation
- In young infants under 3 months — neck stiffness may be absent;
  poor feeding, high-pitched cry, and bulging fontanelle are key signs
- Cerebral malaria presents similarly — both are emergencies;
  do not attempt to differentiate in the community

---

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Suspected meningitis — always urgent to emergency
Any child with fever AND any of:
- Neck stiffness
- Bulging fontanelle
- Seizure with fever and child not recovering well
- Altered consciousness with fever
- Non-blanching rash
- High-pitched inconsolable cry in infant with fever

There is no mild category for suspected bacterial meningitis.
Every case of suspected meningitis is at minimum urgent — most are emergencies.

### Meningococcal septicaemia — always immediate emergency
- Non-blanching purpuric rash — purple or dark spots not fading under pressure
- Shock signs — fast weak pulse, cold grey child
- This is the most rapidly fatal presentation — death within hours possible

---

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Suspected meningitis — any presentation
- Emergency — go to hospital immediately
- No role for home management at any stage
- Do not wait to see if child improves
- Do not give any herbal medicines
- Lumbar puncture, blood culture, and IV antibiotics are needed —
  available only at hospital

Plain language for caregiver:
"This is a very serious infection of the brain covering and your child
needs emergency hospital care immediately. Please go now — do not wait
for any reason. Every minute matters with this illness."

### Meningococcal septicaemia — purpuric rash with shock
- Immediate emergency — fastest possible transfer to hospital
- This child may die within hours without IV antibiotics and supportive care

Plain language for caregiver:
"These dark spots on the skin that do not fade are a sign of a very
dangerous infection in the blood. This is a life-threatening emergency.
Please go to hospital right now — immediately."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS
- Northern Nigeria is in the meningitis belt — seasonal epidemics occur,
  especially in the dry harmattan season (November to May)
- Meningococcal vaccine (MenAfriVac) has been introduced in Nigeria but
  coverage remains incomplete in many Kano communities — ask about vaccination
- Lumbar puncture facilities are limited outside tertiary centres in Kano —
  empirical treatment is often started on clinical grounds
- IV ceftriaxone is the standard empirical treatment — available at
  secondary and tertiary facilities in Kano
- Delay caused by visiting traditional healers or patent medicine vendors
  before hospital is a common cause of death from meningitis in Kano
- Non-blanching purpuric rash must be explained to caregivers clearly —
  the glass test (pressing a glass against the rash to see if it fades)
  is a simple tool caregivers can be taught
- Survivors of bacterial meningitis in Kano frequently have hearing loss —
  hearing assessment after recovery is important
- Cerebral malaria and meningitis cannot be clinically distinguished —
  empirical treatment for both is standard practice at Kano hospitals
