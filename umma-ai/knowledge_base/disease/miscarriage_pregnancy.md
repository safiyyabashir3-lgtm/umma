# UMMA Disease File — Miscarriage (Spontaneous Abortion) in Pregnancy
# Category: Pregnancy illness | Subcategory: Early pregnancy complication
# Last reviewed: 2025

---

## OVERVIEW

Miscarriage (spontaneous abortion) is the loss of a pregnancy before 24 weeks gestation. It is the most common complication of early pregnancy — approximately 10–15% of clinically recognised pregnancies end in miscarriage, with the majority occurring in the first trimester. In Nigeria, miscarriage is underreported and frequently managed outside the formal health system, with significant associated morbidity and mortality from incomplete abortion, infection, and haemorrhage.

The clinical types of miscarriage that UMMA must distinguish are:
- **Threatened miscarriage:** Bleeding with closed cervix; pregnancy may continue
- **Inevitable miscarriage:** Bleeding with open cervix; pregnancy will not continue
- **Incomplete miscarriage:** Partial expulsion of products of conception; most dangerous for haemorrhage and infection
- **Complete miscarriage:** Full expulsion of all products; bleeding settling
- **Missed miscarriage:** Fetal death without expulsion — often asymptomatic
- **Septic abortion:** Infected miscarriage — medical emergency; significant maternal mortality

In Kano and northwest Nigeria, an important and clinically critical context is **unsafe abortion** — induced abortion performed outside safe medical settings. Nigeria's abortion laws are highly restrictive; unsafe abortion is common and is a leading cause of maternal mortality. Septic abortion presentations frequently follow attempted induced abortion. UMMA must be able to recognise septic abortion and refer urgently without judgment, regardless of cause.

**Why it matters for UMMA:** Miscarriage causes physical danger (haemorrhage, sepsis) and profound emotional distress. UMMA must respond to both. Clinical management by severity is essential — not all miscarriage requires emergency hospital attendance immediately, but incomplete and septic abortion do. UMMA's non-judgmental approach is critical in a context where many women will not disclose attempted induced abortion for fear of criminalisation or social punishment.

**Common delays and barriers:**
- Miscarriage managed entirely at home — "it will pass on its own"
- Products of conception not expelled completely — incomplete abortion not recognised
- Sepsis developing slowly before woman presents
- Shame and secrecy around pregnancy loss — particularly for unmarried women
- Fear of disclosure of induced abortion to health providers — fear of legal consequences
- TBA management of incomplete abortion with herbal preparations — worsens haemorrhage and infection
- Financial barriers to hospital evacuation (MVA or D&C)

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation — Threatened miscarriage or very early complete miscarriage

**Threatened miscarriage:**
- Vaginal bleeding — light to moderate spotting; may be pink, red, or brown
- Mild to moderate lower abdominal cramping or backache
- Cervical os closed (assessed at clinic — UMMA cannot assess this)
- Pregnancy symptoms may still be present (nausea, breast tenderness)
- No tissue passed
- Fetal heart may still be present on ultrasound

**Very early complete miscarriage (before 6–8 weeks):**
- Heavier bleeding with clots and cramping, then settling
- Small amount of tissue or clot material passed
- Bleeding and pain resolve after passage
- Pregnancy test may remain positive for days after complete miscarriage (hCG clearance)

### Moderate Presentation — Incomplete miscarriage or inevitable miscarriage

**Incomplete miscarriage:**
- Heavy bleeding — heavier than a period; soaking pads
- Cramping pain — colicky, intermittent or constant
- Tissue or clots passed — but bleeding continuing; this indicates retained products of conception
- Cervical os open (if assessed at clinic)
- Ongoing bleeding that does not settle
- Nausea or dizziness from blood loss

**Inevitable miscarriage:**
- Heavy bleeding with open cervical os
- Cramping pain
- No tissue passed yet — but pregnancy cannot continue
- Moderate blood loss; woman haemodynamically stable

**Missed miscarriage:**
- Pregnancy symptoms (nausea, breast tenderness) disappear
- No bleeding, no pain — woman may notice she "feels less pregnant"
- Fetal movements never felt (too early) or ceased
- Diagnosed on ultrasound showing absent fetal cardiac activity
- Womb does not expel spontaneously — retained non-viable pregnancy

### Severe Presentation — Septic abortion or major haemorrhage

**Septic abortion — life-threatening:**
- Fever — often high (>38.5°C); may be rigors
- Offensive vaginal discharge — foul-smelling, purulent
- Lower abdominal pain — severe, constant
- Uterine tenderness on palpation — exquisite
- Cervical motion tenderness (assessed at clinic)
- Signs of systemic sepsis: tachycardia, hypotension, altered consciousness, cold clammy peripheries
- History may include: insertion of objects into uterus, herbal preparations taken to induce abortion, incomplete expulsion managed at home for days
- No improvement or worsening despite home management

**Major haemorrhage from incomplete abortion:**
- Very heavy bleeding — soaking multiple pads rapidly
- Collapse or near-collapse
- Pale, sweating, tachycardic
- Severe dizziness or syncope
- Hypotension

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Threatened vs Inevitable vs Incomplete miscarriage:**
- Cannot be fully distinguished without speculum and vaginal examination — os open or closed; products present or absent
- UMMA should not attempt to distinguish — if significant bleeding with pain, refer for assessment

**Miscarriage vs Ectopic pregnancy — CRITICAL:**
- Ectopic pregnancy: pregnancy in the fallopian tube; rupture causes catastrophic intraperitoneal haemorrhage
- Ectopic: one-sided lower abdominal pain (may be referred to shoulder tip); bleeding may be lighter than expected; disproportionate haemodynamic compromise for visible bleeding (bleeding is internal); missed period; positive pregnancy test
- Miscarriage: central cramping; heavier external bleeding; tissue may be passed
- CRITICAL FOR UMMA: Ectopic must be considered in ANY woman with a missed period, positive pregnancy test, abdominal pain, and vaginal bleeding. A woman who is collapsing with minimal vaginal bleeding and abdominal pain may have a ruptured ectopic — this is immediately life-threatening
- UMMA cannot safely distinguish ectopic from miscarriage — ANY woman with early pregnancy bleeding and pain must be assessed at hospital same day

**Miscarriage vs Implantation bleeding:**
- Implantation: very early (around expected period); very light spotting only; no pain; no clots; resolves quickly
- Miscarriage: heavier; cramping; clots

**Miscarriage vs Cervical ectropion:**
- Ectropion: small amount post-coital or contact bleeding; no pain; no clots; cervix looks inflamed on speculum
- Miscarriage: heavier; cramping

**Septic abortion vs Pelvic inflammatory disease:**
- Septic abortion: recent pregnancy or abortion event; products may still be retained; heavy offensive discharge; uterine tenderness
- PID: not pregnant or very early pregnancy; bilateral adnexal tenderness; no retained products
- Both require hospital assessment and antibiotics

**Miscarriage vs Molar pregnancy:**
- Molar pregnancy (gestational trophoblastic disease): disproportionately large uterus for dates; very high hCG; no fetal parts on ultrasound; grape-like tissue may be passed
- Requires specialist management at hospital — evacuation followed by hCG monitoring
- UMMA: any woman passing unusual tissue (grape-like, vesicular) should be directed to hospital urgently

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria — assessment needed but not immediate emergency
- Light vaginal bleeding only — spotting or light flow
- Mild cramping
- No tissue passed
- No fever
- Haemodynamically stable — not dizzy, not collapsed
- Suspected threatened miscarriage

### Moderate criteria — same-day hospital assessment required
- Moderate to heavy bleeding
- Cramping with passage of clots or tissue
- Bleeding not settling after tissue passed
- Early pregnancy with any bleeding AND abdominal pain — ectopic must be excluded
- Missed miscarriage (no bleeding, no cardiac activity on scan) — needs management planning
- Incomplete miscarriage confirmed or suspected

### Severe criteria — emergency
ANY ONE:
- Heavy uncontrolled bleeding soaking multiple pads
- Signs of haemodynamic compromise: dizziness, syncope, collapse, tachycardia, pallor
- Fever with offensive vaginal discharge — septic abortion
- Severe abdominal pain disproportionate to visible bleeding — possible ectopic rupture
- Shoulder tip pain + collapse + minimal vaginal bleeding — ectopic rupture
- Altered consciousness
- Signs of septic shock: confusion, cold peripheries, rapid weak pulse, low blood pressure

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild — threatened miscarriage
UMMA should advise:
- Attend ANC or clinic today or tomorrow for assessment — bleeding in early pregnancy always requires clinical review
- Pelvic rest: no intercourse, no heavy lifting
- Avoid strenuous activity — rest
- Monitor bleeding: if bleeding increases or tissue is passed, attend hospital same day
- Emotional acknowledgement: threatened miscarriage is frightening; outcome is uncertain; most threatened miscarriages do not go on to complete
- If fever develops at any point: attend hospital immediately — infection risk
- Pregnancy test result and ultrasound (if available) will guide management at clinic

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Bleeding in early pregnancy is very frightening, and I understand you are scared. Many pregnancies that bleed in the early months continue safely — but you need to be seen at the clinic today or tomorrow to check. Until then, rest, avoid heavy work, and do not have intercourse. If the bleeding gets heavier, or you pass clots or pieces of tissue, or you develop a fever, go to the hospital immediately. I am here with you whatever happens."

### Moderate — incomplete miscarriage or early pregnancy bleeding with pain
UMMA should advise:
- Hospital attendance today — same day; not PHC alone for incomplete miscarriage
- Bring all passed tissue or clots if possible — facility needs to assess whether products are complete
- Incomplete miscarriage requires uterine evacuation (manual vacuum aspiration — MVA, or misoprostol) to stop bleeding and prevent infection — this can only be done at a properly equipped facility
- Ectopic must be excluded urgently if pain is present alongside bleeding
- Do not take herbal preparations to "complete" the miscarriage — these cause uncontrolled uterine contraction, haemorrhage, and infection
- Emotional support: whatever the outcome, she is not alone; UMMA will be available

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"You need to go to the hospital today. If you have passed clots or tissue but the bleeding has not stopped, this means not everything has come away and the doctors need to help complete the process safely. Please collect any tissue in a clean container and bring it with you if you can. Do not take any local medicine or herb to make it come away faster — this is dangerous. I know this is a painful and frightening time. Please go now and let the hospital help you."

### Severe — septic abortion or major haemorrhage
UMMA should advise:
- Emergency hospital immediately — AKTH or nearest general hospital
- If haemorrhaging: lay flat; keep warm; apply pad; go immediately
- If septic: high fever + offensive discharge + abdominal pain = sepsis risk; every hour of delay worsens outcome
- At hospital: IV antibiotics AND uterine evacuation are both required — one without the other is inadequate
- UMMA must be non-judgmental entirely — regardless of cause of the abortion, the woman needs emergency care and deserves it
- Do not disclose or encourage woman to disclose method of abortion if she has not volunteered this — her safety is the only priority; she can tell the doctors what she is comfortable sharing
- Family support if appropriate — but be sensitive to context; some presentations may be situations of privacy

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"You need to go to the hospital right now. The fever and the smell and the pain mean there may be an infection inside the womb — this can be very dangerous and needs treatment urgently. The doctors will give you antibiotics and clean the womb safely. You do not need to explain everything about how this happened — just tell them you are bleeding, you have a fever, and your belly hurts. They are there to help you, not to judge you. Please go immediately."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Herbal preparations to induce or complete abortion — misoprostol-equivalent herbs (e.g. local preparations containing ergot alkaloids); cause severe uterine cramping, incomplete abortion, haemorrhage, and uterine rupture
- Insertion of objects (cassava sticks, wire, local instruments by TBAs) to induce abortion — causes uterine perforation, bowel injury, catastrophic sepsis
- Waiting for "natural completion" of incomplete abortion at home — sepsis can develop within 24–48 hours
- Drinking caustic substances, concentrated herbal teas, or large doses of tablets to induce abortion — systemic toxicity
- TBA management of bleeding in early pregnancy — not equipped for haemorrhage or infection management

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Manual vacuum aspiration (MVA): available at general hospitals and AKTH; increasingly available at some PHCs under IPAS training programmes in Kano — but not universal
- Misoprostol: available at general hospitals; some PHCs; increasing availability in Kano through reproductive health programmes
- Ultrasound: needed for missed miscarriage diagnosis and ectopic exclusion; not at PHC level
- Beta-hCG testing: not available at PHC level; serum hCG available at AKTH and some general hospitals; urine pregnancy test widely available and adequate for initial assessment
- Blood transfusion: required for major haemorrhage; general hospital or above
- Sepsis management: IV antibiotics available at general hospitals; ICU care at AKTH only

**Cultural factors:**
- Miscarriage as punishment or spiritual attack: very common belief in northern Nigeria; women may feel ashamed, guilty, or spiritually condemned; UMMA should gently address this — miscarriage is a medical event that happens in many pregnancies and is not caused by the woman's actions or moral state
- Silence around pregnancy loss: grief after miscarriage is often suppressed — women expected to "move on"; UMMA should acknowledge the loss explicitly and validate grief
- Unsafe abortion and legal risk: Nigeria's legal context means women who present after unsafe abortion are often afraid to disclose; UMMA must be clearly and explicitly non-judgmental; health workers have an ethical obligation to treat regardless of cause; UMMA should never ask probing questions about how the abortion was induced
- Multiple pregnancy losses: recurrent miscarriage is attributed in many communities to spiritual causes (ogbanje, jinn); women may seek spiritual healing rather than medical investigation; UMMA can acknowledge beliefs while encouraging medical evaluation for recurrent loss
- Unmarried women: pregnancy loss in unmarried women is particularly fraught with shame; UMMA should be especially sensitive and non-judgmental in tone

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Women with previous miscarriage — recurrence risk elevated; early ANC attendance important
- Women with uterine abnormalities (fibroids, septum) — higher miscarriage risk; may not know diagnosis
- Women with poorly controlled diabetes or thyroid disease — higher miscarriage risk
- Women with malaria in first trimester — malaria increases miscarriage risk
- Adolescent women — higher risk of both unintended pregnancy and unsafe abortion
- Women presenting after unsafe abortion — highest mortality risk; require immediate non-judgmental emergency care
- Women with antiphospholipid syndrome or other thrombophilia — recurrent miscarriage; rarely diagnosed in this context but should be considered in women with 3+ losses
