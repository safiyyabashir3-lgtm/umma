# UMMA Disease File — Vaginal Infections in Pregnancy: Bacterial Vaginosis & Vulvovaginal Candidiasis
# Category: Pregnancy illness | Subcategory: Genital/reproductive infection
# Last reviewed: 2025

---

## OVERVIEW

Vaginal infections are among the most common conditions in pregnancy globally and in Nigeria. This file covers two principal non-sexually-transmitted vaginal infections presenting in pregnancy:

**Bacterial Vaginosis (BV):** Dysbiosis of normal vaginal flora with overgrowth of anaerobic organisms (Gardnerella vaginalis, Mobiluncus, Prevotella). BV is not a sexually transmitted infection but is associated with sexual activity. Prevalence in pregnant Nigerian women is high — estimated 20–40% in various studies. BV in pregnancy is clinically important because it is associated with preterm birth, preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), and postpartum endometritis.

**Vulvovaginal Candidiasis (VVC):** Overgrowth of Candida species (predominantly C. albicans) in the vagina. Pregnancy is a major predisposing factor — rising oestrogen and glycogen promote Candida growth. Prevalence increases with gestational age. VVC causes significant discomfort but is not directly associated with preterm birth or fetal harm in uncomplicated cases. Neonatal oral thrush and candidal nappy rash can occur if untreated at delivery.

**Why it matters for UMMA:** Both conditions are underdiagnosed and undertreated in northern Nigeria because women are reluctant to discuss vaginal symptoms. Vaginal discharge is commonly attributed to normal pregnancy, evil eye, or spiritual causes. BV in particular carries real obstetric risk that can be mitigated by treatment. UMMA must normalise discussion, enable symptom recognition, and direct to ANC assessment without causing shame.

**Common delays and barriers:**
- Cultural shame around vaginal symptoms — women may not report to ANC providers
- Self-treatment with herbal douching (common in northern Nigeria) — disrupts flora further, worsens BV
- Confusion with normal physiological vaginal discharge of pregnancy
- PMV provision of incorrect antifungals (oral fluconazole — contraindicated in first trimester) or antibiotics without diagnosis
- Partners may discourage hospital attendance for "private" symptoms

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Bacterial Vaginosis

**Mild Presentation:**
- Thin, homogeneous, grey-white or off-white vaginal discharge
- Discharge may be increased in volume compared to pre-pregnancy baseline
- Characteristic fishy or ammoniacal odour — often more noticeable after intercourse or during menstruation (though the latter not applicable in pregnancy)
- Minimal or no vulvar itching — absence of itch helps distinguish BV from candidiasis
- No vulvar redness or soreness
- May be entirely asymptomatic — diagnosed on routine ANC swab or Whiff test

**Moderate Presentation:**
- More pronounced discharge — coating the vaginal walls
- Odour significant enough to cause social embarrassment
- Mild vulvar irritation (secondary — not primary feature)
- No fever, no pelvic pain — if these are present, consider ascending infection (see below)

**Severe Presentation (BV complications):**
BV itself does not present with systemic severity, but its complications do:
- PPROM — sudden gush of clear fluid from vagina at <37 weeks: this is an obstetric emergency; see fetal_distress_pregnancy.md
- Preterm labour — regular painful uterine contractions before 37 weeks
- Signs of ascending infection / chorioamnionitis: fever (>38°C), uterine tenderness, foul-smelling liquor, maternal tachycardia, fetal tachycardia — obstetric emergency

### Vulvovaginal Candidiasis

**Mild Presentation:**
- Vulvar and vaginal itching — the dominant symptom; often intense
- White, thick, curdy or "cottage cheese" discharge — characteristically non-offensive (no fishy odour)
- Discharge may be minimal or copious
- Mild vulvar redness
- Dysuria — burning on passing urine (external — from inflamed vulvar skin, not internal dysuria of UTI)
- Dyspareunia — pain during intercourse
- Symptoms often worse at night or after bathing

**Moderate Presentation:**
- Intense pruritus causing scratching and sleep disturbance
- Visible vulvar erythema (redness) and oedema
- Vulvar excoriation from scratching
- Superficial fissuring of vulvar skin
- Thick white plaques adherent to vaginal walls on examination
- Satellite lesions — small red spots beyond main area of inflammation

**Severe Presentation:**
Severe VVC in pregnancy is uncommon but can occur in diabetic or immunocompromised women:
- Extensive vulvar oedema
- Severe fissuring — pain preventing walking or urination
- Secondary bacterial superinfection of excoriated skin
- Note: VVC does not cause systemic illness (no fever, no pelvic pain); if these features present, consider alternative or coexisting diagnosis

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**BV vs VVC — key distinguishing features:**

| Feature | BV | VVC |
|---|---|---|
| Discharge colour | Grey-white, homogeneous | White, thick, curdy |
| Odour | Fishy/ammoniacal | None or yeasty |
| Itch | Absent or minimal | Prominent — dominant symptom |
| Vulvar redness | Absent | Present |
| pH (if tested) | >4.5 | <4.5 |

**BV vs Physiological discharge of pregnancy:**
- Physiological: white or clear, non-offensive, no itch, increases normally in pregnancy especially second trimester
- BV: grey-white, offensive odour even if mild, Whiff test positive

**BV vs Trichomoniasis:**
- Trichomoniasis: yellow-green, frothy discharge; offensive odour; vulvar itching more prominent than BV; STI — partner treatment required
- BV: grey-white, non-frothy; minimal itch; not primarily STI-driven
- Trichomoniasis in pregnancy also associated with preterm birth — treat if diagnosed

**VVC vs Vulvar dermatitis/contact dermatitis:**
- Contact dermatitis: itching without characteristic white discharge; history of new soap, detergent, sanitary product
- VVC: characteristic discharge present

**VVC vs UTI (both cause dysuria):**
- UTI: internal burning (urethral), frequency, urgency, cloudy/offensive urine, suprapubic pain — no vaginal discharge or vulvar itch
- VVC: external burning (vulvar), characteristic discharge and itch
- Can coexist — both are common in pregnancy

**BV/VVC vs Gonorrhoea/Chlamydia cervicitis:**
- Cervicitis: purulent discharge from cervix, not vaginal walls; pelvic pain; contact bleeding; STI exposure
- Requires ANC assessment if cervicitis is suspected

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
**BV:** Symptomatic discharge with odour; no obstetric complications; no fever or pelvic pain
**VVC:** Vulvar itching with characteristic discharge; mild redness; no fissuring; no secondary infection

### Moderate criteria
**BV:** More pronounced symptoms; any history of previous preterm birth (makes BV higher risk in this pregnancy); or BV diagnosed in second trimester with no prior treatment
**VVC:** Significant pruritus affecting sleep; visible excoriation; oedema; pregnant woman in third trimester (neonatal transmission risk increases)

### Severe criteria — requires same-day ANC or hospital assessment
**BV complications:**
- Any signs of preterm labour (regular contractions before 37 weeks)
- Sudden fluid gush (possible PPROM)
- Fever + uterine tenderness (chorioamnionitis)

**VVC complications:**
- Severe vulvar oedema or fissuring causing inability to urinate
- Signs of secondary bacterial infection (pus, increasing pain, fever)
- Extensive skin breakdown

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild — BV
UMMA should advise:
- Attend ANC as soon as possible — within the next 1–2 days — for assessment and treatment prescription
- Treatment is metronidazole (oral or vaginal gel) — ANC clinician to prescribe and advise on trimester-appropriate regimen
- Do not self-treat with herbal remedies or douches — these worsen BV by further disrupting vaginal flora
- Partner treatment is not required for BV
- Complete the full course of treatment even if symptoms improve
- Avoid vaginal douching at all times during pregnancy
- Monitor for any uterine tightening or fluid loss — return immediately if these occur

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"The symptoms you are describing — a grey-white discharge with a fishy smell — can be a vaginal infection called bacterial vaginosis. This is common in pregnancy and can be treated with medicine from the clinic. Please go to your antenatal clinic in the next day or two. Do not use any local herbs or rinse inside the vagina — this makes it worse. The medicine is safe in pregnancy when the clinic prescribes it. If you feel any tightening in your belly or fluid comes out suddenly, go to the hospital straight away."

### Mild — VVC
UMMA should advise:
- Attend ANC or clinic for assessment — diagnosis should be confirmed before treatment
- Treatment is topical antifungal (clotrimazole pessary or cream) — safe throughout pregnancy; oral fluconazole should be avoided especially in first trimester
- Keep vulvar area clean and dry — cotton underwear; avoid tight clothing
- Avoid soap directly inside the vagina — rinse with clean water only
- Do not scratch — causes skin breakdown and secondary infection
- Symptoms usually begin to improve within 2–3 days of treatment

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"The itching and white, thick discharge you have is likely a fungal infection — very common in pregnancy because of hormone changes. It is not dangerous to your baby but it is uncomfortable. The clinic can give you a pessary (medicine to put inside the vagina) which is safe in pregnancy. Do not take any tablet for it without the clinic prescribing it — some tablets for this infection are not safe in early pregnancy. Wear loose, cotton underwear and do not scratch."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- ANC or clinic attendance same day — especially if BV in a woman with previous preterm birth, or VVC with significant skin breakdown
- For BV in second trimester or history of preterm labour: emphasise to clinician the need for treatment and preterm birth risk discussion
- For VVC with excoriation: clinician may prescribe topical steroid alongside antifungal for comfort

### Severe — BV complications / VVC complications
- Preterm labour signs or PPROM: obstetric emergency — go to hospital immediately (see fetal_distress_pregnancy.md)
- Fever + uterine tenderness: emergency — possible chorioamnionitis — go to hospital immediately
- VVC with urinary retention: same-day hospital assessment

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt (BV complication):**
"If you are feeling regular tightening in your belly, or if fluid has come out suddenly from below, you need to go to the hospital right now — do not wait. This could mean the baby is coming too early. Tell the doctors you also have a vaginal infection."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Vaginal douching — extremely common in northern Nigeria; women use water, soap, herbal solutions, or antiseptics inserted vaginally; this strips protective lactobacilli and dramatically worsens BV and predisposes to ascending infection
- Herbal vaginal pessaries and "tightening" preparations — used culturally; contain caustic substances; cause chemical burns and vaginal ulceration
- Oral fluconazole (Diflucan) purchased from PMV — widely sold; teratogenic risk in first trimester at repeated/high doses; should not be used in pregnancy without prescription
- Misuse of metronidazole in first trimester — some hesitation warranted; ANC clinician should decide on trimester-appropriate regimen
- Self-treatment with leftover antibiotics — worsens dysbiosis without treating the correct organisms

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Whiff test and microscopy for BV diagnosis not reliably available at PHC level — many ANC diagnose clinically
- Clotrimazole pessaries generally available at PHC and pharmacy level
- Metronidazole available at PHC level — affordable and accessible
- STI co-testing (gonorrhoea, chlamydia, trichomoniasis) not routinely available at PHC — send to secondary care if suspected

**Cultural factors:**
- Vaginal discharge is a deeply private topic — UMMA must approach with sensitivity and explicitly normalise reporting symptoms
- Discharge may be attributed to supernatural causes or to the pregnancy itself — UMMA should validate that some discharge is normal in pregnancy while helping the woman distinguish abnormal symptoms
- Stigma: some women fear their husband will assume infidelity if told they have a vaginal infection — UMMA should clarify clearly that BV and VVC are not sexually transmitted and are extremely common in pregnancy
- Female ANC providers preferred — where available, UMMA should acknowledge this preference and reassure that the woman can request a female clinician

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Women with previous preterm birth — BV in this pregnancy carries higher risk of repeat preterm birth; prioritise treatment
- Women with gestational diabetes or pre-existing diabetes — higher risk of recurrent VVC; more severe presentations
- HIV-positive women — higher rates of both BV and VVC; recurrent and treatment-resistant VVC more common
- Women in third trimester — VVC at time of delivery increases neonatal oral thrush risk; treat before delivery
- Adolescent pregnant women — higher rates of BV; less likely to attend ANC; less likely to report symptoms
