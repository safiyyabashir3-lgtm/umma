# UMMA Disease File — Asthma in Pregnancy
# Category: Pregnancy illness | Subcategory: Respiratory illness
# Last reviewed: 2025

## OVERVIEW

Asthma is the most common chronic respiratory condition in pregnancy globally. It affects an estimated 4–8% of pregnant women. Asthma in pregnancy follows a rule of thirds: approximately one third of women improve during pregnancy, one third remain the same, and one third worsen — with worsening most common in weeks 24–36 and severe exacerbations more likely in those with poorly controlled pre-pregnancy asthma.

In Kano and northwest Nigeria, asthma prevalence has been rising, driven by urbanisation, increasing air pollution (vehicular exhaust, dust, industrial burning), and harmattan dust exposure. Many women do not know they have asthma — they describe recurrent episodes of "cough" or "chest tightness" that have been treated repeatedly with antibiotics without diagnosis.

Uncontrolled asthma in pregnancy is associated with preterm birth, low birth weight, pre-eclampsia, and in severe cases maternal death from hypoxia. Conversely, well-controlled asthma carries minimal pregnancy risk. The key clinical message is that asthma medications — including inhaled corticosteroids and salbutamol — are safe in pregnancy, and that the risks of uncontrolled asthma far exceed the risks of treatment.

**Why it matters for UMMA:** Women in northern Nigeria with asthma often do not have a diagnosis, do not have inhalers, and do not know what triggers their symptoms. Exacerbations during pregnancy are life-threatening if severe. UMMA must recognise asthma, classify exacerbation severity, and direct appropriately — including helping women advocate for proper diagnosis and inhaler access at ANC.

**Common delays and barriers:**
- Lack of diagnosis — symptoms attributed to "cold," "dust," or "cough illness"
- No access to inhalers — salbutamol (Ventolin) inhalers available in Kano at higher-level facilities and some pharmacies but not universally; some women cannot afford or access them
- Fear that inhalers or steroids harm the baby — leads to stopping medication in pregnancy
- Harmattan season dramatically increases exacerbation risk; no avoidance strategies known to women
- Exacerbations triggered by cooking smoke, incense burning during pregnancy ceremonies
- PMV provision of antibiotics and cough syrups instead of bronchodilators

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation — Mild exacerbation or mild persistent asthma
- Episodic wheeze — expiratory, may be audible to woman herself
- Chest tightness — woman describes "heaviness" or something "sitting on the chest"
- Cough — often dry, nocturnal, or early morning; this pattern is characteristic
- Breathlessness with moderate exertion (climbing stairs, walking fast)
- Symptoms triggered by specific exposures: harmattan dust, smoke, strong smells, cold air, respiratory infections
- Symptoms variable — better on some days, worse on others
- Woman can speak normally; no use of accessory muscles
- Respiratory rate normal or mildly elevated
- Symptoms respond to salbutamol inhaler (if available) within 20–30 minutes

### Moderate Presentation — Moderate exacerbation
- Breathlessness at rest or with minimal exertion
- Wheeze audible without stethoscope
- Respiratory rate >20/minute at rest
- Woman can speak in phrases but not full sentences without stopping
- Use of accessory muscles (neck, intercostal) visible
- Chest tightness described as severe
- Nocturnal waking from breathlessness or cough — frequent
- Partial response to salbutamol — improvement but incomplete
- Fetal movement may be noted as reduced (maternal hypoxia reduces fetal oxygenation)
- Tachycardia (heart rate >100/minute)
- No cyanosis at rest

### Severe Presentation — Severe exacerbation (EMERGENCY)
- Respiratory rate >30/minute
- Cannot complete sentences — one or two words only between breaths
- Cyanosis — blue lips, tongue, fingernails
- SpO2 <92% (where measured)
- Severe accessory muscle use — visible strain in neck and chest
- Paradoxical breathing (abdomen and chest move in opposite directions)
- Altered consciousness — agitation, drowsiness, confusion (from hypoxia)
- Silent chest on auscultation — wheeze disappears because airflow is too low to generate it; this is a pre-arrest sign, not improvement
- Pulsus paradoxus (>25 mmHg drop in systolic BP with inspiration) — requires measurement equipment; rare to assess in field
- No response to repeated salbutamol doses
- Heart rate >120/minute or bradycardia (late, pre-arrest sign)

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Asthma vs Pneumonia (very important in pregnancy):**
- Asthma: wheeze prominent and bilateral; known history; triggered by exposures; no fever (unless infection triggers exacerbation); responds to bronchodilator; no purulent sputum
- Pneumonia: fever; purulent cough; focal signs; no wheeze or less prominent wheeze; does not respond to bronchodilator
- CRITICAL: Respiratory infection frequently triggers asthma exacerbation — asthma + pneumonia can coexist; treat both if both are present

**Asthma vs Cardiac failure / Pulmonary oedema:**
- Cardiac failure: bilateral basal crackles; ankle oedema; orthopnoea; no wheeze usually; elevated JVP; no history of atopy
- Asthma: wheeze; atopic history; responds to bronchodilator; no oedema
- Cardiac asthma (wheeze from pulmonary oedema) — occurs in severe cardiac failure; rare in young pregnant women unless known CHD

**Asthma vs Pulmonary Embolism:**
- PE: sudden breathlessness WITHOUT wheeze; pleuritic pain; no response to bronchodilator; no history of asthma
- Asthma: wheeze; history; trigger

**Asthma vs Physiological breathlessness of pregnancy:**
- Physiological: gradual; exertional; no wheeze; no cough; no nocturnal symptoms; no history of similar episodes; improves with rest
- Asthma: episodic; wheeze; triggered; nocturnal; history of similar

**Asthma vs Vocal cord dysfunction (VCD):**
- VCD: inspiratory stridor rather than expiratory wheeze; does not respond to salbutamol; psychogenic trigger; uncommon but under-recognised
- Distinguish clinically at hospital level; UMMA need not distinguish — refer

**Features making asthma more likely:**
- Previous episodes of wheeze, chest tightness, or "cough" triggered by dust, smoke, or infections
- Family history of asthma or atopic conditions (eczema, hay fever)
- Clear episodic pattern with symptom-free intervals
- Harmattan season
- Exposure to household cooking smoke, incense, dust
- Previous response to salbutamol or bronchodilator

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Intermittent or mild persistent symptoms
- Can speak normally
- Respiratory rate normal or mildly elevated
- Wheeze present but no distress
- Responds well to salbutamol
- Fetal movements normal

### Moderate criteria — needs same-day clinical assessment
- Breathless at rest or minimal exertion
- Speaking in phrases only
- Respiratory rate >20/minute
- Partial response to salbutamol
- Nocturnal symptoms disturbing sleep
- Fetal movements possibly reduced
- Not improving with home management

### Severe criteria — emergency, ANY ONE
- Respiratory rate >30/minute
- Cannot speak in phrases — words only
- Cyanosis
- SpO2 <92%
- Silent chest (wheeze disappears in woman who was wheezing)
- Altered consciousness
- No response to salbutamol after 2–3 doses
- Heart rate >120 or falling (bradycardia = pre-arrest)
- Signs of preterm labour concurrent with severe exacerbation

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- If woman has her own salbutamol inhaler: use as directed; 2–4 puffs via spacer (or directly); repeat in 20 minutes if incomplete response
- Attend ANC or clinic this week for review — mild but persistent asthma in pregnancy needs regular assessment
- If no inhaler access: attend clinic today to obtain one — do not manage asthma in pregnancy without a bronchodilator available
- Identify and avoid triggers: cooking smoke (cook outside or improve ventilation), incense, dust — cover nose with clean cloth during harmattan
- Do not stop inhaled corticosteroids if prescribed — these are safe in pregnancy and essential for control
- Paracetamol for fever if respiratory infection is a trigger
- Monitor fetal movements — if reduced, attend hospital
- If symptoms worsen over 24 hours, attend hospital

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"You have signs of asthma. This can be managed safely in pregnancy — the inhaler is not harmful to your baby. In fact, not treating your asthma is more dangerous for the baby than using the inhaler. If you have a Ventolin inhaler, use it now as directed. Please go to the clinic this week to get a proper review. During harmattan, try to stay indoors when it is very dusty, and keep cooking smoke away from you. If your breathing gets worse, use the inhaler again and go to the hospital."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Use salbutamol inhaler immediately — up to 4 puffs; repeat every 20 minutes up to 3 times
- If partial response — go to hospital or clinic same day for nebulisation and assessment
- If no inhaler available — go to hospital now; do not wait
- Do not take oral steroids without prescription
- Keep sitting upright — do not lie flat
- Monitor fetal movements — reduced movements with breathing difficulty means the baby may not be getting enough oxygen; go to hospital
- Inform hospital of gestational age and any medications currently taken

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your asthma is flaring up and your breathing is more difficult than usual. Use your Ventolin inhaler now — four puffs — and sit up. If it helps after 20 minutes, use it again. But if you are still struggling to breathe properly, you need to go to the clinic or hospital today — they can give you stronger treatment through a breathing machine. Also check whether your baby is moving — if you have not felt the baby move in the last hour, go straight to the hospital."

### Severe
UMMA should advise:
- Emergency hospital immediately — AKTH or nearest general hospital with obstetric care
- Use salbutamol inhaler continuously on the way if available — this is safe
- Do not lie flat — remain sitting upright
- If lips or fingertips turn blue — this is a critical emergency; go as fast as possible
- If chest becomes silent (wheeze stops but breathing still very difficult) — this is worse not better; go immediately
- Call ahead: pregnant woman with severe asthma attack
- At hospital: IV hydrocortisone, nebulised salbutamol, oxygen, fetal monitoring all required

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"This is an emergency. Your breathing is very bad and your baby may not be getting enough oxygen. Go to the hospital right now — Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital or the nearest general hospital. Keep using your inhaler on the way if you have it. Stay sitting up. If your lips turn blue or the wheezing sound stops, that means it is getting much worse — get there as fast as you can. Tell the doctors you are pregnant and having a severe asthma attack."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Stopping asthma inhalers in pregnancy due to fear of harming the baby — the single most common and dangerous error; salbutamol and inhaled corticosteroids are safe in pregnancy; stopping them causes dangerous exacerbations
- Repeated antibiotic courses for "cough" without asthma diagnosis — delays proper management for years; very common in Kano
- Burning incense during pregnancy naming ceremonies and household prayers — a significant asthma trigger; culturally sensitive but important to discuss
- Herbal steam inhalations — hot steam can trigger bronchospasm in asthmatic women
- Using oral aminophylline tablets from PMV without supervision — narrow therapeutic window; toxicity causes vomiting, seizures; not first-line; do not use without clinician guidance in pregnancy
- Chlorphenamine (antihistamine) for wheeze — does not treat bronchospasm; sedating; not appropriate

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Salbutamol inhalers and spacers: available at general hospitals and some PHCs in Kano; not always in stock; families may need to purchase at private pharmacy
- Nebulisation: available at most general hospitals; some PHCs in Kano have nebulisers with electricity supply
- Peak flow meters: rarely available in clinical settings in Kano — severity assessment is clinical
- Inhaled corticosteroids (beclomethasone, budesonide): available at secondary/tertiary care; limited availability at PHC level
- Spirometry: tertiary care only (AKTH)
- IV hydrocortisone and magnesium sulphate (for severe exacerbation): available at general hospitals and AKTH

**Cultural factors:**
- Harmattan and cultural obligations: the harmattan season coincides with major naming ceremonies, outdoor gatherings, and travel — all high-trigger contexts for asthmatic women; UMMA should help women plan ahead for these events
- Perfume and strong smells: very heavily fragranced soaps, perfumes, and incense are common triggers; sensitively raise this
- Prayer and mosque/church gatherings: incense burning in some congregations; poorly ventilated crowd spaces — discuss management strategies without dismissing religious practice
- Women taking herbal remedies to "strengthen" the pregnancy may unknowingly receive substances (e.g., camphor-containing preparations) that trigger bronchospasm

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Women with severe or uncontrolled pre-pregnancy asthma — highest risk of severe exacerbation in pregnancy
- Women in weeks 24–36 — peak period for asthma worsening in pregnancy
- Women with concurrent respiratory infection — viral infections are the single most common trigger for severe asthma exacerbation
- Smokers (active or passive) — tobacco smoke is a major trigger and worsens both asthma and fetal outcomes; household smoking exposure very common in Kano
- Women with atopy (eczema, allergic rhinitis) — more likely to have severe asthma
- Women who stopped inhaled corticosteroids on becoming pregnant — highest risk group; need urgent re-initiation
