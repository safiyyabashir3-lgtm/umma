# HEAT RASH (MILIARIA) — CHILD (Under 5)
*UMMA Disease File | Paediatric Category*

---

## OVERVIEW

Heat rash (miliaria) is caused by obstruction of sweat ducts leading to sweat leakage into the skin layers. It is one of the most common dermatological conditions in children under 5 in Kano and across northwest Nigeria, given the extreme heat, high humidity (particularly during and after rains), and cultural practices of heavy clothing/wrapping of infants. It is a benign condition in its simple form but is clinically important because:
1. It is frequently the entry point for secondary bacterial skin infections (impetigo, cellulitis)
2. It is often confused with viral exanthems, chickenpox, or allergic reactions — leading to inappropriate treatment
3. Infants are at higher risk of heat illness (heat exhaustion, heat stroke) in the same environmental conditions

**Three types relevant to this population:**
- *Miliaria crystallina*: most superficial, tiny clear vesicles, neonates and young infants mainly
- *Miliaria rubra* (prickly heat): most common, red papules/vesicles, itchy/burning, all ages
- *Miliaria profunda*: deepest, flesh-coloured papules, less common, seen after prolonged heat exposure — impairs sweating and can predispose to heat stroke

**Epidemiology (Kano/Northwest Nigeria):**
- Peak in hot dry season (March–June) and hot humid post-rain period
- Affects the majority of infants and young children at some point — near-universal in this climate
- Neonates particularly affected due to immature sweat gland function
- Exacerbated by heavy nappy use, polyester clothing, multiple layers of wrapping

**Why it matters for UMMA:**
- Most mothers can manage mild heat rash at home with correct advice
- Misidentification and wrong treatment (especially heavy creams/oils making it worse) leads to complications
- Secondary bacterial infection is the major risk — UMMA must screen for signs of infection at every heat rash assessment
- Overheating risk: heat rash indicates the child is too hot — this is the opportunity to counsel on infant overheating and safe sleep

**Common delays/barriers:**
- Belief that babies must be kept warm and heavily wrapped at all times — persists even in hot weather
- Application of shea butter, Vaseline, or heavy creams in response to any skin condition — worsens miliaria
- Confusion with measles/chickenpox leading to unnecessary healthcare visits or treatments

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation (Miliaria crystallina / early miliaria rubra)
- Tiny, clear, superficial fluid-filled vesicles like water droplets on normal-looking skin; burst very easily
- Or: Small (1–2mm) red papules and papulovesicles on a background of normal skin
- Distribution: forehead, scalp, neck, upper trunk, skin folds (axilla, groin, behind knees, neck folds in chubby infants)
- Child is comfortable, afebrile, feeding normally
- Rash appears/worsens in heat, improves in cool environment
- No crusting, no pustules, no spreading redness beyond rash margins
- Mother may report child seems itchy or uncomfortable when hot

### Moderate Presentation (Miliaria rubra — extensive / miliaria profunda)
- More widespread red papulovesicular rash covering large body surface area
- Child is clearly irritable and itchy — scratching visibly
- Skin folds may show early maceration (wet, white, softened skin)
- Low-grade fever possible (37.5–38°C) — due to impaired heat dissipation, not infection
- No honey-coloured crusting, no pustules, no warmth/swelling beyond the rash itself
- Rash not improving despite reasonable measures at home
- Miliaria profunda: flesh-coloured, flat-topped papules particularly on trunk; child may seem to sweat less than expected — concerning for heat accumulation

### Severe Presentation (Complicated heat rash)
- **Secondary bacterial infection (see skin_infections_child.md):** Pustules developing within or around heat rash; honey-coloured crusting; localised areas of increased redness, warmth, swelling; child febrile > 38°C; becoming irritable beyond normal
- **Heat exhaustion overlapping:** Child with extensive miliaria profunda + hot environment + signs of heat stress: irritability, decreased urine output, dry mouth, sunken fontanelle, increased respiratory rate
- **Heat stroke (emergency):** Child with miliaria profunda + high core temperature + confusion/altered consciousness + absent or very reduced sweating — this is a medical emergency

*Note: Uncomplicated heat rash alone does not cause severe illness. Severe = heat rash + complication.*

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Heat rash vs chickenpox (varicella):**
- Chickenpox: lesions in different stages simultaneously (papules, vesicles, pustules, crusts all present at once); starts on trunk/face and spreads centrifugally; child unwell with fever; each lesion on its own red base ("dewdrop on a rose petal"); painful/very itchy
- Heat rash: uniform small vesicles or papules; no crust-vesicle-pustule progression; child well; rash in heat-exposed/covered areas; improves with cooling; no individual lesion on red base

**Heat rash vs measles:**
- Measles: starts behind ears/face, spreads downward; preceded by fever, cough, runny nose, red eyes (Koplik spots on buccal mucosa early); rash is blotchy, confluent, not vesicular; child very unwell
- Heat rash: no prodrome, no Koplik spots, vesicular/papular not blotchy, improves with cooling

**Heat rash vs allergic contact dermatitis / urticaria:**
- Urticaria: wheals (raised, blanching, transient); moves around body; specific trigger often identifiable; dramatic itch
- Contact dermatitis: follows contact pattern (elastic band, clothing seams, jewellery); no relationship to heat specifically

**Heat rash vs impetigo:**
- Impetigo: honey-coloured crust is key; vesicles rupture and crust over; very contagious; responds to antibiotics
- Heat rash: no crusting; resolves with cooling alone

**Heat rash vs neonatal acne / erythema toxicum:**
- Neonatal acne: comedones, papules on face in first weeks; not related to heat
- Erythema toxicum: seen in first days of life; fleeting yellow-white pustules on red base; resolves spontaneously; eosinophils on smear if tested
- Heat rash: appears with heat exposure; distribution follows covered/sweaty areas

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild — all of the following:
- Small, localised, or moderate heat rash
- Child afebrile and comfortable
- No pustules, no crusting, no spreading erythema
- Feeding and activity normal
- Rash location: forehead, scalp, neck, trunk — not generalised

### Moderate — any of the following:
- Extensive rash covering most of trunk/limbs
- Child very irritable with significant scratching
- Low-grade fever (37.5–38°C) without signs of infection
- Miliaria profunda pattern (flesh-coloured deep papules, reduced sweating)
- Skin fold maceration developing
- Not improving with 48 hours of home cooling measures

### Severe — any ONE = requires urgent attention:
- Signs of secondary bacterial infection (fever > 38°C, pustules, crusting, spreading warmth/redness)
- Signs of heat exhaustion: reduced urine output, dry mouth, sunken fontanelle, fast breathing in hot environment
- Any sign of heat stroke: altered consciousness, very high temperature, absent sweating — EMERGENCY
- Neonate (< 4 weeks) with extensive heat rash and any systemic features — EMERGENCY

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild

**Clinical actions (primarily home management):**
- Cool the child: remove excess clothing, dress in single layer of loose lightweight cotton
- Bathe in cool (not cold) water 1–2 times daily — allow skin to air dry or pat gently
- Keep environment cool: move to shaded, ventilated area; fan is helpful; avoid direct sun between 10am–4pm
- No creams, oils, or powders — all worsen miliaria by blocking pores further (this is the most important instruction)
- Keep skin folds clean and dry; change nappy frequently
- Breastfeed frequently to maintain hydration
- Rash should resolve within 24–48 hours of effective cooling
- If not resolved in 3 days or appears infected: attend clinic

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"Your baby has prickly heat — this is very common in our hot weather and it's not dangerous on its own. The most important thing is to keep your baby cool. Remove extra layers of clothing and dress your baby in thin cotton. Give your baby cool baths. Do not put any cream, oil, shea butter, or powder on the rash — this will make it worse by blocking the skin. Make sure the baby is in a cool, shady place. The rash should go away in 1–2 days if you do this. Watch carefully for any yellow-crusted spots, pus, or spreading redness — that means the skin has become infected and needs to be seen at a clinic."

---

### Moderate

**Clinical actions:**
- All above cooling measures — emphasise strongly
- Clinic review if: not improving in 48–72 hours despite cooling, or any doubt about secondary infection
- Calamine lotion (if available) for symptomatic relief — safe in this context
- Hydrocortisone 1% cream for short course only if severe dermatitis component — clinic prescription, avoid in skin folds
- Screen carefully for signs of early secondary infection at clinic visit
- Nutritional assessment if child also malnourished

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"Your child's prickly heat is quite bad right now and they seem very uncomfortable. Keep doing the cooling steps — cool baths, light clothing, cool room. If you have calamine lotion, you can apply it lightly to help with the itching. Do not use other creams. If the rash is not getting better in the next 2 days, or if you see any spots with yellow crusts or pus forming, please take your child to the clinic. Also watch for your child seeming very tired, not passing urine, or seeming confused — go straight to hospital if that happens."

---

### Severe (Complicated)

**Clinical actions:**
- Secondary infection: treat as per skin_infections_child.md (moderate or severe)
- Heat exhaustion: immediate cooling, oral rehydration, clinic same day
- Heat stroke: EMERGENCY — cool immediately (remove clothing, cool water on skin, fan), rush to hospital with IV access needed

**Plain language for UMMA:**
[If secondary infection suspected]:
"Some of your child's heat rash spots now look infected — there's pus or spreading redness. This needs a clinic visit today to get the right medicine. Keep cooling the child while you prepare to go."

[If heat stroke/exhaustion signs]:
"Your child is dangerously overheated. Take off all their clothing right now. Put cool water on their skin and fan them. You need to get to the hospital immediately — this is an emergency. Go now."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Heavy swaddling and multiple clothing layers on neonates and infants regardless of ambient temperature — extremely common in Kano and can cause dangerous overheating; cultural belief that babies must be kept warm persists even in 40°C heat
- Application of shea butter (man shanu), Vaseline, baby oil, or olive oil to "soothe" skin rash — directly worsens miliaria by blocking eccrine sweat ducts
- Talcum powder application — mixed evidence but inhalation risk in infants; does not treat heat rash
- "Gishiri" (rock salt) water bathing — can cause skin irritation and electrolyte disturbance in neonates
- PMV-dispensed antihistamines (e.g. promethazine/phenergan) given to infants for itching — promethazine is contraindicated under 2 years due to respiratory depression risk; must be flagged strongly

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Air conditioning unavailable for the vast majority of families — practical cooling advice must focus on: shade, ventilation, cool water bathing, lightweight clothing, avoiding peak sun
- Calamine lotion is available but not always in stock at PHCs — available in most markets and some chemists
- Heat stroke requires IV fluid cooling and monitoring — requires hospital with paediatric capacity

**Cultural factors:**
- Deeply held belief that cold exposure causes illness in infants — "the cold will enter the baby" — means mothers resist removing clothing even when advised; UMMA should acknowledge this concern and explain that brief cooling bathing and light clothing will not harm the baby, especially when heat rash is present
- Extended family (particularly grandmothers) often make clothing decisions — advice may need to reach the extended family, not just the mother
- Religious practices: in some households, windows are kept shut and rooms maintained warmer than ambient — ventilation counselling needed

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Neonates under 4 weeks: immature thermoregulation, cannot regulate temperature effectively, highest risk of heat stroke from overswaddling
- Exclusively formula-fed infants in hot weather: higher risk of dehydration if feeds are not increased in heat
- Malnourished children: poor skin barrier, impaired sweating mechanism
- Children with eczema: heat rash and eczema frequently coexist; heavy moisturisers for eczema worsen miliaria — need to distinguish which is predominant
