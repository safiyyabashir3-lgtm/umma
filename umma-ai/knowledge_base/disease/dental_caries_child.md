# DENTAL CARIES (TOOTH DECAY) — CHILD (Under 5)

## OVERVIEW
Dental caries is progressive demineralisation and destruction of tooth structure caused by acid produced by bacteria (predominantly Streptococcus mutans) fermenting dietary sugars. In young children, the pattern is called early childhood caries (ECC) — defined as any carious lesion in a child under 6 years old. In its severe form (severe early childhood caries, S-ECC), it involves the smooth surfaces of anterior teeth in children under 3 and causes rapid, extensive destruction.

In Kano and northwest Nigeria, dental caries in young children is highly prevalent but significantly underreported and underrecognised. Dental health is rarely prioritised in the context of the severe acute disease burden families face. Key risk factors in this population are highly concentrated: frequent consumption of sugary drinks (Fanta, bottled juice, malt drinks given to infants), prolonged bottle use with sweetened liquids, widespread use of sugar in weaning foods, and near-zero access to paediatric dental services.

Dental pain from caries causes significant feeding difficulty, weight faltering, sleep disruption, and developmental impact in young children. Dental abscess — the most serious complication — causes facial swelling, fever, and severe pain, and can progress to life-threatening deep space neck infection or orbital cellulitis.

Teeth affected by caries are primary teeth — their loss matters. Early loss of primary teeth causes feeding difficulty, speech development problems, and loss of space for permanent teeth. The belief that "they are baby teeth — they will fall out" is dangerous and leads to delayed care.

Why it matters for UMMA: Dental caries is a chronic condition that UMMA encounters as a background finding during assessments of feeding difficulty, weight faltering, or facial swelling. Most mothers do not associate their child's irritability, poor feeding, or facial swelling with teeth. UMMA must be able to identify dental pain and abscess, give practical dietary and hygiene advice that is realistic for Kano context, and escalate dental abscess urgently. Paediatric dental services are extremely limited — most families will be directed to adult dentist chairs or general dental officers at secondary level.

Common delays and barriers:
- Dental pain not identified as cause of child's irritability and poor feeding
- Belief that primary (baby) teeth don't matter and don't need treatment
- Paediatric dental services essentially unavailable outside of teaching hospitals
- High cost of dental treatment at private facilities
- Transport barriers to facilities with dental services
- Tooth extraction without addressing infection first — high risk of spread if done while abscess active
- Patent medicine vendor antibiotics given for dental abscess without drainage — inadequate treatment

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
- Visible white spot lesions on tooth surface — earliest sign of demineralisation, chalky white appearance, most commonly on upper front teeth in early childhood caries
- Brown or yellowish-brown discolouration on tooth surface
- No pain at rest; mild sensitivity to sweet foods or very cold/hot items
- Child eating normally but may avoid certain foods
- Upper anterior (front) teeth most commonly affected first in bottle/breastfed children with ECC
- Mother may have noticed "brown marks on teeth"
- No gum swelling, no facial swelling, no fever

### Moderate Presentation
- Visible cavitation (hole) in one or more teeth — may appear as brown/black pit or larger destruction of tooth crown
- Multiple teeth affected
- Pain on eating — child winces, chews on one side, avoids hard foods
- Sleep disturbance from dental pain
- Feeding difficulty beginning — reduced oral intake, irritability at mealtimes
- No swelling of gum or face
- No fever
- Possible mild gum inflammation (gingivitis) around affected teeth — red, swollen gum margin
- In severe ECC: virtually all upper front teeth affected, crowns partially or fully destroyed, teeth appear as brown stumps

### Severe Presentation
- Dental abscess: localised pus collection at root apex — presents as:
  - Tender swelling of gum (parulis — "gum boil") adjacent to affected tooth
  - Facial swelling — cheek, lip, or submandibular region
  - Pus may be visible draining from gum
  - Severe localised pain, child holding face
  - Fever (38°C or above)
  - Child refusing all oral intake due to pain
- Severe facial cellulitis: diffuse facial swelling with skin erythema, warmth, tenderness — infection spreading beyond localised abscess
- Deep space neck infection: swelling extending below jaw to neck, difficulty swallowing, trismus (inability to open mouth fully), voice change — LIFE-THREATENING, airway at risk
- Orbital cellulitis from upper jaw abscess: eye swelling, proptosis, restricted eye movement — very rare but reported
- Sepsis from dental origin: fever, tachycardia, unwell child with dental/facial source

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Dental caries vs normal tooth discolouration:**
- Normal: some teeth erupt with mild surface variation; iron-containing liquid supplements cause harmless black/grey surface staining that wipes off or is non-destructive on enamel surface
- Caries: surface destruction (cavitation), tactile roughness, loss of tooth substance, associated pain

**Dental abscess vs parotitis (parotid gland swelling):**
- Dental abscess: swelling centred on jaw/cheek related to a specific tooth, tender gum, pus may be visible at gum, fever, tooth pain
- Parotitis: swelling anterior to and below ear, obscures angle of jaw, elevated parotid duct opening on inner cheek opposite upper molar, associated with mumps (bilateral parotid swelling) or suppurative parotitis

**Dental abscess vs lymphadenopathy:**
- Submandibular lymph node abscess: discrete node palpable, moves slightly, no specific tooth associated, may follow URTI or skin infection
- Dental abscess: overlying or adjacent to a specific tooth, gum involvement present

**Dental abscess vs facial cellulitis from skin source:**
- Dental origin: palpate gum for tenderness adjacent to a decayed/missing tooth, look for dental history
- Skin origin: wound, insect bite, or skin break as entry point identified, no gum involvement

**Dental abscess vs noma (cancrum oris):**
- Noma: gangrenous, rapidly spreading destruction of soft tissue and bone, characteristic foul smell, dark necrotic tissue, occurs in severely malnourished children, not primarily tooth-centred pain
- Dental abscess: painful, purulent, tooth-specific, no gangrenous tissue

**Features of dental pain in pre-verbal children:**
- Refusing solid foods or specific textures
- Chewing on one side
- Holding or touching face
- Waking from sleep crying (dental pain is worse at night — recumbent position increases pulp pressure)
- Increased drooling (from avoiding swallowing due to pain)
- Irritability and poor feeding attributed to teething incorrectly

**Common coexistences in Nigerian context:**
- Dental caries + severe acute malnutrition — bidirectional relationship; poor nutrition weakens enamel, dental pain reduces intake, worsening malnutrition
- Dental caries + frequent sugar intake (sugar-sweetened beverages, weaning pap with sugar)
- Dental abscess + untreated malaria — fever attribution challenge (both cause fever)
- Dental caries + sickle cell disease — orofacial complications more common; vaso-occlusive episodes can cause jaw pain mimicking dental abscess

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Visible spots or discolouration only — no cavitation
- OR cavitation present with no or minimal pain
- Feeding normal or near-normal
- No gum swelling
- No facial swelling
- No fever

### Moderate criteria
- Visible cavitation in one or more teeth
- Pain affecting feeding or sleep
- Multiple teeth involved
- Gum inflammation (gingivitis) around affected teeth
- No abscess, no facial swelling
- Feeding partially affected — child still taking adequate overall intake
- Severe ECC pattern (multiple crown destructions) without abscess

### Severe criteria — any ONE = severe
- Gum abscess (parulis) — pus collection at gum adjacent to tooth
- Facial swelling — any visible swelling of cheek, lip, or submandibular region with dental origin
- Fever with dental pain source
- Child unable to take any oral intake due to dental pain
- Deep space neck infection features: swelling below jaw extending to neck, difficulty swallowing, trismus, voice change — EMERGENCY
- Orbital involvement from upper jaw abscess
- Systemic signs of sepsis with dental source

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- Take child to a dental clinic or PHC for dental assessment when possible — mild caries can be remineralised or treated with preventive treatment if caught early
- Toothbrushing: start as soon as first tooth erupts. Use a soft baby toothbrush with a small amount of fluoride toothpaste — where fluoride toothpaste is available. Brush twice daily, especially before bed.
- Where fluoride toothpaste is not available: clean teeth with a soft cloth or finger-brush after meals — mechanical removal of food debris
- Diet advice (realistic for Kano context):
  - Reduce sugar-sweetened drinks (Fanta, juice, malt) — especially in feeding bottles or sippy cups at sleep/nap time. Giving these drinks in a bottle at bedtime is the single most destructive habit for young teeth.
  - Water and breast milk are the safest liquids for infants
  - Reduce sugar in weaning pap/porridge — common practice to add large amounts of sugar
  - Avoid giving sugary snacks between meals
- Do not share spoons or test baby food with mother's mouth then give to child — Streptococcus mutans can be transmitted from mother to infant through saliva
- Reassure that primary teeth matter — they affect feeding, speech, and space for adult teeth

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child's teeth show early signs of decay. The good news is that if you make some changes now and get the teeth checked, you can stop it getting worse. Try to clean your child's teeth with a soft cloth or baby toothbrush after meals, especially at night before sleep. The most important thing is to stop giving sweet drinks like Fanta or juice in a bottle — especially at bedtime or nap time. This is the main cause of this kind of tooth decay in young children. Water and breast milk are the best drinks. Try to see a dentist or health worker when you can to have the teeth properly assessed."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Dental assessment is needed — PHC may refer to a general dental officer at secondary hospital (Murtala Muhammad Specialist Hospital, general hospitals in Kano with dental units)
- Pain management: paracetamol syrup for dental pain — appropriate dose for weight, given regularly, not just when pain is severe. Child in dental pain is not eating adequately — pain relief supports feeding.
- Soft diet: offer soft foods that are easier to eat (pap, soup, mashed foods) to maintain nutritional intake while dental treatment is being arranged
- Continue dietary counselling — reduce sugar urgently
- Oral hygiene: cleaning teeth even with cavities is important — reduces bacterial load and pain
- If no dental access available soon, explain that the condition will worsen without treatment and provide escalation instructions
- Child with significant dental pain and poor intake needs weight monitoring

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child's teeth have cavities that are causing pain and making it hard to eat. They need to see a dentist, but while you arrange this, you can give paracetamol syrup (the correct dose for your child's weight) regularly to help with the pain so they can eat more. Offer soft foods like pap, soup, or mashed yam. Stop giving sweet drinks completely — they are making the decay faster. The decay will keep getting worse without dental treatment, so please try to reach a hospital with a dentist as soon as you can."

### Severe
UMMA should advise — EMERGENCY RESPONSE:
- Dental abscess with facial swelling or fever: go to hospital emergency or general hospital with dental unit TODAY — child needs drainage and antibiotics; do not go to patent medicine vendor
- Deep space neck infection (swelling below jaw, difficulty swallowing, cannot open mouth, voice change): go to hospital emergency NOW — this is a life-threatening airway emergency
- While travelling: give paracetamol for pain if available, keep child calm, do not apply heat to swelling (worsens spread)
- Do NOT have tooth extracted by traditional tooth puller or informal provider — extraction without treating active infection can spread bacteria into bloodstream
- At hospital: child will likely need incision and drainage of abscess + systemic antibiotics + tooth extraction once infection controlled
- If child cannot swallow at all: mention this to hospital immediately — may need IV fluids

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child's tooth infection has spread and caused a serious swelling. This needs hospital treatment today — not a chemist or a traditional tooth puller. The swelling must be drained properly and your child needs antibiotics through a drip or injection, not just tablets. [For neck swelling:] The swelling has reached your child's neck and they are having difficulty swallowing/opening their mouth. This is very serious — the infection could block the airway. Go to the emergency department of a hospital right now and tell them your child cannot open their mouth properly / is having difficulty swallowing. Do not delay."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Traditional tooth extraction by non-professionals ("tooth pullers"): widely practised in Kano markets, painful and performed without anaesthesia, high risk of extracting wrong tooth, infection spread, broken root fragments, jaw fracture. In a child with active dental abscess, traditional extraction can cause bacteraemia and sepsis.
- Applying groundnut oil, clove oil, or cut chilli directly to painful tooth: chilli causes severe mucosal burn; clove oil is effective analgesic but can damage soft tissue if applied undiluted
- Teething powders and gels from patent medicine vendors: some contain undisclosed pharmacological agents; teething rarely causes the tooth pain being treated
- Sugar in infant feeding: adding sugar to pap/akamu is almost universal in northern Nigeria — perceived as increasing energy and palatability. This is the primary driver of early childhood caries. Advice must acknowledge this is a normal practice while explaining the harm clearly and practically.
- Bottle feeding with sweetened drinks at bedtime: formula, juice, malt, sweetened water left in bottle — child sleeps with sweetened liquid pooling around teeth. This is the classic cause of "nursing bottle caries" or "baby bottle tooth decay."

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Paediatric dental services: essentially only at AKTH in Kano — extremely limited access
- General dental officers at secondary hospitals (Murtala Muhammad Specialist Hospital): available but long waiting times, equipment often limited
- Most PHCs have no dental services whatsoever
- Dental X-ray: not available at PHC, limited at secondary level — clinical diagnosis is the norm
- Fluoride toothpaste: widely available at pharmacies in Kano city, less available in rural LGAs and may be unaffordable — UMMA should not assume universal access

**Cultural factors:**
- Baby teeth widely seen as disposable — the phrase "they are just milk teeth" is a significant barrier to care-seeking
- Dental pain in a young child may be attributed to teething (irrespective of the child's age) — treatment delayed
- Sugar is a sign of good care in many households — giving a child a sweet drink or sugary pap is an expression of love and financial capability; UMMA must frame reduction advice sensitively
- Some families seek traditional dental care (often painful) at markets before or instead of clinic
- Male children may be taken for dental care more promptly than female children

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Children given sweetened drinks in bottles — especially at night
- Children weaned on high-sugar pap/porridge (very common in Kano)
- Children with severe acute malnutrition — enamel hypoplasia and immune compromise increase caries risk
- Children with sickle cell disease — orofacial complications more common
- Children born preterm — enamel hypoplasia of primary teeth increases caries susceptibility
- Children with no access to fluoride toothpaste
- Children of mothers with high S. mutans burden (heavily decayed maternal teeth) — early colonisation in infant
