# CHOLERA — CHILD
## Disease File for UMMA AI | Maternal and Child Health | Kano/Northwest Nigeria Context

---

## OVERVIEW

Cholera caused by Vibrio cholerae O1 (El Tor biotype) is endemic in Kano and northwest Nigeria, with epidemic surges occurring during and after the rainy season (June–October) and during periods of flooding. Kano's dense urban areas, open drains, pit latrine proximity to water sources, shared boreholes, and practice of buying street food and untreated water create ideal transmission conditions. Children under 5 are at highest risk of death from cholera because they dehydrate faster, have smaller physiological reserves, and deteriorate rapidly.

Epidemic cholera in northwest Nigeria has been declared multiple times in recent years, with high case fatality rates in communities with poor access to oral rehydration therapy and intravenous fluids. Many deaths occur at home or en route to facility because families delay, attempt home treatment, or are unable to access healthcare.

UMMA relevance: During rainy season or when a mother reports multiple family members with watery diarrhoea, cholera must be at the top of the differential. Any child with profuse watery diarrhoea must be treated as potential cholera until proven otherwise — the window between illness onset and death can be less than 12 hours in severe cases.

Common delays:
- Families first use oral salts or herbal remedies at home
- Transport to hospital in rural LGAs is costly and slow
- Night-time onset in a home with no phone or transport is particularly dangerous
- Cholera diagnosis is stigmatised — families may deny or minimise
- Patent medicine vendors give antidiarrhoeals (which are contraindicated in cholera)

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
- Watery diarrhoea, 3–5 loose stools in 24 hours
- Stools may be yellow-green or starting to become paler
- Mild vomiting or nausea
- Child is alert, able to drink, not visibly dry
- Skin turgor normal
- Mucous membranes slightly dry
- Child may be irritable but consolable
- Urine output slightly reduced
- Often at start of illness — first 6–12 hours

### Moderate Presentation
- Frequent watery stools — 6–10 or more in 24 hours
- Stools becoming rice-water in character — pale, watery, slight fishy odour
- Repeated vomiting limiting oral fluid intake
- Visible signs of dehydration: sunken eyes, dry mouth, reduced skin turgor (skin pinch returns slowly — over 1 second)
- Child is restless and irritable but still conscious
- Decreased urine output — mother notices no wet nappies in several hours
- Mild to moderate tachycardia
- Child drinks eagerly when offered ORS
- Fontanelle sunken in infants

### Severe Presentation
- Profuse rice-water stools — continuous outpouring, child cannot hold stool
- Severe dehydration: sunken eyes and fontanelle, very dry or cracked lips, no tears when crying, skin pinch returns very slowly (more than 2 seconds) or skin remains tented
- Child is lethargic, limp, or unconscious
- Unable to drink — too weak or unconscious
- Absent or barely palpable peripheral pulse (radial pulse)
- Tachycardia with hypotension or shock
- Cold peripheries, mottled skin, prolonged capillary refill
- Eyes rolled back, no response to stimulation
- Muscle cramps (older children) — severe electrolyte disturbance
- No urine output for 6 or more hours

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

Cholera vs Rotavirus gastroenteritis:
- Rotavirus: common in under-2s, vomiting prominent and often precedes diarrhoea, watery but typically yellow/green stools, usually epidemic in dry season (November–February)
- Cholera: rice-water stool is pathognomonic, volume of stool is typically much larger, onset can be explosive, epidemic in rainy season, affects all ages in household
- Both cause severe dehydration in under-5s — management of dehydration is the immediate priority regardless of aetiology

Cholera vs Dysentery (Shigella):
- Dysentery: bloody/mucoid stool, fever, tenesmus, lower abdominal cramping
- Cholera: no blood in stool (unless coexisting pathology), profuse watery stool, low or no fever, muscle cramps rather than abdominal tenesmus
- Fever in a child with watery diarrhoea makes cholera less likely — consider mixed infection or Salmonella

Cholera vs ETEC (Traveller's diarrhoea / endemic watery diarrhoea):
- ETEC: clinically indistinguishable in mild-moderate cases; less explosive volume
- In epidemic context, treat as cholera

Cholera vs Intussusception:
- Intussusception: "redcurrant jelly" stool (blood and mucus), colicky severe abdominal pain, palpable sausage-shaped abdominal mass, child draws up legs with pain
- No rice-water stool
- Key distinction — critical to not miss

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild Criteria (Plan A — home ORS)
- Less than 5 stools in 24 hours
- Alert and drinking
- No visible dehydration: normal skin turgor, moist mouth, normal eyes
- Normal or near-normal urine output
- Able to retain oral fluids

### Moderate Criteria (Plan B — supervised ORS at facility)
- 6 or more watery stools in 24 hours
- Some dehydration: sunken eyes, dry mouth, reduced skin turgor, restless/irritable
- Drinks eagerly — not yet unable to drink
- Fontanelle sunken (infants)
- Tachycardia present
- Reduced but not absent urine output

### Severe Criteria — ANY ONE = EMERGENCY (Plan C — IV fluids urgently)
- Signs of severe dehydration: very sunken eyes, skin pinch returns > 2 seconds, cracked lips, absent tears
- Altered consciousness, lethargy, or inability to drink
- Absent or barely palpable radial pulse
- Signs of shock: cold peripheries, hypotension, prolonged capillary refill
- Continuous rice-water stools with inability to maintain oral intake
- Muscle cramps with altered mental status
- Age under 6 months with any dehydration signs (escalate faster)

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- Start oral rehydration salts (ORS) immediately — this is the most important action
- ORS sachets are available at health centres and some patent medicine vendors — the correct one is the low-osmolarity WHO ORS (blue sachet in Nigeria)
- Mix one sachet in 1 litre of clean (boiled and cooled) water — not juice, not Lucozade, not salt-sugar alone unless ORS unavailable
- Give small sips frequently — for children under 2, one teaspoon every 1–2 minutes; for older children, small cups frequently
- Continue breastfeeding throughout
- Do not stop feeding — after rehydration, restart food
- Do not give antidiarrhoeals (Imodium, Flagyl, Buscopan) — these do not help and can harm
- Monitor closely: if stools increase, vomiting worsens, or child becomes more lethargic, go to clinic immediately
- Zinc supplementation (if available) reduces duration and severity of diarrhoea in children

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"Your child has diarrhoea but is still alert and drinking. The most important thing right now is to give ORS — oral rehydration salts. Get a blue ORS sachet and mix it in one litre of clean water that has been boiled and cooled. Give small sips very frequently. Keep breastfeeding. Do not give any medicine to stop the diarrhoea — this can be dangerous. Watch your child carefully. If your child starts to look very tired, the eyes become sunken, or the diarrhoea gets much worse, go to the health centre immediately."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Take child to health centre or hospital now — do not manage at home
- Continue giving ORS during transport — every sip counts
- At facility: supervised ORS rehydration (Plan B), approximately 75ml/kg over 4 hours
- Zinc should be given for 10–14 days
- Antibiotic therapy (doxycycline for older children, azithromycin for under-8s) may be given at facility discretion — advise mother to ask clinician about this
- After 4-hour rehydration, reassess and re-categorise
- Do not leave facility until child is reassessed and dehydration is resolving
- If no improvement or worsening, escalate to IV fluids

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"Your child is showing signs of dehydration — sunken eyes and dry mouth — and needs help at the health centre today. Keep giving ORS on the way. Tell the nurse or doctor your child has had [number] watery stools and has sunken eyes. The health centre can give proper treatment. Please go now."

### Severe
UMMA should advise:
- This is a life-threatening emergency — go to hospital immediately, do not wait
- If child is unconscious, limp, or has no pulse — call for help and go directly to emergency unit
- Do not give anything by mouth to an unconscious child — choking risk
- At hospital: IV Ringer's Lactate (or normal saline) given rapidly — this is the treatment, not oral fluids
- Do not stop at a patent medicine vendor
- Antibiotic therapy will be given at hospital
- Continuous monitoring of pulse, urine output, and consciousness is needed

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"Your child is very sick and may die without immediate treatment. Please go to the hospital emergency unit right now. Do not stop anywhere else. If your child is unconscious, do not try to give water — go immediately. This is an emergency."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Antidiarrhoeals (loperamide/Imodium) sold widely at patent medicine vendors — these slow gut motility and can cause toxic megacolon in cholera; they must not be used
- Salt-sugar solution (home-made ORS) made incorrectly is dangerous — excess salt causes hypernatraemia; UMMA should always direct to pre-packaged ORS sachets
- Herbal preparations for "running stomach" are widely used in Kano — these delay proper rehydration and some contain agents that worsen dehydration
- Metronidazole (Flagyl) is frequently given by PMVs for diarrhoea — not appropriate for cholera and delays effective treatment

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- IV fluid availability can be limited at primary health centres — Ringer's Lactate in particular — families may need to go directly to secondary facility for severe cases
- Blood banking and ICU facilities are only at tertiary facilities (AKTH, specialist hospitals)
- During cholera outbreaks, facilities become overwhelmed — community ORS points (cholera treatment points) may be set up; UMMA should advise families to seek these if available
- Mothers in purdah may require male escort to access care, which delays emergency presentation

**Cultural factors:**
- "Running stomach" (gudun ciki) is not always seen as serious — many families wait 1–2 days hoping it resolves
- Communal food and water sharing means multiple household members are often affected simultaneously — UMMA should ask if others in the home are also sick
- Water from shared boreholes, water tankers, or open wells are common sources — UMMA should advise boiling all water during outbreak periods

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Children under 2 (smallest reserves, fastest deterioration)
- Malnourished children (SAM/MAM) — much faster dehydration, higher case fatality
- Children born to HIV-positive mothers (may have impaired immunity)
- Children in flood-affected communities in Kano (Fagge, Gwale, Dala — low-lying flood-prone LGAs)
- Children in IDP camps or densely crowded settlements
