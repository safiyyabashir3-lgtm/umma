# UMMA Disease File — Bronchiolitis (Child, Under 5)
# Category: Child illness | Subcategory: Respiratory illness
# Last reviewed: 2025

---

## OVERVIEW

Bronchiolitis is an acute viral lower respiratory tract infection causing inflammation and obstruction of the small airways (bronchioles), primarily affecting infants aged 1–12 months, with peak incidence at 2–6 months. It is caused most commonly by Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), with rhinovirus, parainfluenza, and other viruses also contributing. In Kano, bronchiolitis peaks during the harmattan season (November–February) and the early rainy season (April–June) when viral respiratory illnesses circulate.

Bronchiolitis is distinguished from pneumonia by its characteristic wheeze and widespread crackles rather than focal consolidation, and by its typical age group (under 12 months). However, in Nigerian clinical practice, the two conditions overlap and frequently coexist — a child with bronchiolitis can develop secondary bacterial pneumonia.

**Why it matters for UMMA:** Bronchiolitis causes significant distress and feeding difficulty in young infants and can cause life-threatening apnoea, particularly in infants under 6 weeks and premature infants. It is commonly over-treated with antibiotics and bronchodilators that have no proven benefit, and under-recognised in its severe form. UMMA must help families recognise when bronchiolitis becomes severe and requires hospital care.

**Common delays and barriers:**
- Wheeze and respiratory difficulty attributed to teething — very common in Kano
- Purchase of cough syrups from PMVs — no benefit; some harmful
- Antibiotic courses started without effect — families persist with antibiotics rather than escalating care
- Frequent night-time worsening not acted upon until morning
- Feeding difficulty not recognised as a sign of severity
- Premature infants at high risk — may not have been identified as premature or high-risk at community level

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
- Age typically 2–6 months; can occur up to 12 months
- Coryzal prodrome (runny nose, mild fever) for 1–2 days preceding respiratory symptoms — this progression is characteristic
- Cough — initially dry, may become wet-sounding
- Wheeze — expiratory, often audible without stethoscope in severe cases; in mild cases may be heard only on auscultation
- Mildly fast breathing at rest
- Mild feeding difficulty — takes breast or formula more slowly, requires more frequent breaks
- Low-grade fever (often <38.5°C) or no fever
- Mild crackles on auscultation (if available)
- Child alert, maintains some interaction with mother
- SpO2 >95% (where measured)

**Nigerian-specific note:** Many Kano families will describe a "wheezing chest" or note that the child's chest "makes noise" — this is the wheeze and should be taken seriously in a young infant.

### Moderate Presentation
- Persistent fast breathing at rest (≥50 in infant 2–11 months)
- Prominent wheeze — audible to parent or caregiver
- Chest indrawing — subcostal and intercostal recession
- Nasal flaring
- Head bobbing in infants (head nods forward with each breath — sign of severe work of breathing)
- Feeding significantly impaired — infant cannot complete a feed without pausing repeatedly to breathe; feeding time prolonged >30 minutes or feeds significantly reduced in volume
- Irritable, unsettled, difficult to comfort
- Mild cyanosis possible around lips during feeds or crying
- Periods of apnoea (pauses in breathing) — particularly in young infants <3 months; may be subtle (brief pause, colour change)
- Fever may persist or new fever may indicate secondary bacterial superinfection

### Severe Presentation
- Any ONE of:
  - Central cyanosis at rest — lips, tongue, fingertips blue
  - Severe respiratory distress: severe indrawing, grunting at rest, marked nasal flaring
  - Apnoea episodes: pauses in breathing >20 seconds, or shorter pauses with colour change or limpness
  - Inability to feed — child too breathless to suck at all
  - Altered consciousness — not rousable, unresponsive, limp
  - SpO2 <90% at rest (where measured)
  - Severe exhaustion — child has been working very hard to breathe and is now becoming quiet and less responsive (this apparent "improvement" is actually deterioration)

**Nigerian-specific note on apparent improvement:** In bronchiolitis, a young infant who has been very distressed and then becomes quiet and limp is NOT improving — this is respiratory exhaustion and indicates imminent respiratory failure. UMMA must flag this as a danger sign.

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Bronchiolitis vs Pneumonia (most important differential):**
- Bronchiolitis: age under 12 months; wheeze prominent; widespread bilateral crackles; coryzal prodrome; viral picture; RSV season
- Pneumonia: any age; cough prominent; focal signs on auscultation; less wheeze; responds to antibiotics
- KEY DISTINGUISHING FEATURE: Wheeze in bronchiolitis is typically widespread and bilateral; pneumonia gives focal signs on one side
- CRITICAL: Both can coexist — secondary bacterial pneumonia complicates approximately 1 in 4 hospitalised bronchiolitis cases; suspect if fever is high, child deteriorates after initial improvement, or focal signs develop

**Bronchiolitis vs Asthma/Recurrent wheeze:**
- Asthma: typically older child (>12 months; usually >2 years for reliable diagnosis); previous episodes of wheeze; family history of atopy; responds to bronchodilator
- Bronchiolitis: usually first episode; under 12 months; does not reliably respond to bronchodilator
- Note: Some infants who have bronchiolitis go on to develop recurrent wheeze — first episode bronchiolitis and early asthma can be difficult to distinguish in infants

**Bronchiolitis vs Congenital heart disease:**
- CHD: failure to thrive; poor feeding from birth (not just this episode); murmur; hepatomegaly; cyanosis from birth or early infancy
- Bronchiolitis: previously well infant; acute deterioration; wheeze; coryzal prodrome
- CHD child with bronchiolitis: very high risk — decompensates rapidly; requires immediate hospital referral

**Bronchiolitis vs Foreign body inhalation:**
- Foreign body: sudden onset; often no fever or prodrome; unilateral wheeze or reduced air entry; no coryzal history
- Bronchiolitis: bilateral wheeze; gradual onset after coryzal illness

**Bronchiolitis vs Pertussis:**
- Pertussis: paroxysmal cough with whoop or cyanosis; post-tussive vomiting; apnoea in infants; prolonged illness; contact history; unimmunised or incompletely immunised
- Bronchiolitis: wheeze more prominent; coryzal prodrome; typical RSV season

**Bronchiolitis vs Malaria (fever + fast breathing):**
- Fast breathing from malaria fever resolves after paracetamol — bronchiolitis fast breathing persists
- Malaria in infants under 3 months is rare — bronchiolitis is more likely in this age group
- Both can coexist — always consider malaria in any febrile infant in Kano

**Features making bronchiolitis more likely:**
- Age 2–6 months (peak)
- Coryzal illness in preceding days
- Wheeze audible to parent
- Harmattan season or early rainy season
- Sibling or household member with viral respiratory illness
- Child was premature or low birth weight

**Features making bronchiolitis less likely:**
- Age over 12 months (consider asthma or pneumonia)
- No wheeze
- Focal chest signs
- High fever >39°C with no response — bacterial cause more likely
- Previous episodes responding to bronchodilators

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Fast breathing that is mild
- Feeding reduced but infant taking >50–75% of normal feeds
- Wheeze audible on exam but no severe distress
- Alert and rousable
- No cyanosis
- No apnoea
- Age >3 months and previously healthy
- SpO2 >95% (where measured)

### Moderate criteria — hospital or PHC assessment same day
- Fast breathing persisting at rest
- Chest indrawing
- Feeding <50% of normal or each feed taking very long
- Wheeze clearly audible
- Irritable and unsettled
- Mild cyanosis during feeding or crying (not at rest)
- Brief apnoeic episodes in young infant
- Age <3 months with any respiratory symptoms
- Premature infant or infant with congenital heart disease — any severity = at least moderate referral threshold

### Severe criteria — ANY ONE = EMERGENCY
- Cyanosis at rest
- Severe respiratory distress with grunting
- Apnoea: any episode >20 seconds or with colour change/limpness
- Cannot feed at all
- Altered consciousness / limpness / not rousable
- Child appears "better" after severe distress — quiet, limp, less reactive (respiratory exhaustion)
- SpO2 <90%
- Age under 6 weeks with any bronchiolitis symptoms

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- PHC or clinic assessment same day — diagnosis needs to be confirmed; rule out pneumonia requiring antibiotics
- No antibiotics unless clinical signs suggest secondary bacterial infection — UMMA should advise family not to buy antibiotics from PMV; they do not help bronchiolitis
- No bronchodilators (salbutamol) routinely recommended — may be trialled once at clinic but should not be continued if no response
- No cough syrups
- Continue breastfeeding — breast milk provides immune protection and hydration; offer more frequently but in smaller amounts if infant tires quickly
- Clear nasal passages gently before feeds (saline drops if available) to help infant breathe and feed
- Position infant at slight incline (30 degrees) if feeding difficulty — do not prop with pillows unsupervised
- Keep infant away from cigarette smoke and cooking smoke — exacerbates airway inflammation
- Monitor feeding: count wet nappies; if fewer than 4 in 24 hours, return to clinic
- Monitor breathing at rest: if chest begins to indent or child cannot complete a feed, go to hospital
- Reassess in 24–48 hours — bronchiolitis often worsens on day 2–3 before improving

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your baby's chest is wheezing because of a viral infection in her small airways. The health centre needs to see her today to make sure it is not also a chest infection needing antibiotics. Antibiotics and cough syrups do not help this — the most important thing is to keep her feeding. Give the breast more often, in smaller amounts. Clear her nose gently before feeds. Watch her breathing tonight — if her chest pulls in or she cannot feed at all, take her to hospital straight away. This illness often gets a little worse before it gets better, so watch her closely for the next two to three days."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Hospital or general hospital assessment today — not PHC alone
- Child may need oxygen and close monitoring
- Feeding support may be needed (nasogastric tube feeding at hospital if oral feeding failing)
- Supportive care is the main treatment — oxygen, hydration, monitoring
- If travel is long, go to nearest PHC to be assessed and stabilised first
- Keep infant upright during transport — do not lay flat
- Watch continuously for apnoea during transport — if infant stops breathing or turns blue, stimulate gently and go as fast as possible

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your baby is working very hard to breathe and is not feeding well enough. She needs to be seen at the hospital today — they may need to give her oxygen and help with feeding. Try to keep her slightly upright on the way there. Watch her breathing closely — if she stops breathing for even a few seconds or turns blue, that is an emergency and you need to get there as fast as you can."

### Severe
UMMA should advise:
- Emergency hospital immediately — AKTH or nearest general hospital
- This is life-threatening — infant may stop breathing
- Keep upright during transport
- If apnoea occurs: stimulate infant (rub back firmly), call for help, get to hospital
- Do not stop at PHC — this requires oxygen and monitoring equipment
- Call ahead if possible

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"This is a medical emergency. Your baby needs to go to the hospital right now — she may stop breathing and needs oxygen and close monitoring. Keep her sitting up in your arms on the way. If she stops breathing, rub her back firmly to stimulate her and get to the hospital as fast as you can. Go to Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital or the nearest general hospital — please do not delay."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Salbutamol (Ventolin) nebulisation purchased from PMV without clinical assessment — no evidence of benefit in bronchiolitis; delays appropriate care
- Antibiotic courses from PMV (amoxicillin, ampicillin) given without clinical indication — no effect on viral bronchiolitis; may mask early bacterial superinfection symptoms
- Camphor, Robb, Mentholatum applied near infant's nostrils or on chest — camphor is toxic to infants; menthol-containing products can cause respiratory depression in young infants; avoid entirely in infants under 2 years
- Herbal steam inhalation over hot water — burn risk to infant face and airway; no benefit
- Laying infant flat during sleep when wheezing — worsens respiratory effort; slight elevation of head end preferred
- Giving herbal liquids by mouth to breathless infant — aspiration risk; can cause aspiration pneumonia on top of bronchiolitis
- Cough syrups containing antihistamines — sedating; worsen apnoea risk in young infants

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Pulse oximetry not reliably available at PHC — severity assessment is clinical; UMMA should emphasise clinical signs
- Oxygen concentrators available at some PHCs; more reliably at general hospitals — families should be directed to where oxygen is available
- Nasogastric feeding tubes — available at hospital level; important for infants too breathless to feed
- RSV testing not routinely available — diagnosis is clinical
- Premature infant follow-up: many premature infants in Kano are not formally tracked after NICU discharge; UMMA should ask about birth history for all infants

**Cultural factors:**
- Teething: wheeze and breathing difficulty in infants under 12 months are very commonly attributed to teething in Kano; this is incorrect but deeply held; UMMA should acknowledge the concern without dismissing the parent while clearly communicating the respiratory danger
- Keeping infant warm: overdressing or swaddling tightly during respiratory illness — increases respiratory effort; infants should be appropriately dressed, not overdressed
- Indoor smoke: cooking over open fire is culturally and economically embedded; UMMA should provide practical harm reduction (cook outside, ventilate room, keep infant in another room during cooking) rather than simply advising to stop
- Night-time seeking of care: cultural reluctance to travel at night in some Kano communities; UMMA must communicate clearly that respiratory emergencies in infants cannot wait for morning

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Premature infants (born before 37 weeks) — immature airways, immature respiratory drive, higher risk of apnoea; treat any bronchiolitis in premature infant as at least moderate
- Infants under 6 weeks — apnoea risk extremely high; any respiratory illness in this age group requires same-day hospital assessment
- Infants with congenital heart disease — even mild bronchiolitis can cause rapid decompensation; any respiratory illness = urgent referral
- Severely malnourished infants — impaired immunity, reduced respiratory muscle reserve; higher severity threshold
- Infants who are not breastfed — formula-fed infants have higher bronchiolitis severity; lack of immune factors from breast milk
- Infants with previous RSV bronchiolitis — may have recurrent wheeze; check history of previous episodes
