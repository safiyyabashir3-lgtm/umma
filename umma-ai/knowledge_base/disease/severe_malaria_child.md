# UMMA Disease File — Severe Malaria (Child, Under 5)
# Category: Child illness | Subcategory: Febrile illness — EMERGENCY
# Last reviewed: 2025

---

## OVERVIEW

Severe malaria is a medical emergency with high case fatality in children under 5. In Kano and northwest Nigeria, it is a leading cause of paediatric inpatient death. It represents the end of a continuum from uncomplicated malaria — the transition can occur within hours, particularly in children under 2, severely malnourished children, and those with sickle cell disease.

Severe malaria is defined by the presence of Plasmodium falciparum parasitaemia (or strong clinical suspicion) plus one or more features of organ dysfunction or clinical danger signs. In Nigerian clinical practice, diagnosis is often clinical because laboratory confirmation is not always available before treatment must begin.

**Why it matters for UMMA:** The window between recognisable severe malaria and death can be 6–24 hours. UMMA's role is to identify transition to severity as early as possible and generate an immediate, unambiguous emergency referral. Delay at any point — in recognition, in family decision-making, in transport — is fatal. UMMA must be prepared to communicate urgency clearly and without softening.

**Common delays and barriers to reaching care:**
- Family not recognising that altered consciousness or inability to feed is an emergency
- Attempting herbal or spiritual intervention before hospital
- No transport available at night — high proportion of severe malaria deaths occur overnight
- Financial barriers to hospital admission and treatment
- Previous bad experience at health facilities causing reluctance
- Multi-step referral — PHC to general hospital to AKTH — causes dangerous delays; UMMA should direct straight to highest appropriate facility
- Families sometimes wait for senior male family member to authorise hospital admission

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
Not applicable — severe malaria by definition requires referral. Any feature below is a danger sign.

### Moderate Presentation
Not applicable — see malaria_child.md for moderate uncomplicated malaria.

### Severe Presentation — Clinical Syndromes

Severe malaria presents in several overlapping syndromes. A child may have more than one simultaneously.

**1. Cerebral malaria**
- Impaired consciousness: Blantyre Coma Score <3 (child cannot localise pain, eyes do not open to voice, abnormal or absent motor response)
- In practical Nigerian context: child cannot recognise mother, does not respond to name, cannot be roused by normal stimulation
- Seizures: generalised, may be subtle (eye deviation, rhythmic limb movements, abnormal posturing) — repeated or prolonged
- Abnormal posturing: opisthotonus, decerebrate, decorticate
- Retinal haemorrhages (if fundoscopy available — usually only at tertiary level)
- No neck stiffness (distinguishes from meningitis — though both can coexist)
- Eyes may be open but unfocused ("looking through" the mother)

**2. Severe malarial anaemia**
- Very common in Nigeria due to high transmission intensity and poor nutrition baseline
- Severe pallor of conjunctivae, palms, nail beds, lips — Hb typically <5 g/dL
- Respiratory distress from cardiac compensation — fast breathing, tachycardia
- Cardiac flow murmur
- Hepatosplenomegaly often marked
- Child may still be conscious — severe anaemia does not always cause altered consciousness until very late
- Often occurs in younger, more chronically exposed children with repeated infections

**3. Respiratory distress (malaria acidosis)**
- Deep, laboured breathing — Kussmaul pattern
- Chest indrawing
- Nasal flaring
- Grunting
- Distinct from pneumonia: no productive cough, no focal chest signs; due to metabolic acidosis
- Can coexist with pneumonia — requires hospital assessment to distinguish

**4. Prostration**
- Child who could previously sit unsupported cannot do so
- Extreme weakness — cannot stand, cannot hold head up
- Not simply sleeping or tired — the child cannot perform these actions even when stimulated

**5. Hyperparasitaemia**
- Not clinically assessable without laboratory — >2–5% parasitised red blood cells on smear
- Suspect in child with high fever and rapid clinical deterioration
- Manage as severe regardless

**6. Hypoglycaemia**
- Often clinically occult — cannot distinguish from cerebral malaria without blood glucose
- Suspect in: child with convulsions, altered consciousness, poor feeding, any child who has not eaten for >12 hours
- More common in severely malnourished children
- At hospital: check blood glucose immediately in any unconscious child

**7. Haemoglobinuria (Blackwater fever)**
- Dark brown or black urine — haemolysis of parasitised and bystander red blood cells
- Associated with severe intravascular haemolysis and acute kidney injury
- Rare but high mortality — requires IV fluids and close monitoring at hospital

**8. Abnormal bleeding**
- Bleeding from venepuncture sites, gums, skin
- Indicates coagulopathy — disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
- Rare; very high mortality

**Nigerian-specific note on convulsions:** Febrile convulsions in malaria are common and do not always indicate cerebral malaria. Features suggesting cerebral malaria rather than simple febrile convulsion:
- Convulsion lasting >15 minutes
- More than one convulsion in 24 hours
- Focal convulsion
- Failure to return to normal consciousness within 30 minutes after convulsion stops
- Any impairment of consciousness between convulsions

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Severe malaria vs Bacterial meningitis:**
- Most critical differential — both cause altered consciousness and seizures in febrile children
- Meningitis: neck stiffness (may be absent in very young infants), bulging fontanelle, photophobia, more gradual onset of consciousness change
- Severe malaria: no neck stiffness (usually); consciousness impairment may be rapid; profound anaemia more likely
- IN PRACTICE: Cannot safely distinguish at community level — both require emergency hospital referral
- UMMA should not attempt to distinguish — refer as emergency for both

**Severe malaria vs Sepsis/Severe bacterial infection:**
- Sepsis: often in younger infants; source may be identifiable (umbilicus, skin, ear); temperature may be low rather than high; no splenomegaly
- Severe malaria: older infants and children more typical; splenomegaly; anaemia
- Both cause shock, altered consciousness, poor perfusion
- Cannot safely distinguish at community level — both require emergency referral

**Severe malaria vs Hypoglycaemia (other causes):**
- Hypoglycaemia mimics cerebral malaria exactly — cannot be distinguished without blood glucose
- Both require emergency assessment — do not delay referral attempting to distinguish

**Severe malaria vs Status epilepticus (other causes):**
- Convulsions in malaria can be prolonged
- All prolonged or repeated convulsions in febrile children require emergency referral regardless of cause

**Features making severe malaria the most likely diagnosis in Kano:**
- Age 6 months to 4 years
- Fever preceding consciousness change or convulsion
- Rainy season (June–October) or recent travel to endemic area
- Rapid deterioration over 24–72 hours
- Profound anaemia
- Positive RDT (where tested)
- Prior history of malaria episodes

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
Not applicable.

### Moderate criteria
Not applicable.

### Severe criteria — ANY ONE of these = EMERGENCY REFERRAL NOW

**Consciousness / Neurological:**
- Cannot be roused normally (does not respond to voice, does not recognise mother)
- Blantyre Coma Score <5
- Repeated convulsions: more than one convulsion in 24 hours
- Prolonged convulsion: >15 minutes duration
- Focal convulsion at any duration
- Post-convulsion — no return to normal consciousness within 30 minutes

**Feeding / Hydration:**
- Cannot drink or breastfeed at all
- Unable to swallow

**Activity:**
- Prostration — cannot sit unsupported (child who previously could)

**Respiratory:**
- Respiratory distress: fast breathing + chest indrawing + grunting (at rest, not only with fever)

**Circulation / Anaemia:**
- Severe pallor of conjunctivae, palms, lips (suspected Hb <5 g/dL)
- Signs of shock: cold peripheries, capillary refill >3 seconds, weak pulse, altered consciousness

**Urine:**
- Haemoglobinuria: dark brown or black urine

**Bleeding:**
- Spontaneous bleeding from any site

**Other:**
- Jaundice in child under 5 with malaria
- Clinical hypoglycaemia: sweating, pallor, seizures, reduced consciousness in fasting child

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
Not applicable.

### Moderate
Not applicable.

### Severe — UMMA should advise immediate emergency action

**Step 1 — UMMA communicates the emergency:**
Do not soften the message. State clearly this is a life-threatening emergency requiring immediate hospital attendance.

**Step 2 — Pre-referral action (while transport is being arranged):**
- If rectal artesunate suppository is available (some PHCs and community health workers in Kano carry these): administer rectally and go immediately — do not wait for effect
- If convulsing: position child on side, protect airway, do not insert anything in mouth
- Do not give food or water by mouth to a child with altered consciousness — aspiration risk
- Keep child warm during transport — cold peripheries worsen acidosis

**Step 3 — Where to go:**
- Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital (AKTH) Paediatric Emergency — primary destination for children in Kano
- Murtala Mohammed Specialist Hospital Kano — secondary option with paediatric inpatient capacity
- Nearest general hospital with paediatric ward and IV capacity — if AKTH is too far and child is critical
- Do NOT stop at PHC for severe malaria unless child is actively dying and cannot be moved — PHC cannot give IV artesunate or blood transfusion

**Step 4 — What to tell the hospital:**
- Child's age and weight (if known)
- How long child has been unconscious or convulsing
- Number of convulsions
- Whether rectal artesunate was given (dose and time)
- Whether child has had any oral medications or herbal remedies

**Step 5 — At hospital:**
UMMA advises family to insist on:
- Blood glucose check immediately
- IV artesunate (first-line; superior to quinine for severe malaria)
- Malaria RDT or blood smear if not already done
- Blood transfusion if severe anaemia confirmed

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"What you are describing is very serious — your child needs to go to the hospital RIGHT NOW. This is not something the health centre can treat. Go straight to Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital or the nearest general hospital. Do not wait. If your child is convulsing, lay her on her side and keep her mouth clear — do not put anything in her mouth. Do not give her anything to eat or drink while she is like this. When you get to the hospital, tell them she has been unconscious [or convulsing] and you think it may be malaria. They need to check her blood sugar and start a drip. Please go now — I will be here when you are ready to talk again."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Giving oral medications (including artemether) to unconscious or semi-conscious child — aspiration pneumonia risk; IV or rectal route only
- Prolonged home management with paracetamol alone before recognising severity — paracetamol reduces fever but does not treat malaria
- Inserting objects in mouth during convulsion — causes tooth trauma, airway obstruction, injury
- Sitting child upright during convulsion — increases aspiration risk
- Giving herbal enemas during severe illness — case fatality worsening documented in Nigerian paediatric wards
- Delaying hospital for prayer or traditional healing — must be communicated sensitively but firmly by UMMA

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- IV artesunate: available at AKTH and most general hospitals in Kano; NOT reliably available at PHC level
- Blood transfusion: requires general hospital or above; blood bank stock unpredictable — families may be asked to provide replacement donor
- Rectal artesunate suppositories: distributed through some community health programmes in Kano but not universally available; PHCs should stock them per national guidelines
- ICU/high dependency paediatric care: limited to AKTH in Kano; ventilatory support very limited
- Generator dependency for power at hospitals — night admissions sometimes face delays in lab processing
- Overnight transport: motorcycle taxis (achaba) are often the only available transport in peri-urban Kano at night — UMMA should not assume car/ambulance availability

**Cultural factors:**
- Families may interpret loss of consciousness as spiritual possession — UMMA must validate fear while redirecting to hospital urgently
- Mothers reporting to UMMA while husband is away — UMMA should help identify who can authorise/accompany without causing delay
- Some families request to pray first before going to hospital — acknowledge this but state the child can receive prayers at the hospital too; every minute matters
- Previous deaths of children in the family from similar presentations may cause fatalistic thinking — UMMA should name this gently and give realistic hope that treatment saves lives

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Children under 12 months with any of the above features — treat as severe malaria emergency without hesitation
- Severely malnourished children (SAM) — hypoglycaemia more likely; hypothermia may mask fever; altered consciousness may develop faster; mortality dramatically higher
- Sickle cell disease — severe haemolytic anaemia can develop within hours of malaria infection; any fever in a known SCD child is urgent
- Children with recent quinine treatment failure — consider artemisinin-resistant falciparum (emerging concern — currently low but monitor)
- HIV-positive children — more rapid progression; immunosuppression may blunt fever response giving false reassurance
