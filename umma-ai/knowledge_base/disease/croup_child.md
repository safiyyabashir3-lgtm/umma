# DISEASE FILE: CROUP (LARYNGOTRACHEOBRONCHITIS) — CHILD
**Category:** Child (0–5 years)
**Subcategory:** Respiratory / Upper airway
**Version:** 1.0
**Context:** Kano and northwest Nigeria

---

## OVERVIEW

Croup (viral laryngotracheobronchitis) is a common childhood illness caused by viral infection of the subglottic airway, producing characteristic subglottic oedema and airway narrowing. It is the most common cause of acute stridor in children under 5 in Nigeria. The peak age is 6 months to 3 years, with boys slightly more affected.

The most common causative virus is parainfluenza virus (types 1 and 2), followed by RSV, adenovirus, and influenza. In Kano, croup occurs year-round but increases during cooler dry-season months (harmattan, November–February) and at the onset of rainy season, corresponding to peaks in respiratory virus circulation.

UMMA's population context:
- Croup is very frequently confused by families and even some clinicians with severe asthma, foreign body airway obstruction, or epiglottitis
- The characteristic barking cough and stridor are often frightening — parents present to hospital in high anxiety, sometimes at moderate stages of illness
- Most croup is mild and self-limiting; however, severe croup with significant airway compromise can be rapidly life-threatening in young infants
- Epiglottitis (Haemophilus influenzae type b) is less common since Hib vaccination introduction but not eliminated — it mimics croup but is a distinct, rapidly lethal entity requiring different management
- Foreign body aspiration must always be considered in the differential, particularly in the crawling-to-walking age group in Kano households where small objects and food items are accessible

Common delays and barriers:
- Night-time presentation (croup typically worsens at night — families arrive distressed at 2–3am)
- Confusion of stridor with wheeze — asthma treatment given inappropriately
- Steam tent recommendations from patent medicine vendors may worsen anxiety without treating obstruction
- Delay in recognising worsening airway compromise as emergency

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
- 1–2 day prodrome: low-grade fever, runny nose, mild cough — indistinguishable from common URTI at this stage
- Classic barking cough: harsh, "seal bark" or metallic cough — often described by mothers as "abnormal cough" or "barking like a dog"
- Hoarse voice or cry (laryngeal involvement)
- Stridor: inspiratory noise on inhalation — heard when child is upset or active, but NOT at rest
- No or minimal respiratory distress at rest
- Child comfortable between coughing episodes
- Able to drink and feed
- Oxygen saturation normal (>95%)

### Moderate Presentation
- Stridor present at rest (not only when agitated)
- Suprasternal recession (notch above sternum draws in with each breath)
- Intercostal recession
- Child visibly working harder to breathe — nasal flaring, accessory muscle use
- Agitated, anxious, difficult to console
- Sleeping is disturbed
- Eating/drinking reduced
- Mild-moderate hypoxia (SpO2 92–95%) if pulse oximetry available
- Voice and cry remain hoarse
- Cough worsens at night (classic croup pattern)

### Severe Presentation
- Stridor loud and continuous at rest — may paradoxically become quieter as child tires (silent chest = impending arrest)
- Severe suprasternal, intercostal, and subcostal retractions
- Cyanosis (central — blue lips/tongue) or pallor
- SpO2 <92%
- Child exhausted, hypotonic, barely responding
- Cannot drink or swallow
- Altered consciousness — drowsy, confused, floppy
- Tachycardia disproportionate to fever
- Signs of impending respiratory failure: irregular breathing, gasping, exhaustion

**Epiglottitis (must distinguish from croup — see differentials):**
- Very toxic appearance: high fever >39°C, rapidly progressive
- Drooling (cannot swallow secretions) — highly specific for epiglottitis
- Muffled "hot potato" voice rather than barking cough
- Child sits upright and forward ("tripod position") — will NOT lie down
- NO barking cough — stridor is inspiratory and more musical
- NO prodromal cough/URTI
- Rapid progression (hours not days)
- Do NOT examine throat — can precipitate complete airway obstruction

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**From epiglottitis:**
- Croup: gradual onset (1–3 days); barking cough; hoarse voice; mild-moderate systemic upset; child can lie down
- Epiglottitis: abrupt onset (hours); NO barking cough; drooling; very toxic; child in tripod position; refuses to lie down
- Both produce stridor but epiglottitis is rapidly fatal without airway management — NEVER examine throat if epiglottitis suspected
- Hib vaccination: reduces but does not eliminate epiglottitis

**From foreign body aspiration:**
- Foreign body: sudden onset in a well child (no prodrome); often witnessed choking episode; unilateral reduced breath sounds; asymmetric wheeze
- Croup: preceded by viral prodrome; gradual onset; symmetric findings; characteristic barking cough
- In Nigerian context: nuts (groundnuts), seeds, corn are common aspirated objects in children learning to walk
- If sudden stridor/wheeze without prodrome in child aged 6 months–3 years = foreign body until proven otherwise

**From bacterial tracheitis:**
- Rare but dangerous: starts like croup, then deteriorates rapidly with high fever and toxic appearance
- Purulent secretions; no response to adrenaline nebulisation; needs ICU airway management
- Consider if child with "croup" rapidly deteriorates despite treatment

**From acute asthma / bronchiolitis:**
- Asthma/bronchiolitis: expiratory wheeze (not inspiratory stridor); no barking cough; lower airway
- Croup: inspiratory stridor; upper airway; barking cough; hoarse voice
- Stridor (inspiratory, upper airway) vs. wheeze (expiratory, lower airway) — key distinction
- Both can coexist in a child with underlying reactive airways

**From retropharyngeal or peritonsillar abscess:**
- "Hot potato" muffled voice; severe odynophagia; trismus; visible swelling; high fever
- Children typically 2–5 years; neck stiffness may be present
- May produce stridor if large — imaging at tertiary level needed

**From diphtheria:**
- Declining but not eliminated in incompletely vaccinated Nigerian populations
- Bull neck (cervical lymphadenopathy); grey adherent membrane in throat; toxin effects (myocarditis, neuropathy)
- Incomplete DTP vaccination history raises suspicion
- Treat as diphtheria if characteristic membrane seen — do not attempt to remove it

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria (Westley Croup Score equivalent — mild)
- Barking cough present
- Stridor only when agitated (not at rest)
- No or mild sternal retraction
- Child alert, able to drink and feed
- Normal colour
- SpO2 >95%
- Comfortable at rest

### Moderate criteria
- Stridor at rest
- Suprasternal and/or intercostal retractions at rest
- Mild agitation or anxiety
- Able to drink with difficulty
- SpO2 92–95%
- No cyanosis
- Not exhausted

### Severe criteria — any ONE = emergency
- Severe stridor at rest OR paradoxically decreased stridor with worsening distress (tiring)
- Severe retractions (suprasternal + intercostal + subcostal)
- Cyanosis or SpO2 <92%
- Altered consciousness, exhaustion, or floppiness
- Cannot swallow or drink
- Suspected epiglottitis (drooling, tripod position, very toxic, no barking cough)
- Suspected foreign body with complete or near-complete obstruction

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- Most mild croup resolves within 3–5 days — reassurance is important as the barking cough is frightening
- Keep child calm — crying and agitation worsen stridor by increasing negative pressure and airway turbulence; a calm child breathes easier
- Cool or humidified air may provide comfort (sit outside in cool night air in Kano — safe and helpful for mild cases)
- Ensure adequate oral fluids
- Fever management with paracetamol
- Do NOT use steam tents — risk of burns; evidence of benefit is poor
- Seek care at health facility if: stridor develops at rest; retractions worsen; child becomes breathless or cannot drink; colour changes
- Return to UMMA or seek care immediately if child deteriorates overnight (croup is typically worse at night)

**Plain language for UMMA:** "Your child has signs of croup — this is a viral infection causing swelling around the voice box. The barking cough sounds alarming but most croup is not dangerous. Keep your child calm and comfortable — crying makes it worse. You can try sitting outside in the cool night air. Give paracetamol if there is fever and make sure they keep drinking. Watch closely overnight. If your child's breathing becomes laboured, you see the throat or chest pulling in with each breath, or they develop any blueness, take them to hospital immediately."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Child needs assessment at a health facility today — dexamethasone (single oral dose) is the treatment and is safe and effective; it is available at many health facilities
- Do not leave child alone — can deteriorate rapidly
- Keep child calm during transport (held upright by parent, not lying flat)
- Do not attempt to examine the throat
- If a nebuliser with adrenaline is available at a health centre, this is appropriate emergency treatment
- Advise family that child may need monitoring overnight even if initial treatment is effective — can relapse

**Plain language for UMMA:** "Your child's breathing is more difficult and they need to be seen at a clinic or hospital today. There is a medicine called dexamethasone that works well for croup and many health centres have it. Keep your child calm and upright during the journey — do not let them lie flat. Do not try to look in their throat. Go today."

### Severe
UMMA should advise:
- Life-threatening airway emergency — go to hospital with emergency paediatric capacity immediately
- AKTH or nearest hospital with a paediatric emergency department
- Keep child upright (in parent's arms) at all times during transport — do NOT lie flat
- Do not attempt to look in or touch the throat
- If child stops breathing or goes blue: go as fast as possible, shout for help on arrival
- If epiglottitis suspected — do not give food, water, or attempt any examination — get to hospital

**Plain language for UMMA:** "This is a breathing emergency. Please go to AKTH or the nearest hospital with an emergency department right now. Hold your child upright in your arms — do not lay them down. Do not give food or water. Do not let anyone look in the throat. When you arrive at the hospital, tell the staff immediately that your child is having difficulty breathing. This is an emergency."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Steam tent treatment: traditional advice to put child over hot steam — burns risk; does not help airway oedema; may agitate child (worsening stridor)
- Forceful examination of throat by parent or patent medicine vendor — can cause complete airway closure in epiglottitis
- Giving chlorpheniramine or sedating antihistamines — respiratory depression can tip mild/moderate croup into respiratory arrest
- Assuming all stridor is "asthma" — treating with salbutamol alone in severe croup or epiglottitis wastes critical time
- Delay for spiritual or herbal treatment of "abnormal cough"

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Pulse oximetry not universally available at PHC level — severity must be assessed clinically
- Dexamethasone oral solution (the key treatment) not consistently stocked at all facilities
- Nebulised adrenaline available at secondary and tertiary facilities only
- Paediatric intubation capacity limited to AKTH and specialist centres — any child deteriorating to impending airway failure needs to reach AKTH quickly
- Epiglottitis requires surgical airway management capacity — secondary facilities in Kano may not be equipped

**Cultural factors:**
- Barking cough is often frightening to families and attributed to spiritual causes or "dog spirit" — reassurance about the viral nature is important
- Night-time deterioration (typical of croup) causes significant family alarm — UMMA should set clear overnight watch criteria for mild cases
- Mothers may attempt throat inspection or insert fingers to "clear" throat — must be advised against

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Children under 12 months with croup — smaller airway diameter; greater risk of severe obstruction from same degree of mucosal oedema
- Previously intubated children (subglottic stenosis increases risk)
- Children with underlying airway anomaly (laryngomalacia — common presenting as noisy breathing since birth)
- Incompletely vaccinated children (Hib — epiglottitis risk)
- Children in harmattan season — cold dry air increases airway mucosal irritability and dries secretions
