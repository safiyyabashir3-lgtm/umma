# OTITIS MEDIA / OTITIS EXTERNA / CSOM — CHILD
## Disease File for UMMA AI | Maternal and Child Health | Kano/Northwest Nigeria Context

---

## OVERVIEW

Ear disease in children in Kano and northwest Nigeria encompasses three main conditions: Acute Otitis Media (AOM), Chronic Suppurative Otitis Media (CSOM), and Otitis Externa (OE). These are grouped here because they frequently present to mothers as "discharge from the ear" or "ear pain" and UMMA must differentiate between them.

Acute Otitis Media is extremely common in Nigerian children under 5, frequently following or concurrent with upper respiratory tract infections. It is a leading cause of antibiotic prescription and, when poorly treated, a leading cause of CSOM. CSOM — chronic, persistent ear discharge through a perforated tympanic membrane lasting more than 2 weeks — affects an estimated 3–5% of children in Nigeria and is a major cause of childhood hearing loss. In Kano, CSOM is significantly undertreated: parents often normalise persistent ear discharge, and aural toilet (the cornerstone of treatment) is not widely known or practised.

Otitis externa — infection of the external ear canal — is less serious but commonly confused with CSOM and incorrectly treated.

The consequence of untreated CSOM and AOM goes beyond ear pain — sensorineural or conductive hearing loss from childhood leads directly to speech delay, poor school performance, and long-term developmental disadvantage. In a context where educational opportunities are already constrained, this matters profoundly.

UMMA relevance: Persistent ear discharge in a Nigerian child must never be dismissed. Any child with recurrent ear infections or ongoing discharge must be referred. Complications of AOM (mastoiditis, meningitis, intracranial abscess) while rare are life-threatening and occur more frequently in contexts of delayed or inadequate treatment.

Common delays:
- Ear discharge normalised ("children often have this")
- Cotton buds used inside ear — worsening infection and pushing debris against drum
- Ear drops from PMV without diagnosis — may contain inappropriate agents
- ENT services are limited — available only at secondary and tertiary level in Kano
- Child's hearing loss not recognised until school age

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### ACUTE OTITIS MEDIA (AOM)

**Mild:**
- Ear pain — child holds or pulls ear, especially at night (younger children cannot localise pain verbally)
- Irritability and disturbed sleep following or during an URTI (runny nose, cough)
- Mild fever (37.5–38.5°C)
- Reduced hearing (child not responding to sounds normally)
- No visible discharge from ear
- Child still feeding and consolable

**Moderate:**
- Significant ear pain — child crying and inconsolable, especially when lying flat
- Fever 38.5–39.5°C
- Clear or slightly yellow discharge from ear IF tympanic membrane has just spontaneously perforated (this often brings pain relief — parents may report "fluid came out and child calmed down")
- Child unwell, reduced feeding, not sleeping
- Possible vomiting from pain or fever

**Severe (AOM with complications):**
- High fever (above 39.5°C) persisting beyond 48–72 hours of treatment
- Swelling, redness, and tenderness behind the ear (mastoid area) — post-auricular swelling pushing the ear forward/outward = mastoiditis
- Severe ear pain unrelieved by simple analgesics
- Facial weakness (rare — facial nerve involvement)
- Neck stiffness, photophobia, altered consciousness — meningitis complication
- Dizziness, vomiting, loss of balance — labyrinthitis
- Ear discharge that started then stopped suddenly with worsening headache and systemic deterioration

### CHRONIC SUPPURATIVE OTITIS MEDIA (CSOM)

**Mild:**
- Persistent ear discharge (mucoid, mucopurulent, or frankly purulent) for more than 2 weeks from one or both ears
- No ear pain (absence of pain is characteristic of CSOM — if pain is present, consider AOM or cholesteatoma)
- Discharge may be noticed on pillow, on collar, or as visible wetness at ear
- Mild hearing loss — child responds less to sounds, turns louder ear toward sounds, teacher may have commented
- No fever, no systemic signs
- Child otherwise well

**Moderate:**
- Persistent foul-smelling discharge (suggests secondary infection or cholesteatoma)
- Hearing loss more noticeable — child asking for repetition, speech slightly delayed or unclear
- Recurrent episodes — discharge clears then returns
- No systemic signs but ongoing impairment

**Severe (CSOM with complications):**
- Foul-smelling ear discharge + headache + fever = possible intracranial extension
- Facial nerve palsy: drooping of one side of face, inability to close eye = serious complication
- Postauricular swelling (mastoiditis)
- Vertigo and balance problems
- Any neurological signs: altered consciousness, neck stiffness, seizures — meningitis/brain abscess

---

### OTITIS EXTERNA (OE)

**Mild:**
- Itch in the ear canal — child scratches or pokes ear
- Mild pain on pulling the outer ear (pinna) or pressing the tragus — characteristic feature of OE
- Minimal or clear watery discharge
- Ear feels blocked
- Commonly precipitated by: swimming/water in ear, cotton bud use (trauma to canal), humidity during rainy season

**Moderate:**
- Significant pain on touching or moving the pinna
- Swelling of the ear canal visible
- Thick, yellowish discharge from canal
- Reduced hearing due to canal oedema/discharge
- Mild periauricular lymphadenopathy

**Severe (Malignant/Necrotising OE — rare in children, relevant in immunocompromised):**
- Severe unrelenting ear pain out of proportion to findings
- Fever and systemic toxicity
- Visible granulation tissue at bony-cartilaginous junction of canal
- Facial nerve palsy
- Usually in immunocompromised — diabetics, HIV-positive
- Rare in healthy children under 5 but must not be missed

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

AOM vs CSOM:
- AOM: acute onset, preceded by URTI, fever and pain prominent, discharge acute if drum perforates
- CSOM: chronic course weeks to months, minimal or no pain, no fever, persistent discharge, hearing loss prominent
- Key rule: pain = AOM or complication; no pain with chronic discharge = CSOM

CSOM vs Otitis Externa:
- CSOM: discharge comes from behind the drum (through perforation), pulling pinna does NOT worsen pain significantly, hearing loss by conduction
- OE: discharge from canal, pulling pinna or pressing tragus causes significant pain, usually no perforation
- Differentiation matters: OE should not be treated with oral antibiotics; CSOM requires aural toilet not topical canal drops

CSOM vs Cholesteatoma (important, often missed):
- Cholesteatoma: destructive skin growth in middle ear, presents with foul-smelling discharge, can have ear polyp visible in canal, often causes facial nerve palsy or intracranial complications
- Must be suspected in CSOM with foul odour or any neurological complications
- Requires ENT referral and surgery

AOM vs Dental/Jaw pain referred to ear:
- Dental/jaw: no fever, pain worse with eating, examination of ear is normal
- AOM: fever, URTI history, pain worse lying flat, tympanic membrane inflamed

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild Criteria
- AOM: ear pain, irritability, mild fever, no discharge, no systemic toxicity
- CSOM: persistent discharge >2 weeks, no pain, no fever, no neurological signs
- OE: itching and mild canal discomfort, pain only on pinna manipulation, clear discharge

### Moderate Criteria
- AOM: significant pain, fever above 38.5°C, acute discharge, child clearly unwell
- CSOM: foul-smelling discharge, significant hearing loss, recurrent episodes
- OE: significant pain, canal swelling, thick discharge, periauricular lymphadenopathy

### Severe Criteria — ANY ONE = EMERGENCY
- Post-auricular (mastoid) swelling or tenderness with outward displacement of ear
- Facial nerve palsy: facial droop, inability to close eye
- Altered consciousness, neck stiffness, seizures, photophobia
- Severe headache + fever + ear symptoms
- Vertigo with falls
- High fever persisting after 48–72 hours of antibiotic treatment for AOM
- Any CSOM with neurological signs

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild

**AOM Mild:**
UMMA should advise:
- Child should be seen at a health centre — an inflamed ear drum needs examination
- Paracetamol or ibuprofen for pain and fever (advise mother to ask clinician for appropriate dose for weight)
- Antibiotics are required for AOM in children under 2 — do not wait
- Do not put anything inside the ear — no cotton buds, no eardrops from PMV without prescription
- Do not put breast milk or herbal oil in the ear

**CSOM Mild:**
UMMA should advise:
- Persistent ear discharge for 2 or more weeks must be assessed by a nurse or doctor
- Aural toilet (gentle cleaning of ear canal with dry wicking using a twisted cloth/tissue) is the most important treatment — the ear must be kept dry
- Antibiotic ear drops may be prescribed — these should be used correctly (lie child on side, pull pinna back, instil drops, keep still for 5 minutes)
- Cotton buds should never be used inside the ear — they push infection deeper
- Keep ear dry — protect from water during bathing
- Hearing assessment should be requested
- Referral to ENT if not improving in 2–4 weeks of aural toilet

**OE Mild:**
UMMA should advise:
- Keep ear completely dry
- Do not insert anything into ear canal
- Topical antibiotic/antifungal drops (prescribed by clinician)
- Avoid further trauma to canal

**Plain language for UMMA (ear discharge/pain):**
"Your child's ear has been [discharging / hurting] and this needs to be checked by a nurse or doctor. Do not put cotton buds or oil or any medicine inside the ear before seeing a health worker — this can make things worse. Keep the ear dry. If your child has a fever and the ear is very painful, go to the health centre today."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Same-day clinic visit for assessment
- For AOM: antibiotic prescription required — amoxicillin is first-line in most cases (clinician decision)
- For CSOM: aural toilet teaching at facility, appropriate topical antibiotic drops, hearing assessment
- For OE: canal cleaning at facility, topical antifungal/antibacterial drops
- Follow-up to confirm resolution

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"Your child's ear needs to be seen today. The [pain/discharge] has not gone away and needs proper treatment. Please go to the health centre. The doctor or nurse may need to clean the ear and give medicine. Please go today."

### Severe
UMMA should advise:
- Go to hospital emergency unit immediately — do not wait
- Mastoiditis, facial palsy, or neurological signs require ENT and possibly neurosurgical input
- Secondary or tertiary facility required — PHC is not adequate
- IV antibiotics and possible surgical drainage are needed for mastoiditis or intracranial complications

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"Your child has a very serious problem with their ear that needs hospital treatment right now. Please go to the hospital — not a health centre — immediately. If you notice your child's face drooping on one side, or they are having fits or cannot wake up, go to the emergency unit now."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Cotton buds used by virtually all families in Kano for "ear cleaning" — causes canal trauma, pushes wax and infection deeper, can rupture an already inflamed tympanic membrane
- Breast milk instilled into ear (widely practised as home remedy for ear pain) — no evidence of benefit, adds moisture which worsens OE and CSOM
- Herbal ear drops (local medicinal oils, kaolin-based preparations) — can obstruct the canal, introduce infection, and mask symptoms
- Eardrops from PMV dispensed without knowing whether the tympanic membrane is intact — some commercial ear drops are ototoxic if they reach the middle ear through a perforation

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Otoscopes are not universally available at PHC level in Kano — tympanic membrane cannot be visualised without one
- ENT services are available at AKTH and a small number of secondary facilities — waiting times can be long
- Hearing assessment (audiology) services are very limited — most children with CSOM-related hearing loss are never formally assessed
- Surgical management of cholesteatoma and mastoiditis requires tertiary care

**Cultural factors:**
- Persistent ear discharge is widely normalised — "many children have this" — and is not seen as requiring urgent attention
- Hearing loss in young children is sometimes attributed to stubbornness or inattention rather than an organic cause
- Ear cleaning is a culturally valued practice — advising against cotton bud use requires sensitive explanation

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Children with cleft palate (Eustachian tube dysfunction — very high AOM/CSOM risk)
- Children with recurrent URTI (including those exposed to household smoke from firewood cooking — very common in northwest Nigeria)
- HIV-positive children (higher risk of AOM and complications)
- Children in crowded compound settings with recurrent viral URTI transmission
- Children who have never had AOM treated adequately — highest progression risk to CSOM
