# UMMA Disease File — Anaemia in Pregnancy
# Category: Pregnancy illness | Subcategory: Haematological
# Last reviewed: 2025

---

## OVERVIEW

Anaemia in pregnancy is defined as haemoglobin (Hb) <11 g/dL in the first and third trimesters and <10.5 g/dL in the second trimester (WHO). It is one of the most prevalent conditions in pregnant Nigerian women — national estimates suggest 60–70% of pregnant women in northern Nigeria are anaemic, with a significant proportion having moderate to severe anaemia.

The aetiology in Kano and northwest Nigeria is almost universally multifactorial:
- **Iron deficiency** — the most common cause; driven by poor dietary iron intake, multiple closely-spaced pregnancies, and chronic blood loss
- **Folate deficiency** — inadequate intake, increased pregnancy demand
- **Malaria** — causes both acute haemolysis and suppression of erythropoiesis; a major contributor to severe anaemia especially in first and second trimesters
- **Hookworm** — chronic intestinal blood loss; endemic in parts of Nigeria
- **Sickle cell disease** — compound haemolytic anaemia; pregnancy is particularly dangerous in HbSS women
- **HIV** — anaemia of chronic disease; drug-related (zidovudine)

Anaemia in pregnancy is associated with maternal mortality (haemorrhage risk), preterm birth, low birth weight, perinatal mortality, and maternal cardiac failure in severe cases. It is also a major cause of maternal death at delivery when PPH occurs in an already severely anaemic woman.

**Why it matters for UMMA:** Anaemia is profoundly underdiagnosed in northern Nigeria because pallor is less visible in dark skin, and symptoms (fatigue, breathlessness) are normalised as part of pregnancy. Women present to hospital only when decompensated — in cardiac failure or after haemorrhage. UMMA has a unique role in probing for anaemia symptoms, encouraging Hb measurement at ANC, and promoting iron supplementation adherence.

**Common delays and barriers:**
- ANC attendance is low in parts of Kano, meaning Hb is never checked
- Iron tablets from ANC not taken — common reasons: nausea, constipation, forgetting, belief that iron tablets "harden" the baby or make delivery difficult
- Malaria not treated promptly — continues to suppress bone marrow
- Dietary restrictions in pregnancy — various food avoidances common in northern Nigeria reduce iron and folate intake
- Women normalise fatigue as a pregnancy symptom and do not report it at ANC

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation — Hb 9–10.9 g/dL
- Fatigue — often described as excessive tiredness beyond what the woman feels is normal for her stage of pregnancy
- Mild pallor — most reliably assessed in: conjunctivae (lower eyelid pulled down), palms, nail beds, tongue
- Mild breathlessness on significant exertion (climbing many stairs)
- Mild palpitations on exertion
- Headache — mild, non-specific
- Reduced appetite
- Woman is able to carry out daily activities though more slowly
- No cardiac symptoms at rest
- Heart rate may be mildly elevated

**Nigerian-specific note:** Pallor assessment on conjunctivae is the most reliable sign in dark-skinned women — it requires pulling down the lower eyelid and looking at the inner surface. Pale or white conjunctivae (instead of normal pink-red) indicates significant anaemia. This should be assessed at every ANC visit.

### Moderate Presentation — Hb 7–8.9 g/dL
- Significant fatigue — woman finds it difficult to perform household tasks
- Moderate pallor easily visible on conjunctivae, palms, and tongue
- Breathlessness at rest or with minimal exertion (walking short distances)
- Palpitations at rest or with minimal activity
- Dizziness, lightheadedness on standing
- Ankle swelling — from cardiac decompensation
- Tachycardia — heart rate elevated at rest
- Cardiac flow murmur may be audible (haemic murmur — high-output state)
- Reduced fetal movements may be noted (placental insufficiency from anaemia)
- Headache, difficulty concentrating
- Woman may feel she cannot walk far from home

### Severe Presentation — Hb <7 g/dL (or <6 g/dL in context of acute haemolysis)
- Severe pallor — strikingly white conjunctivae, very pale palms and tongue
- Severe breathlessness at rest — cannot lie flat (orthopnoea)
- Cardiac failure signs: basal crackles, elevated JVP, gallop rhythm, significant ankle oedema, hepatomegaly
- Severe tachycardia at rest (>120/minute)
- Severe weakness — cannot rise from bed without help
- Pre-syncope or syncope — fainting episodes
- Chest pain — from myocardial ischaemia in severe anaemia
- In sickle cell crisis: bone pain, jaundice, haemoglobinuria in addition to above
- Acute haemolysis from malaria: sudden severe pallor, dark urine (haemoglobinuria), jaundice, fever — this is a specific emergency

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Anaemia vs Normal fatigue of pregnancy:**
- Normal fatigue: does not worsen progressively; woman can perform normal tasks; no pallor; Hb normal at ANC
- Anaemia: progressive; limits activity; pallor present; Hb low

**Iron deficiency anaemia vs Malaria-related anaemia:**
- Malaria anaemia: acute/subacute onset; fever present; tender splenomegaly; history of recent or current malaria; may have haemoglobinuria; RDT positive
- Iron deficiency: gradual; no fever; koilonychia (spoon nails — late sign); history of poor diet, multiple pregnancies

**Anaemia vs Pre-eclampsia (both cause oedema and headache):**
- Pre-eclampsia: hypertension; proteinuria; headache more severe (frontal, occipital); visual disturbance; no pallor; Hb may be normal or elevated (haemoconcentration)
- Anaemia: hypotension or normal BP; no proteinuria; prominent pallor

**Anaemia vs Cardiac failure from other causes:**
- Peripartum cardiomyopathy: presents in late pregnancy/postpartum; cardiomegaly on CXR; no pallor initially; specific echocardiographic findings
- Severe anaemia causing cardiac failure: profound pallor; Hb very low; haemic murmur; responds to blood transfusion

**Anaemia vs Breathlessness of pneumonia or asthma:**
- Pneumonia: fever; productive cough; focal chest signs; not corrected by treating anaemia
- Asthma: wheeze; triggered; responds to bronchodilator; Hb normal
- Anaemia: pallor; no cough; no wheeze; tachycardia; responsive to transfusion

**Sickle cell disease in pregnancy — important variant:**
- HbSS: known diagnosis usually; painful crises; chronic anaemia with acute haemolytic episodes; jaundice; all obstetric complications at higher frequency
- Sickle cell trait (HbAS): usually asymptomatic; Hb may be mildly low; increases UTI risk in pregnancy
- Always ask about sickle cell status at first UMMA interaction in pregnancy

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria — Hb 9–10.9 g/dL (estimated clinically)
- Mild pallor
- Fatigue with significant exertion only
- No breathlessness at rest
- No cardiac symptoms at rest
- Normal daily activities possible
- No oedema

### Moderate criteria — Hb 7–8.9 g/dL (estimated clinically)
- Moderate pallor of conjunctivae, palms, tongue
- Breathlessness on minimal exertion
- Resting tachycardia
- Palpitations
- Ankle swelling
- Dizziness
- Difficulty with daily activities

### Severe criteria — Hb <7 g/dL / cardiac decompensation — emergency
- Severe pallor — strikingly white conjunctivae
- Breathlessness at rest / orthopnoea
- Signs of cardiac failure (crackles, oedema, gallop)
- Syncope or near-syncope
- Severe weakness — cannot mobilise
- Chest pain
- Acute haemolysis: sudden pallor + fever + dark urine + jaundice (malaria emergency)
- Sickle crisis: bone pain + severe pallor + jaundice + fever

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- Attend ANC — Hb measurement is essential; do not estimate severity at home
- Iron and folic acid supplementation: take daily as prescribed; most important intervention for iron deficiency anaemia
- Address common reasons for not taking iron: take with food or at night to reduce nausea; constipation managed with increased fluid and fruit intake; do not take with tea or coffee (reduces absorption) — take with orange juice or water
- Anti-malarial (IPTp with sulphadoxine-pyrimethamine) at each ANC visit as scheduled — prevents malaria-related anaemia
- Dietary advice: increase dark leafy vegetables (spinach, ugu), beans, liver, fortified foods; cooking in iron pots may increase dietary iron
- Deworm (mebendazole or albendazole) if not done — safe in second and third trimester per national guidelines
- Return to ANC for Hb recheck in 4 weeks to assess response to iron supplementation

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"You have signs of anaemia — your blood is low. This is common in pregnancy but important to treat. Please go to the antenatal clinic soon to have your blood level checked. If the clinic gives you iron tablets, take one every day — they work best if you take them with a small amount of food and not with tea. Eat foods like beans, green leaves, and liver as often as you can. Make sure you also take your malaria prevention medicine at every clinic visit."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- ANC attendance today or tomorrow — clinical assessment and Hb testing urgently needed
- Oral iron may not be sufficient — clinician may consider IM iron (iron sucrose or ferric carboxymaltose where available) or blood transfusion depending on Hb and gestational age
- Reduce physical exertion — rest when breathless; do not do heavy household work alone
- Monitor fetal movements — reduced movements require immediate hospital attendance
- No traditional bloodletting or herbal remedies — these worsen anaemia
- If concurrent malaria suspected or confirmed: treat urgently (see malaria section)

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your blood level seems quite low — this is putting both you and your baby under strain. You need to go to the antenatal clinic today or tomorrow to have your blood tested. Depending on how low it is, they may need to give you a stronger medicine or even a blood transfusion. Rest as much as you can. Check that your baby is moving — if you do not feel the baby moving for an hour or more, go to the hospital straight away."

### Severe
UMMA should advise:
- Emergency hospital immediately — AKTH or general hospital with blood bank
- Severe anaemia in pregnancy is life-threatening; cardiac failure or haemorrhage at delivery can be fatal with Hb this low
- Blood transfusion will be needed — hospital may ask family to provide replacement blood donor; advise family to think about this in advance
- If acute haemolysis (fever + pallor + dark urine): this is a malaria emergency — go to hospital immediately; bring any medicines currently being taken
- Do not lie flat if breathless — sit upright
- Do not give any herbal remedies or try to manage at home

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"What you are describing is very serious — your blood level is dangerously low and your heart is working too hard. You need to go to the hospital right now. They will likely need to give you blood through a drip. The hospital may ask someone in your family to donate blood for you — please start thinking about who could do this. Do not lie down flat if you are having trouble breathing — sit up. Go now, do not wait."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Not taking prescribed iron tablets — the single most modifiable contributor to anaemia in pregnancy; UMMA should explicitly explore barriers at every interaction with an anaemic pregnant woman
- Traditional practices: bloodletting (venesection) performed by traditional healers for "excess blood" — causes iatrogenic severe anaemia
- Eating clay (pica/geophagia) — extremely common in pregnant northern Nigerian women; clay binds dietary iron and worsens iron deficiency anaemia; UMMA should ask about this practice sensitively
- Herbal "blood tonics" — some may contain toxic substances or interfere with iron absorption; not a substitute for iron supplementation
- Refusing blood transfusion for religious reasons — uncommon but occurs; very high maternal mortality risk; UMMA should acknowledge beliefs while communicating the life-threatening risk
- Tea and coffee with meals — culturally very common; dramatically reduces iron absorption; advise taking iron supplement and iron-rich foods away from tea drinking times

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Hb measurement: Sahli haemoglobinometer or HemoCue available at many PHCs in Kano; not always functional; Hb testing at secondary care more reliable
- IV iron (iron sucrose): available at general hospitals and AKTH; not routinely at PHC
- Blood transfusion: requires general hospital or above; blood bank availability variable; families commonly required to provide replacement donors
- Anti-malarial IPTp (sulphadoxine-pyrimethamine): should be available at all ANC sites in Kano per national policy; stock-outs occur
- Mebendazole: available at most PHCs

**Cultural factors:**
- Pica — eating clay, soil, or other non-food substances is culturally normalised in pregnancy in many communities in Kano; it is not seen as harmful and women may not volunteer this information; UMMA should ask directly and non-judgementally
- Food restrictions in pregnancy: various beliefs restrict pregnant women from eating protein-rich foods (meat, eggs, fish) that are important iron sources; UMMA should explore dietary habits without dismissing beliefs, and suggest alternative iron sources
- Multiple closely-spaced pregnancies: extremely common in northern Nigeria; each pregnancy depletes iron stores further; birth spacing is an important anaemia prevention strategy — UMMA can gently introduce this topic
- Fatigue normalisation: "pregnancy is supposed to be hard" — many women and their families do not seek care for anaemia symptoms because fatigue is expected; UMMA should help women understand that severe fatigue and breathlessness are not normal

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Women with sickle cell disease (HbSS): chronic haemolytic anaemia + pregnancy haemolysis + folic acid deficiency; all pregnancies managed at specialist level; require higher-dose folate supplementation; any acute deterioration = emergency
- Women with prior PPH: pre-existing anaemia + risk of repeat haemorrhage = very high mortality risk; all prior PPH women should have Hb optimised before delivery
- HIV-positive women on zidovudine (AZT): medication-related anaemia compounds nutritional deficiency; monitor Hb closely
- Grand multiparous women (≥5 deliveries): cumulative iron depletion; high risk of severe anaemia
- Adolescent pregnant women: iron demands of adolescent growth + pregnancy = very high anaemia risk
- Women in first and second trimester with malaria: most acute haemolytic episodes occur in early pregnancy when placental malaria is becoming established
