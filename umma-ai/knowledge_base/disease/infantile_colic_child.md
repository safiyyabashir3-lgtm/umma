# INFANTILE COLIC — CHILD
## Disease File for UMMA AI | Maternal and Child Health | Kano/Northwest Nigeria Context

---

## OVERVIEW

Infantile colic is a benign but distressing condition defined by the Wessel "rule of threes": crying for more than 3 hours per day, more than 3 days per week, for more than 3 weeks in an otherwise healthy, well-fed infant under 5 months of age. The aetiology is not fully understood but likely multifactorial — gut immaturity, transient lactose overload, gut microbiome dysbiosis, maternal anxiety, and parenting stress all contribute. It resolves spontaneously in almost all infants by 3–4 months of age, though this is rarely reassuring to exhausted parents.

In Kano and northwest Nigeria, infantile colic has particular significance not because it is more common than elsewhere, but because:
1. It is frequently misattributed to serious illness — families seek treatment aggressively from PMVs and traditional healers
2. Dangerous treatments (gripe water with alcohol, herbal preparations, antispasmodics) are widely given
3. Mothers experience intense shame and pressure when they cannot soothe their crying infant — postpartum mental health implications are significant
4. Colic-like crying can mask serious diagnoses — UMMA must not anchor on colic and miss dangerous conditions

UMMA's primary roles for colic are: (1) reassure appropriately when colic is genuinely the most likely diagnosis, (2) screen carefully for dangerous alternative diagnoses, and (3) support the mother's wellbeing.

Critical rule for UMMA: Colic is a diagnosis of exclusion. Before advising reassurance, dangerous causes of crying must be systematically considered and ruled out. A child who is growing well, feeding well, and is entirely normal between crying episodes is more likely to have colic. A child who is unwell between episodes, not feeding, or has any danger signs has something else until proven otherwise.

Common delays and harms:
- Dangerous treatments given before serious cause is ruled out
- Mother blamed for "not feeding correctly" or "not holding properly" — delayed presentation
- Serious diagnosis missed because colic assumed

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation (typical colic)
- Crying episodes that are predictable — typically in the evening (late afternoon to night)
- Infant draws up legs, appears to strain, face flushes red during episode
- Excessive passing of wind before, during, or after episode
- Episode lasts 20–60 minutes then infant settles spontaneously or with holding/rocking
- Infant is completely normal between episodes: alert, feeding well, normal weight gain, no fever
- Age 2–8 weeks most typical onset; usually resolves by 3–4 months
- Infant is well nourished and hydrated
- Mother is exhausted and distressed but infant appears healthy

### Moderate Presentation (significant colic, or colic with uncertainty)
- Crying episodes longer than 1–2 hours, more than once daily
- Infant difficult to console even with feeding and holding
- Mother unsure if infant is feeding adequately — breast milk supply may be affected by stress
- Infant may have some mucousy stool but is not frankly unwell
- Mother has tried multiple remedies without relief
- Sleep deprivation is severe — mother may be showing signs of distress or postnatal depression

### Severe (colic diagnosis must be questioned)
- Note: there is no "severe" colic — if an infant appears severely ill or crying is accompanied by any danger sign, this is NOT colic. See danger signs below.

## CRITICAL DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSES — WHAT COLIC IS NOT

This section is essential. UMMA must systematically screen for these before considering colic.

**Intussusception — must not miss:**
- Episodes of severe, colicky abdominal pain with child drawing up legs then going limp between episodes (episodic pattern — pain then apparent improvement then pain again)
- Red-currant jelly stool: blood and mucus without stool — may be seen after 12–24 hours
- Vomiting, increasingly bilious
- Palpable sausage-shaped abdominal mass (right side of abdomen)
- Child becomes increasingly lethargic between episodes
- Peak age: 6 months to 2 years (later than peak colic age)
- EMERGENCY — surgical treatment needed

**Strangulated hernia:**
- Child with known inguinal hernia (bulge in groin) that is suddenly painful, firm, and cannot be reduced
- Vomiting, abdominal distension
- Child inconsolable
- EMERGENCY

**Testicular torsion (male infants):**
- Sudden onset severe crying, unilateral swollen firm tender testis
- Overlying skin may become red
- EMERGENCY — time-critical surgical emergency

**Meningitis / Encephalitis:**
- High-pitched cry (cerebral irritability) — distinct tone from colic crying — piercing, inconsolable
- Fever
- Bulging fontanelle at rest (not just with crying)
- Neck stiffness (in older infants)
- Photophobia — child calmer in dark room, cries more with bright light
- Seizures
- Reduced feeding and alertness between episodes
- EMERGENCY

**Serious bacterial infection / Sepsis:**
- Fever (or hypothermia in neonates)
- Not feeding
- Lethargic or limp between crying episodes
- Abnormal colour — pale, grey, mottled, jaundiced
- EMERGENCY

**Urinary tract infection (UTI):**
- Fever with no obvious source in infant
- Irritability and crying, especially with urination (may be noticed as squirming when nappy is changed)
- No localising symptoms in infants
- Urine dipstick and culture required for diagnosis
- Treat with antibiotics — not reassurance

**Corneal abrasion / Hair tourniquet syndrome:**
- Corneal abrasion: sudden onset inconsolable crying, child rubbing eye, photophobia
- Hair tourniquet: thread or hair wrapped around finger, toe, or penis — look carefully at digits and genitalia in any unexplained crying infant
- Finger/toe: swollen, discoloured, infant crying specifically when area touched
- These are missed regularly — UMMA should prompt physical inspection

**Gastro-oesophageal reflux (GORD):**
- Crying associated with or after feeding
- Arching of back during or after feeds
- Visible regurgitation or "silent reflux" with distress but no visible vomit
- May have poor weight gain if severe
- Often responsive to positional changes and feeding modifications

**Cow's milk protein allergy (CMPA):**
- In formula-fed infants or breastfed infants of mothers with high dairy intake
- Colic-like crying with additional features: mucousy/bloody stool, eczema, vomiting, poor weight gain
- Responds to maternal dairy elimination or hypoallergenic formula

**Lactose overload (functional):**
- Breastfed infant receiving excessive foremilk (frequent switching of breasts before breast is drained)
- Explosive, frothy, green stools
- Excessive wind and abdominal distension
- Responds to feeding modification

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

Colic vs Intussusception (highest priority distinction):
- Colic: under 3 months, consistent evening pattern, infant normal between episodes, no blood in stool
- Intussusception: usually 6 months to 2 years, episodic with limp-then-crying pattern, blood/mucus in stool, progressive deterioration
- If any doubt — refer

Colic vs Meningitis:
- Colic: no fever, normal fontanelle, normal colour, feeds normally between episodes
- Meningitis: fever, high-pitched cry, bulging fontanelle, not feeding, photophobia

Colic vs UTI:
- UTI: fever without obvious source, urine may smell unusual, infant looks unwell
- Colic: no fever, normal appearance between episodes

Colic vs GORD:
- GORD: crying clearly associated with feeds, arching, visible regurgitation
- Colic: crying pattern not strongly tied to feeding timing

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild (typical colic — reassurance appropriate)
- All of the following must be present:
  - Age 2 weeks to 4 months
  - Crying pattern is episodic (not continuous), predictably worse in evenings
  - Infant completely normal between episodes: alert, feeding well, gaining weight
  - No fever
  - No blood in stool
  - Fontanelle not bulging at rest
  - Normal colour and tone when not crying
  - No vomiting (or only small posseting)
  - No localising signs of pain on examination
  - No groin or scrotal swelling

### Moderate (colic with concern — clinic assessment warranted)
- Typical colic pattern but: infant feeding poorly, weight gain questionable, or mother highly distressed
- Crying episodes increasing in frequency or severity
- Mother unable to cope — postnatal mental health concern
- Any feature that makes clinician uncertain — refer

### Severe (NOT COLIC — immediate action required)
- ANY danger sign present: fever, blood in stool, bilious vomiting, bulging fontanelle, limp/lethargic infant, groin swelling, abnormal cry, hair tourniquet identified, eye redness with crying
- Immediate referral — do not reassure

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild (confirmed typical colic)
UMMA should advise:
- Reassure mother clearly: colic is very common, it is not dangerous, and it will resolve on its own by 3–4 months
- This is not caused by anything the mother is doing wrong
- Techniques that may help: gentle abdominal massage in clockwise direction, carrying in upright position, skin-to-skin contact, gentle rocking, white noise
- Continue exclusive breastfeeding — do not switch to formula because of colic
- If formula-fed: check preparation — overconcentrated formula is a cause of infant distress; do not add extra scoops
- Gripe water with alcohol should NOT be given
- Herbal preparations (dawanye, kayan sheba) should NOT be given — these are not evidence-based and some are harmful
- If breastfeeding: try ensuring infant drains one breast fully before switching — reduces foremilk/hindmilk imbalance
- Advise return if: fever develops, blood appears in stool, infant becomes lethargic, infant stops feeding, or the pattern changes
- Ask about mother's own wellbeing — a severely sleep-deprived mother needs support

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"What you are describing sounds like colic. This is very common in babies this age and it is not dangerous. It will get better on its own, usually by the time your baby is 3–4 months old. You are not doing anything wrong. Some things that may help are holding your baby upright, gently massaging the tummy in circles, and keeping your baby close to your body. Please do not give any herbal medicine or gripe water — these are not needed and some can be harmful. If your baby develops a fever, you see blood in the nappy, your baby becomes limp or stops feeding, come to the clinic right away."

### Moderate (colic with concern)
UMMA should advise:
- Clinic assessment is recommended — to rule out GORD, CMPA, UTI, and to assess weight gain
- Postnatal mental health screening should be included — ask the clinician about support for the mother
- Feeding assessment by a nurse or lactation supporter may identify a modifiable cause

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"This might be colic, but since your baby is also [not feeding well / losing weight / you are very worried], I think it is a good idea for a nurse or doctor to check your baby. Please go to the health centre this week to make sure everything is okay. You are right to take this seriously."

### Severe (danger signs present)
UMMA should advise:
- Do not reassure — escalate immediately
- If blood in stool, bilious vomiting, bulging fontanelle, limp infant, groin swelling, or abnormal-sounding cry — hospital immediately
- If hair tourniquet identified — go to clinic urgently for removal
- If corneal abrasion suspected — go to clinic today

**Plain language for UMMA:**
"I am concerned about your baby. The crying combined with [describe symptom] is not typical colic — your baby needs to be seen by a doctor today. Please go to the health centre or hospital now. This is important."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Gripe water: widely sold in Nigeria, many formulations contain significant alcohol — alcohol is toxic in infants and has no benefit for colic; UMMA must advise against it
- Herbal preparations for infant "belly pain" (dawanye, specific Hausa herbal mixtures for infants): unregulated, some contain liver-toxic alkaloids, some contain undisclosed pharmaceutical agents
- Antispasmodics (dicyclomine/hyoscine) — occasionally given to infants by PMVs; hyoscine can cause respiratory depression in infants under 6 months — dangerous
- Paregoric (camphorated opium tincture) — occasionally still sold in some markets — absolutely contraindicated in infants
- Excess water or glucose water given to "soothe stomach" in breastfed neonates — causes hyponatraemia and displaces breast milk

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- No diagnostic tools needed for colic reassurance — but differentials (UTI, intussusception) require clinic-level investigation
- Intussusception requires ultrasound and surgical capacity — available only at secondary/tertiary level
- In Kano, corneal abrasion diagnosis requires slit lamp or fluorescein staining — available at specialist facilities
- Postnatal depression support is extremely limited in northwest Nigeria

**Cultural factors:**
- A crying baby in a compound setting creates communal pressure on the mother — neighbours and mothers-in-law may insist on treatment, making it difficult for a mother to simply "wait it out"
- In some families, persistent infant crying is attributed to spiritual causes (evil eye, jinn) — traditional healing may be sought before or instead of medical care
- Mothers may be blamed for a crying infant — attributed to their diet ("too much pepper"), their milk quality, or their emotional state — UMMA should actively counter harmful attribution
- Exclusive breastfeeding is sometimes abandoned because of colic — community belief that formula is "heavier" and will stop the crying — UMMA must actively support breastfeeding continuation

**High-risk subgroups for missed diagnosis:**
- Infants with inguinal hernia (risk of strangulation presenting as "colic")
- Male infants (testicular torsion)
- Infants of mothers with high dairy intake (CMPA risk)
- Infants with any prior URTI or fever in the week preceding crying episodes (UTI, meningitis risk)
- Infants whose normal pattern of colic suddenly changes — a colic infant who develops new fever, new blood in stool, or becomes lethargic has a new diagnosis
