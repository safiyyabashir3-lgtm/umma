# DISEASE FILE: MALARIA IN A CHILD UNDER 5
## Nigerian Context (Kano / Northwest Priority) — Awaiting Clinician Verification

---

## OVERVIEW
Malaria is the leading cause of death in Nigerian children under 5, accounting
for approximately 35% of under-5 mortality. Nigeria carries the highest malaria
burden in the world. In Kano and northwest Nigeria, Plasmodium falciparum is
responsible for virtually all malaria. Children under 5 have not yet developed
immunity and are at highest risk of severe and fatal disease. Malaria commonly
coexists with pneumonia, anaemia, and malnutrition in this population.
Many children are brought to care late — after days of home treatment with
patent medicine or herbal remedies.

---

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN CHILDREN UNDER 5

### Mild Presentation
- Fever — may be continuous or episodic
- Chills and rigors — though may be absent in young infants
- Sweating after fever
- Vomiting — very common
- Poor appetite and reduced feeding
- Irritability and crying
- Child looks unwell but still responsive and alert
- Able to drink

### Moderate Presentation
- Higher persistent fever
- Repeated vomiting — difficulty keeping fluids down
- Significant weakness — child not wanting to move
- Pallor developing — inner eyelids becoming pale
- Fast breathing from developing anaemia
- Child less responsive than usual
- Diarrhoea may be present

### Severe Presentation — Any ONE feature = Severe Malaria
- Prostration — child cannot sit up or is too weak to feed
- Impaired consciousness — difficult to wake, not recognising caregivers
- Repeated convulsions — more than two in 24 hours
- Severe anaemia — very pale eyelids, palms, lips with fast breathing
- Very fast breathing — respiratory distress
- Inability to drink or breastfeed at all
- Abnormal bleeding
- Jaundice — yellowing of eyes and skin
- Hypoglycaemia signs — sweating, sudden deterioration, seizure
- Dark or cola-coloured urine — blackwater fever

---

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES
- Fever pattern — episodic chills then heat then sweating is classic but
  may be absent in young children
- Vomiting is almost universally present in significant malaria in children
- Pallor alongside fever — strongly suggests malaria with anaemia
- Fast breathing without clear chest signs — suggests malaria anaemia not
  pneumonia
- Child sleeping under no net or inconsistent net use
- Rainy season or post-rainy season — higher transmission

---

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria — all of these must be true
- Child alert and responsive
- Able to drink and keep fluids down
- No convulsions
- No severe pallor
- No fast or difficult breathing at rest
- Fever present but child not prostrated

### Moderate criteria — any of these
- Repeated vomiting — struggling to keep fluids down
- Significant weakness — not wanting to move or play
- Moderate pallor of eyelids
- Fast breathing present but no chest indrawing
- Fever persistent more than 2 days without improvement

### Severe criteria — any ONE = severe malaria, emergency
- Convulsion
- Impaired or lost consciousness
- Unable to drink or feed at all
- Severe pallor — eyelids almost white
- Chest indrawing or severe respiratory distress
- Prostration — cannot sit up unsupported
- Jaundice
- Dark urine
- Child deteriorating rapidly

---

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
- Urgent clinic visit today — same day
- Blood test (RDT or blood film) is needed to confirm
- Do not start antimalarial treatment without diagnosis
- Paracetamol for fever — correct dose for weight
- Continue oral fluids and breastfeeding
- Monitor closely — return immediately if worsens

Plain language for caregiver:
"Your child needs to be seen at a clinic today to check for malaria.
Please do not give malaria medicine before the test. Keep giving fluids
and paracetamol for the fever. Go now — do not wait."

### Moderate
- Urgent clinic or hospital today — same day
- Needs proper assessment, blood test, and treatment
- May need injectable treatment if vomiting prevents oral medicine
- Do not attempt home treatment

Plain language for caregiver:
"This is more serious and your child needs to go to hospital today.
The vomiting means medicine by mouth may not work — the doctors need
to assess the best way to treat your child."

### Severe
- Emergency — go to hospital immediately
- This is life-threatening
- Do not wait for any reason
- Cerebral malaria and severe anaemia can kill within hours

Plain language for caregiver:
"This is very serious and your child needs emergency hospital care right now.
Please go immediately — do not wait. Every minute matters."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS
- Many caregivers in Kano begin treatment with patent medicine vendors
  before any diagnosis — always ask what has already been given
- Counterfeit antimalarial medicines are common in northern Nigerian markets
  — treatment failure does not always mean drug-resistant malaria
- Herbal medicines given for fever in children can mask symptoms and
  delay presentation — always ask
- Severe anaemia from malaria may need blood transfusion — only available
  at hospital
- Glucose (sugar) given in hypoglycaemia during severe malaria — only safe
  under medical supervision
- Mosquito net use must be reinforced at every contact — low consistent
  use in Kano households
- Children with sickle cell disease are at especially high risk of severe
  malaria — escalate earlier
- Malnourished children deteriorate faster with malaria — escalate earlier
