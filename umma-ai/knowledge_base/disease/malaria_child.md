# malaria_child.md
# UMMA Disease File — Uncomplicated Malaria (Child, Under 5)
# Category: Child illness | Subcategory: Febrile illness
# Last reviewed: 2025

---

## OVERVIEW

Malaria caused by Plasmodium falciparum is the single most common cause of fever and childhood death in Kano and northwest Nigeria. Nigeria carries the highest malaria burden globally, and children under 5 — particularly those under 2 — are the most vulnerable. In Kano, malaria transmission is seasonal (intensifying June–October with rains) but year-round, with perennial low-level transmission even in the dry season.

For UMMA's population, malaria is the default assumption for any febrile child under 5 unless another cause is clearly established. It frequently coexists with pneumonia, severe anaemia, malnutrition, and urinary tract infection — complicating diagnosis and worsening outcomes.

**Why it matters for UMMA:** Most malaria deaths in children are preventable with early recognition and prompt artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT). The critical danger is delay — families often manage fever at home for 2–4 days with paracetamol or herbal remedies before seeking care, by which point uncomplicated malaria has frequently progressed to severe malaria.

**Common delays and barriers:**
- Initial management at patent medicine vendors (PMVs) with subtherapeutic chloroquine or single-drug artemisinin (monotherapy) — both ineffective and dangerous
- Preference for herbal remedies and spiritual treatment in early illness
- Cost of transport to secondary/tertiary care
- Belief that fever is teething, evil eye, or spiritual attack — especially in infants
- Distance from functioning primary health centres (PHCs) in peri-urban and rural Kano
- Female decision-making constraints — mothers may need husband or mother-in-law approval before seeking care

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation
- Fever — may be continuous or intermittent; does not reliably follow the classic tertian pattern in young children
- Child is irritable, crying more than usual, or unusually quiet
- Reduced feeding — particularly reduced breastfeeding in infants
- Mild vomiting — one or two episodes
- Mild diarrhoea in some children — loose stools without blood
- Child is hot to touch; sweating episodes may occur
- Child is alert, recognises mother, responds to stimulation
- No respiratory distress
- No pallor or mild pallor only

**Nigerian-specific note:** Fever in infants under 3 months is always high-risk regardless of apparent severity. Malaria in very young infants may present with hypothermia rather than fever — particularly in malnourished infants.

### Moderate Presentation
- Persistent high fever (often >38.5°C) for more than 24–48 hours
- Repeated vomiting — struggling to keep oral fluids down
- Reduced urine output — mother notices fewer wet nappies or less urination
- Child is lethargic — drowsy, less responsive than usual, but still rousable
- Moderate pallor of conjunctivae, palms, or lips
- Mild abdominal distension; possible hepatosplenomegaly on palpation
- Febrile convulsion (single, brief, generalised, <15 minutes) — common in Nigerian children aged 6 months to 5 years with malaria fever; requires urgent assessment but does not automatically indicate cerebral malaria
- Mild jaundice possible
- Child will still feed or take oral fluids if encouraged

### Severe Presentation
See severe_malaria_child.md for full detail. Key transition features:
- Altered consciousness — child cannot be fully roused, does not recognise mother
- Repeated convulsions (more than one in 24 hours, or prolonged >15 minutes)
- Inability to drink or breastfeed
- Severe pallor (severe anaemia)
- Respiratory distress — fast breathing, chest indrawing, grunting
- Prostration — child cannot sit unsupported (in child who could previously do so)

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Malaria vs Pneumonia:**
- Malaria: fever-dominant, often without cough at onset; respiratory distress if present is from anaemia or acidosis, not pulmonary consolidation
- Pneumonia: cough is prominent; fast breathing present even when fever controlled; chest indrawing; may have focal signs
- CRITICAL: These coexist frequently — a child with malaria can develop superimposed pneumonia. If fast breathing persists after paracetamol reduces fever, consider pneumonia additionally.

**Malaria vs Typhoid:**
- Typhoid: more gradual onset over days; fever often without chills; relative bradycardia; abdominal tenderness; rose spots (rarely seen in Nigerian context); diarrhoea or constipation
- Malaria: more acute onset; chills and rigors more prominent; splenomegaly in chronic/repeated infections

**Malaria vs Meningitis:**
- Meningitis: neck stiffness, bulging fontanelle in infants, photophobia, rash (rare in Nigerian context); altered consciousness with headache
- Malaria: altered consciousness in severe disease; neck stiffness absent in uncomplicated malaria
- CRITICAL: Both can cause seizures and altered consciousness — meningitis must always be considered when consciousness is impaired

**Malaria vs UTI:**
- UTI: fever without localising symptoms; dysuria if child old enough to report; offensive urine; commoner in girls
- These frequently coexist — malaria diagnosis does not exclude UTI

**Malaria vs Febrile seizure:**
- Febrile seizure is a complication of malaria fever, not a separate differential; treat the malaria

**Features making malaria more likely in Kano context:**
- Age 3 months to 5 years
- Fever during or after rainy season (June–October)
- No ITN use / inadequate bednet use
- No recent malaria prophylaxis
- Sibling or household member with recent malaria
- Splenomegaly on examination
- Positive RDT (where available at PHC)

**Features making malaria less likely:**
- Age under 3 months (malaria is rare; consider sepsis, meningitis)
- No fever at any point
- Localising features pointing to another source (ear discharge, throat exudate, urinary symptoms)

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Fever with no danger signs
- Child alert and responsive
- Able to feed or drink
- No or mild vomiting (able to keep fluids down)
- No pallor or mild pallor only
- No respiratory distress
- No convulsions (or single brief febrile convulsion now resolved with child fully conscious)
- Illness duration <48 hours

### Moderate criteria
- Fever >48 hours not responding to home management
- Repeated vomiting but still able to take some oral fluids
- Lethargy — drowsy but rousable
- Moderate pallor
- Single febrile convulsion (now resolved, fully conscious)
- Unable to attend school or normal activity but not prostrate
- Reduced but not absent urine output

### Severe criteria — ANY ONE of these = refer immediately as emergency
- Impaired consciousness / cannot be roused normally
- Repeated convulsions (>1 in 24 hours) or prolonged convulsion (>15 minutes)
- Inability to drink, breastfeed, or swallow
- Prostration (cannot sit or stand unsupported — child who previously could)
- Severe respiratory distress (fast breathing + chest indrawing + grunting)
- Severe pallor (very pale conjunctivae, palms, lips — suggests Hb <5 g/dL)
- Haemoglobinuria (black/dark brown urine — blackwater fever)
- Jaundice in child under 5
- Abnormal bleeding

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- Seek care at the nearest PHC or clinic for malaria testing (RDT) and prescription of ACT — do not buy from PMV
- Give paracetamol for fever at home while arranging to go — tepid sponging can help comfort the child
- Continue breastfeeding; encourage oral fluids (clean water, ORS, breast milk)
- Do not give aspirin or ibuprofen to children with suspected malaria
- Do not give chloroquine — it is no longer effective against falciparum malaria in Nigeria
- Do not give herbal remedies alongside medicines — some interact dangerously
- Use insecticide-treated bednet from tonight — even if child already has malaria, prevents reinfection and reduces mosquito biting
- Return immediately if child becomes harder to rouse, stops feeding, has a fit, or breathing becomes fast or laboured

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child has signs of malaria. Please take her to the health centre today for a test and medicine. While you are getting ready, you can give paracetamol for the fever and make sure she keeps drinking — breast milk or clean water. Do not buy medicine from a chemist shop for this — malaria medicine needs a proper prescription. Come back to me straight away if she becomes very sleepy, stops feeding, or has a convulsion."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Same-day attendance at PHC or secondary care (general hospital) — do not wait until tomorrow
- If vomiting is making oral medications difficult, rectal artesunate suppository (where available) may be given as bridge to facility care — advise family to ask clinic
- Oral ACT should be given at facility — ensure full course is completed even when child improves
- Assess for anaemia at facility — if moderate anaemia suspected, facility will decide on iron supplementation after acute treatment
- Monitor for worsening: if child cannot keep any fluids down, becomes more difficult to rouse, or has a fit — treat as severe, go to hospital immediately
- Return review in 24–48 hours if treated at PHC

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child needs to be seen at the health centre today — not tomorrow. She has been sick for a few days and the fever is not getting better on its own. If she vomits the medicine at the clinic, tell the nurse — they may have a suppository option. Watch her very closely tonight. If she stops recognising you, stops drinking completely, or has a convulsion, go straight to the general hospital — do not wait for morning."

### Severe
UMMA should advise:
- This is an emergency — go to the nearest secondary or tertiary hospital now (general hospital or teaching hospital — AKTH in Kano)
- Do not stop at PHC unless it has inpatient capacity and IV artesunate — most do not
- If convulsing on the way: place child on side, do not put anything in the mouth, keep airway clear
- Pre-referral rectal artesunate suppository if available and child cannot swallow — this saves lives in transit
- Call ahead if possible — some families in Kano have contact numbers for AKTH emergency paediatrics
- Do not delay for any reason — severe malaria can kill within hours
- Alert the UMMA conversation that this is a referral situation — UMMA should offer to help family identify transport

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"This is very serious. Your child needs to go to the hospital right now — not the health centre, the general hospital or Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital. This cannot wait. If your child is having a fit, turn her on her side and keep her mouth clear. Go now and tell the doctors she has been having fits and cannot wake up properly. I know it is hard but please do not delay."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Chloroquine purchased from PMVs — widespread resistance means this is ineffective and delays proper treatment
- Artemisinin monotherapy (artemether alone without a partner drug) — sold widely in Kano markets; drives resistance and causes treatment failure
- Herbal concoctions given alongside or instead of ACT — some cause hepatotoxicity; many cause vomiting worsening dehydration
- "Push-to-back" syndrome — lay belief that fever has "gone inside" and requires rubbing with harsh substances or giving enemas; causes harm
- Delaying hospital for spiritual/prayer intervention in severe cases — culturally important to acknowledge but must not delay life-saving care
- Giving aspirin for fever in children — risk of Reye syndrome; common in some communities

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- RDT availability variable at PHC level — many diagnose clinically; both over- and under-treatment result
- IV artesunate not reliably available below secondary care level in Kano
- Blood transfusion capacity limited to secondary and tertiary care — children with severe anaemic malaria must reach general hospital
- Electricity and drug cold chain failures affect ACT stock at PHCs periodically
- Transport from rural LGAs in Kano state (e.g., Tudun Wada, Doguwa) to AKTH can take 1.5–3 hours

**Cultural factors:**
- Teething is a very common attribution for fever in infants — UMMA must not dismiss this belief but must redirect urgency appropriately
- Mothers may have limited autonomy to take child to hospital without husband or mother-in-law; UMMA should acknowledge this and help problem-solve
- Purdah practices may limit mother's ability to travel alone; may need female companion or male escort
- Ramadan fasting — some families reluctant to give children medicine during Ramadan; UMMA should clarify sick children are exempt

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Children under 12 months — highest mortality risk; fever in this age group always requires same-day assessment
- Severely malnourished children (SAM/MAM) — malaria presentation atypical; immunity further impaired; treat as higher severity
- Children with sickle cell disease — malaria precipitates rapid haemolytic crises; any fever = urgent same-day assessment
- Children of recently arrived rural migrants to Kano urban areas — lower acquired immunity from different transmission zones
- Children not sleeping under ITN — highest exposure risk
