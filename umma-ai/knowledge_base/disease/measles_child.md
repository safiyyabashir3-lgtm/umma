# MEASLES — CHILD (Under 5)

## OVERVIEW
Measles is a highly contagious paramyxoviral infection and a leading vaccine-preventable cause of childhood mortality in Nigeria. Kano State and northwest Nigeria carry a disproportionate burden of measles cases in Africa. Despite the availability of measles vaccine through Nigeria's Routine Immunisation (RI) programme and Supplemental Immunisation Activities (SIAs), coverage in northern Nigeria remains critically low — Kano State has recorded routine immunisation coverage as low as 30–40% in some LGAs, leaving large cohorts of susceptible children.

Measles in Nigerian children under 5 is frequently severe. It kills primarily through its complications — pneumonia (the most common fatal complication), encephalitis, and measles-associated blindness (corneal ulceration driven by vitamin A deficiency). Diarrhoea complicating measles causes dehydration and malnutrition. The disease causes immune suppression for weeks to months after recovery ("immune amnesia"), making previously vaccinated pathogens dangerous again.

Epidemic cycles occur in Kano — large outbreaks have affected thousands of children. Outbreaks cluster in unimmunised communities, during harmattan season (when dry air aids viral transmission), and in the post-rainy season. Crowded conditions in Kano — compound living, Quranic schools (Almajiri system), markets — amplify transmission.

Why it matters for UMMA: Measles must be identified early. Vitamin A supplementation is a life-saving intervention that must be initiated at any healthcare contact during measles illness. Many Nigerian mothers do not recognise early measles (prodrome phase), delaying care until complications develop. The rash may be less visible or atypically coloured on darker skin. Complications are the norm, not the exception, in malnourished or vitamin A-deficient children.

Common delays and barriers:
- Prodrome (fever, cough, runny nose, red eyes) mistaken for ordinary cold — care not sought until rash appears
- Rash may be less visually striking on dark skin — mothers may not recognise it as measles
- Measles managed at home with herbal remedies; healthcare sought only when complications develop (pneumonia, encephalitis)
- Vitamin A supplementation not routinely given at PHC level during measles illness
- Significant stigma in some communities — families may conceal measles cases, reducing access to care
- Almajiri children (Quranic school students, often from rural LGAs) are a particularly vulnerable, mobile, and immunisation-deprived group

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation (Early Prodrome — Days 1–4 before rash)
- Fever — initially moderate, rising progressively
- Coryza (runny nose, nasal congestion)
- Cough — dry, becoming prominent
- Conjunctivitis — bilateral red watery eyes, photophobia
- Koplik's spots — PATHOGNOMONIC: small white spots with red halo on buccal mucosa (inner cheeks opposite lower molars), appearing 1–2 days before rash, disappearing as rash emerges. May be overlooked if not specifically examined. Appear as "grains of salt on a red background."
- Child unwell, irritable, anorexic but ambulatory
- No complications yet

Note: "Mild" in measles is relative — even the prodrome phase requires vitamin A and monitoring. There is no truly low-risk measles in a malnourished or vitamin A-deficient Nigerian child under 5.

### Moderate Presentation (Rash Phase — Days 4–7)
- High fever (39–40.5°C), often peaking as rash appears
- Maculopapular rash appearing first at hairline/behind ears, spreading downward over face, neck, trunk, limbs over 3 days — reaches feet by day 3 of rash
- On dark skin: rash may appear as slightly elevated papules with subtle colour change — brown/reddish tones rather than bright red. Must feel the skin (palpable papules) not just look.
- Rash confluent on face, discrete on limbs
- Severe conjunctivitis with photophobia — child unable to tolerate light
- Persistent cough, possible early signs of pneumonia
- Oral ulcers, stomatitis
- Lymphadenopathy (cervical, suboccipital)
- Diarrhoea — very common in Nigerian children with measles, contributes to dehydration
- Child looks ill, anorexic, markedly unwell
- If malnourished: rash may be sparse, atypical; noma (cancrum oris) can develop around mouth

### Severe Presentation (Complicated Measles)
- **Measles pneumonia**: increased respiratory rate for age, chest indrawing, grunting, cyanosis — the most common fatal complication
- **Measles encephalitis**: altered consciousness, seizures (beyond simple febrile seizures), abnormal behaviour, focal neurological signs — occurs 5–7 days after rash onset, high mortality and neurological sequelae
- **Corneal ulceration / measles blindness**: white clouding of cornea, severe photophobia, eye pain, eye discharge — VISION EMERGENCY, especially in vitamin A-deficient children
- **Severe dehydration from diarrhoea**: sunken eyes, reduced skin turgor, no tears, weak pulse, reduced urine
- **Croup / laryngotracheitis**: harsh barking cough, inspiratory stridor, voice change — airway compromise risk
- **Cancrum oris (noma)**: rapidly spreading gangrenous destruction of mouth/face tissues — occurs in severely malnourished children, high mortality
- **Bacterial superinfection**: otitis media, purulent conjunctivitis, skin secondary infections
- Severe malnutrition triggered or worsened by measles illness
- Any child with measles who is not maintaining consciousness, breathing normally, or tolerating any oral intake

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**Measles vs roseola infantum (HHV-6):**
- Roseola: fever resolves THEN rash appears (fever-then-rash pattern); rash is faint pink, non-confluent; child looks well when rash appears; no cough/coryza/conjunctivitis; younger age group (6–18 months)
- Measles: rash appears DURING fever; child looks ill throughout; cough, coryza, conjunctivitis prominent; Koplik's spots

**Measles vs rubella:**
- Rubella: milder prodrome, rash finer and less confluent, prominent posterior auricular and suboccipital lymphadenopathy, shorter duration, no Koplik's spots, less severe conjunctivitis
- Measles: more severe illness, more prominent cough and coryza

**Measles vs meningococcal rash:**
- Meningococcal: petechial or purpuric (non-blanching) rash, rapid progression, meningism, shock — EMERGENCY
- Measles: rash blanches on pressure, maculopapular, follows cephalocaudal progression, fits prodrome pattern

**Measles vs drug rash:**
- Drug rash: no cough/coryza/conjunctivitis, no Koplik's spots, clear drug exposure history, may be urticarial

**Measles vs dengue:**
- Dengue: petechial element common, severe bone pain ("breakbone fever"), thrombocytopenia, no coryza or Koplik's spots
- Note: dengue is less common in Kano (dry climate) but should be considered in children from southern areas or with travel history

**Key distinguishing features for measles:**
- The 3 C's of prodrome: Cough + Coryza + Conjunctivitis
- Koplik's spots (pathognomonic if present)
- Cephalocaudal (head-to-toe) rash progression
- Child looks systemically unwell throughout

**Common coexistences in Nigerian context:**
- Measles + severe acute malnutrition (SAM) — dramatically worsens prognosis, increases noma risk
- Measles + vitamin A deficiency — corneal ulceration risk very high
- Measles + malaria — fever may mask classic pattern; both present with high fever; consider both
- Measles + bacterial pneumonia (secondary infection)
- Measles + TB reactivation (measles-induced immune suppression)
- Measles + HIV (undiagnosed) — atypical and prolonged course

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Clear prodrome features (fever, cough, coryza, conjunctivitis, Koplik's spots) or early rash phase
- Fever manageable, child alert and responsive
- Maintaining oral intake (breastfeeding or drinking)
- No respiratory distress (breathing rate normal for age, no chest indrawing)
- No neurological changes
- No corneal involvement
- No signs of severe dehydration
- Nutritional status acceptable (no visible SAM)

### Moderate criteria
- Rash phase with high fever and systemic illness
- Diarrhoea present but child still tolerating some oral fluids
- Conjunctivitis severe with photophobia but no corneal haziness
- Mild increased respiratory rate but no chest indrawing
- Oral ulcers/stomatitis reducing oral intake
- Early signs of undernutrition
- Otitis media (ear pain, discharge)
- Child irritable but responding to mother, not obtunded

### Severe criteria — any ONE = severe/emergency
- Respiratory distress: fast breathing for age + chest indrawing + grunting or cyanosis (measles pneumonia)
- Altered consciousness: difficult to wake, confused, not responding normally
- Seizures (especially after first 48 hours of rash — suggests encephalitis)
- Corneal haziness or white spot on eye (measles blindness risk)
- Severe dehydration: sunken eyes, loss of skin turgor, no tears, very weak
- Stridor (barking cough with noisy breathing in — croup)
- Facial tissue destruction/noma (any gangrenous mouth lesion)
- Severe acute malnutrition (visible wasting, oedema) + measles = automatic severe
- Child unable to take any oral intake
- Haemorrhagic measles (bleeding into rash — rare but very high mortality)

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
UMMA should advise:
- Go to PHC or clinic TODAY — even mild measles requires healthcare contact for vitamin A supplementation (this is the single most important intervention UMMA must communicate)
- Vitamin A must be given by a health worker — explain this clearly to mother
- Continue breastfeeding — breast milk is vital during measles illness
- Encourage oral fluids — clean water, ORS if diarrhoea present
- Tepid sponging for fever — do not over-bundle child
- Keep child away from other children and infants — measles is extremely contagious
- Clean eyes gently with clean damp cloth — do not apply tiro or breast milk to eyes
- Do not apply anything to the rash
- Monitor for danger signs and return immediately if: breathing becomes fast or laboured, child becomes drowsy or has a seizure, any white spot appears on eye, diarrhoea becomes severe
- Report suspected measles to PHC — this is a notifiable disease in Nigeria

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"The symptoms you're describing sound like they could be measles. Even if your child seems okay right now, you need to go to the clinic today — not tomorrow, today. There is a very important medicine called Vitamin A that must be given during measles to protect your child's eyes and help them fight the illness. The clinic needs to give this. Keep breastfeeding your child and give extra water or ORS if there is diarrhoea. Keep your child away from other children because measles spreads very easily. If your child starts breathing fast, becomes very sleepy or hard to wake up, has a fit, or if you see any cloudiness in the eye, go to the hospital immediately."

### Moderate
UMMA should advise:
- Take child to PHC or hospital today — child needs clinical assessment, vitamin A, monitoring
- If diarrhoea is significant, ORS should be started immediately during transport
- Vitamin A is the priority treatment message
- PHC provider should assess for early pneumonia (respiratory rate count), dehydration, corneal involvement
- Feeding support — high-energy foods when child can tolerate, breastfeeding continued
- Analgesic/antipyretic for fever and oral pain — to be prescribed at clinic
- Oral hygiene — clean mouth gently if stomatitis present
- Child likely needs more frequent review — daily if possible
- Educate family about danger signs requiring emergency response
- Notify other household members — check immunisation status of other children

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child's measles is more serious than a mild case. You need to take them to the clinic or hospital today for a proper check-up. The health worker needs to check your child's breathing, check their eyes, and give the Vitamin A injection or capsule — this is very important to prevent blindness. If your child has diarrhoea, start giving ORS now while you prepare to go. Keep breastfeeding. At the clinic, tell the health worker if your child has had any fits, if they are very sleepy, or if you see any cloudiness in their eyes."

### Severe
UMMA should advise — EMERGENCY RESPONSE:
- Go to hospital with emergency capacity NOW — not PHC, not patent medicine vendor
- Bring child directly to emergency/children's ward
- If cyanosis, seizure, or loss of consciousness: describe emergency clearly — child needs immediate triage
- While travelling: maintain airway (keep head tilted, ensure breathing), continue breastfeeding if child able, start ORS if available and child swallowing
- For corneal involvement — do not put anything in the eye, go directly to a hospital with eye/ophthalmology services if possible (AKTH, Murtala Muhammad Specialist Hospital)
- For seizure: position on side, nothing in mouth, time the seizure, go to hospital
- Inform hospital that child has measles — infection control measures needed
- Emphasise: children die from measles complications, not measles itself — the complication is the emergency

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"This is a medical emergency. Your child has measles with a very dangerous complication. You must go to hospital right now — not a patent medicine shop, not a PHC. [For breathing difficulty:] Your child is struggling to breathe — this needs hospital treatment immediately. [For unconsciousness/seizures:] Your child is not responding properly/is having fits — this is a brain emergency from measles. Go now. [For eye:] The cloudiness in your child's eye means measles is attacking their sight — they could go blind. Go to a hospital with an eye doctor immediately. Do not wait to see if it gets better. Every minute matters."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- Herbal bathing of child with measles rash — traditional practice across northern Nigeria; herbal preparations may cause secondary skin infections or systemic absorption
- Restricting diet during measles illness — "fever foods" beliefs lead to withholding protein and energy at exactly the moment the child needs it most; worsens nutritional status
- Applying oil, shea butter, or locally compounded preparations to rash — blocks skin, risk of secondary infection
- Applying breast milk or tiro to infected measles eyes — very dangerous in the context of vitamin A deficiency
- Home management throughout entire illness — vitamin A not given, complications not detected
- Traditional healers (boka) consulted first — delays hospital presentation for encephalitis and pneumonia

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Vitamin A supplementation: available at PHC level during immunisation contacts and National Vitamin A Supplementation days — but during individual measles illness, stock-outs are common. UMMA should direct to the nearest facility with vitamin A capsules and encourage the mother to request it specifically.
- Measles diagnosis is clinical — no rapid test available at PHC level in most of Kano
- Encephalitis management (including mannitol, anticonvulsants, ITU support) only at tertiary hospitals — significant transport barrier from rural LGAs
- Ophthalmology for corneal ulceration only at tertiary level
- Oxygen for measles pneumonia — intermittently available at general hospitals, more reliably at AKTH
- Almajiri children: no fixed caregiver, no immunisation records, mobile — may present very late to hospital, may arrive at any facility along their route

**Cultural factors:**
- Measles has traditional names in Hausa (kyanda) — recognised as a culturally significant illness with traditional management protocols. Families may feel the illness "must run its course" and resist hospitalisation.
- Belief that measles is caused spiritually (evil eye, ancestral punishment) is present in some communities — families may visit marabou first
- Male health-seeking preference: mothers may need husband's permission to take child to hospital; this delay can be fatal in measles encephalitis
- Measles may be concealed from neighbours due to stigma — reduces community case-finding

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Unimmunised children (highest burden — check vaccination card at every contact)
- Almajiri children — unimmunised, malnourished, mobile, no fixed caregiver
- Children with severe acute malnutrition (SAM) — noma, encephalopathy, overwhelming sepsis risk
- Children with vitamin A deficiency — endemic in northwest Nigeria, dramatically increases corneal ulceration and blindness risk
- Children under 9 months (not yet eligible for routine measles vaccine in Nigeria's schedule — though some SIA campaigns reach 6 months)
- Children with undiagnosed HIV — atypical, prolonged, fatal measles
- Infants born to non-immune mothers — no or short-lived passive immunity
- Children in high-density settings: Almajiri schools, internally displaced persons (IDP) camps
