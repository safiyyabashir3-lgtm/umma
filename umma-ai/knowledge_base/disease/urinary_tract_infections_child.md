# DISEASE FILE: URINARY TRACT INFECTIONS (CHILD)
**Category:** Child (0–5 years)
**Subcategory:** Genitourinary / Febrile illness
**Version:** 1.0

---

## OVERVIEW

Urinary tract infections (UTI) in children encompass lower UTI (cystitis — bladder infection) and upper UTI (pyelonephritis — kidney infection). In children under 5, UTI is a particularly important diagnosis because it is a common cause of fever without an obvious source, clinical features are non-specific especially in infants, and untreated or inadequately treated pyelonephritis causes renal scarring that leads to hypertension and chronic kidney disease in adulthood.

In Kano and northwest Nigeria, UTI in children is significantly underdiagnosed. The condition is rarely considered as a primary diagnosis by community health workers or patent medicine vendors. Fever is attributed to malaria, teething, or "typhoid" while an underlying UTI goes untreated, allowing progression to pyelonephritis and renal damage.

**Epidemiology in Kano/northwest Nigeria:**
- UTI affects approximately 3–8% of febrile children under 5 presenting to Nigerian emergency departments
- Girls are more susceptible after infancy; boys under 1 year (especially uncircumcised) have higher rates than older boys
- In Kano, male circumcision is nearly universal (religious practice) — this confers significant protection in male infants compared to non-circumcised populations
- Common causative organisms in Nigeria: E. coli (most common), Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis, Enterococcus species, Staphylococcus saprophyticus
- Antimicrobial resistance is a serious and growing problem — E. coli resistance to ampicillin and co-trimoxazole exceeds 70% in many Nigerian studies; nitrofurantoin and cefuroxime generally retain activity
- Vesicoureteric reflux (VUR) and other structural urological abnormalities are important underlying risk factors — recurrent UTI should prompt investigation

**Why it matters for UMMA:**
UTI is a common and potentially serious condition that is rarely considered by mothers or community health workers in this context. UMMA has an opportunity to flag UTI as a possible cause of fever without obvious source, particularly in girls and in young infants. Early recognition and referral prevents the serious long-term consequence of renal scarring. The key skills for UMMA are: recognising when a febrile child needs urine evaluation, advising appropriate care-seeking, and warning against inadequate short-course treatment from PMVs.

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation (Lower UTI / Cystitis — typically older children 2–5 years)
- Dysuria: pain or burning on passing urine — child cries when urinating or holds genitals
- Urinary frequency: passing urine very often in small amounts
- Urgency: child suddenly needs to urinate and cannot hold it
- Suprapubic discomfort: child complains of tummy pain low down, or holds lower abdomen
- Haematuria: pink or blood-tinged urine — this frightens mothers; important to report
- Urine may smell strongly offensive
- No or low-grade fever (< 38°C)
- Child is well-looking, active, eating normally
- No systemic features
- Note: lower UTI/cystitis is less common in under-2s — the younger the child, the more likely to be upper tract involvement

### Moderate Presentation (Pyelonephritis — upper tract, febrile UTI)
- Fever 38–39.5°C — this is the dominant feature, especially in infants under 2 where urinary symptoms may be absent
- Fever without obvious source in a young child — no ear, throat, or chest findings
- In infants: non-specific features — irritability, poor feeding, vomiting, lethargy, fever
- In toddlers and older children: may have back or loin pain (costovertebral angle tenderness on examination — child winces when loin is tapped)
- Nausea and vomiting common
- Reduced appetite
- Dysuria and frequency may be present in older children but often absent in infants and toddlers
- Child looks unwell — not as active, not interacting normally
- Abdominal pain — may be suprapubic or flank
- Offensive-smelling urine
- Urine may appear cloudy or dark

### Severe Presentation (Urosepsis / Complicated Pyelonephritis)
- High fever > 39.5°C or hypothermia (in young infants — a worrying sign)
- Rigors (shaking chills)
- Child appears toxic — pale, grey, very unwell looking
- Marked lethargy or reduced consciousness
- Severe vomiting, unable to retain oral fluids or medications
- Hypotension or poor perfusion (cold extremities, weak pulse, prolonged capillary refill)
- Signs of systemic sepsis: tachycardia, tachypnoea, altered mental status
- Any febrile infant under 3 months — UTI at this age can rapidly progress to urosepsis and meningitis
- Jaundice in young infants with UTI (UTI is a cause of neonatal and early infant jaundice)
- Renal angle tenderness with systemic compromise
- Known structural urological abnormality with febrile illness
- Recurrent febrile UTI — suggests vesicoureteric reflux or structural abnormality, increasing renal scarring risk

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**UTI vs Malaria:**
- Most critical differential in Kano — malaria is the first diagnosis considered for any febrile child
- Malaria: typically higher fever spikes, rigors, pallor/anaemia, positive RDT or blood film
- UTI: fever may be lower grade, no rigors in most, RDT negative; urinary symptoms may point toward UTI in older children; urine dipstick showing leukocytes and nitrites is highly suggestive
- Critical: malaria and UTI can coexist — a positive RDT does not exclude UTI
- If a child has fever that does not fully resolve with antimalarial treatment, UTI should be considered

**UTI vs Typhoid:**
- Typhoid: prolonged fever, abdominal distension, stepladder fever, hepatosplenomegaly
- UTI: urinary symptoms in older children; in infants, urinalysis is the differentiating investigation
- Both present with fever without source — urine culture and blood culture may both be needed

**UTI vs Viral fever:**
- Viral fever: upper respiratory symptoms (cough, runny nose, sore throat), short duration, no urinary symptoms, urine dipstick normal

**UTI vs Appendicitis:**
- Both can cause right lower quadrant/abdominal pain and fever
- Appendicitis: focal tenderness at McBurney's point, guarding, no urinary symptoms, worsens progressively; urine usually normal
- UTI: suprapubic or loin pain, urinary symptoms, urine dipstick abnormal

**UTI vs Vulvovaginitis:**
- Vulvovaginitis: dysuria due to external perineal inflammation rather than bladder infection; midstream urine culture is negative; examine perineum for redness, discharge, or irritation
- UTI: midstream urine culture positive; internal rather than external source of dysuria

**UTI in young infants — special consideration:**
- In infants under 3 months: fever without source = must exclude UTI (and meningitis, sepsis)
- Urinary symptoms are completely absent in this age group — only fever, poor feeding, vomiting, and non-specific irritability
- Clean-catch urine or catheter specimen is required — bag specimens have high contamination rates and are unreliable for culture

**Common coexistences:**
- UTI + malaria (both cause fever; both must be treated)
- UTI + constipation (constipation is a risk factor for recurrent UTI)
- UTI + vesicoureteric reflux (identified on investigation of recurrent UTI)
- UTI + pinworm infection in girls (Enterobius can carry bacteria from perianal area to urethra)

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Older child (2–5 years) with dysuria, frequency, and urgency
- No fever or low-grade fever only (< 38°C)
- No systemic illness, eating and drinking normally
- No loin/back pain
- Symptoms for fewer than 3 days
- Child not an infant (over 2 years)

### Moderate criteria
- Fever ≥ 38°C without obvious source in any child under 5
- Any febrile UTI in a child under 2 years (all febrile UTIs in this age group are treated as upper tract involvement)
- Loin pain or costovertebral angle tenderness
- Vomiting limiting oral intake
- Child unwell-looking but alert
- Abnormal urine dipstick (leukocytes, nitrites, blood)
- Able to tolerate oral antibiotics

### Severe criteria — ANY ONE = severe/emergency
- Any febrile infant under 3 months with suspected UTI
- Signs of sepsis: hypotension, tachycardia, poor perfusion, altered consciousness
- Unable to tolerate oral medications due to persistent vomiting
- Fever with rigors and toxic appearance
- Jaundice in young infant with fever
- Known structural urological abnormality with febrile illness
- Second or third UTI episode — needs investigation referral (risk of renal scarring)
- Child with single kidney or known renal abnormality

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild — UMMA advice
**Clinical approach:** Child with symptoms of lower UTI (dysuria, frequency, no fever) can be directed to PHC or clinic for urine dipstick and culture, and to start treatment. Should not be managed without at least a urine test.

- Child needs to go to a clinic or PHC — urine needs to be tested before starting any treatment.
- At the clinic: urine dipstick for leucocytes and nitrites; midstream clean-catch specimen for culture and sensitivity if available.
- Treatment will be a prescribed antibiotic — do not buy antibiotics from a patent medicine vendor without urine testing. The wrong antibiotic will not work and the infection may spread to the kidneys.
- Ensure the child drinks plenty of fluids — water, ORS, breast milk.
- Advise on perineal hygiene: front to back wiping in girls, regular bathing.
- Complete the full course of antibiotics even if symptoms improve.
- Return immediately if fever develops, child worsens, or symptoms do not improve within 2 days.

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child may have a urine infection. This needs to be checked at a clinic — the doctor will test the urine before giving medicine. Please do not buy antibiotics from the chemist without a urine test first. Give your child plenty of water to drink. If your child develops a fever, starts vomiting, or gets worse, go to the clinic straight away."

---

### Moderate — UMMA advice
**Clinical approach:** Febrile UTI in child under 5, especially under 2, needs same-day clinic or hospital assessment with urine testing, blood tests, and prescription antibiotics. May need inpatient management if vomiting or if infant under 6 months.

- Child must be seen at a clinic or hospital today. A febrile child with a possible urine infection — especially a baby under 2 — needs proper assessment including a urine test and blood tests.
- At the facility: urine dipstick and culture, blood culture if child looks septic, full blood count, malaria test. IV antibiotics may be needed depending on the child's age and severity.
- Infants under 6 months with febrile UTI are typically admitted for IV antibiotics — UMMA should prepare mother for this possibility.
- Do not give antibiotics from a chemist before going — resistance is common and culture-guided treatment is important.
- Give paracetamol for fever and comfort on the way. Keep feeding and hydrating the child.

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child has a fever that may be caused by a kidney infection. This needs to be seen by a doctor today — not tomorrow. Kidney infections in young children can cause lasting damage if not treated properly and quickly. Take your child to a clinic or hospital. They will test the urine and decide on the right medicine. Your child may need a drip if the infection is bad. Please go today."

---

### Severe — UMMA advice
**Clinical approach:** Sepsis, infant under 3 months with fever, or child unable to tolerate oral medication requires emergency hospital care with IV antibiotics.

- This is a medical emergency. Your child needs to go to a hospital with emergency and inpatient facilities now.
- In Kano: AKTH emergency, general hospital emergency — not a PHC or patent medicine store.
- The child needs IV antibiotics urgently — an infection in the blood from the kidneys can be life-threatening.
- If the child is a young baby (under 3 months) with fever: go immediately, do not wait to see if it improves.
- Do not give any antibiotics at home before arriving. Paracetamol for fever is fine.

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"Your child is very ill and needs emergency hospital care right now. A young baby with fever needs to be seen immediately — do not wait. Please go to the hospital emergency unit now. Your child may need a drip and antibiotics given through the drip. Do not stop at a chemist."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous local practices:**
- Amoxicillin and ampicillin from PMVs: resistance rates to these drugs are over 70% for E. coli causing UTI in Nigeria. Children treated with these drugs appear to improve initially (fever reduces) but develop recurrent or complicated UTI. UMMA must strongly advise against self-treatment with ampicillin/amoxicillin for suspected UTI.
- Inadequate treatment duration: PMVs often dispense 3-day or incomplete courses. UTI in children under 5 — especially febrile/upper tract UTI — requires 7–14 days of appropriate antibiotic therapy. Short courses lead to relapse and renal scarring.
- Urine "testing" at PMVs: some PMVs use urine dipsticks and interpret results without clinical context — may over-diagnose or under-diagnose. Urine culture is the gold standard and is only available at hospitals.
- Herbal remedies for "bladder" symptoms: various traditional preparations are used, including some with nephrotoxic potential.
- Delaying treatment in infants: mothers of young babies may not consider infection as a cause of fever without obvious localising signs — fever in an infant under 3 months must always be taken seriously.

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- Urine culture is not available at PHC level — midstream urine specimens must be transported to hospital labs; delays in processing result in unreliable cultures
- Urine dipstick availability is inconsistent at PHCs
- Renal ultrasound for structural abnormality investigation is available at AKTH and some general hospitals but long wait times
- Micturating cystourethrogram (MCUG) for VUR investigation is limited to tertiary facilities
- Nitrofurantoin — the preferred oral drug for lower UTI — is frequently out of stock in Kano public health facilities

**Cultural factors:**
- Dysuria in girls may not be reported openly — considered a private or embarrassing complaint; mothers may describe it euphemistically
- Perineal hygiene practices: some communities use harsh soaps, powders, or lotions on the perineum of infant girls that cause vulvovaginitis and increase UTI risk
- Belief that kidney problems are spiritually caused may delay care for recurrent UTI
- Water intake is often low in northern Nigeria — UMMA should actively encourage fluid intake in children with any urinary symptoms

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Infants under 3 months: any fever is an emergency — UTI at this age carries risk of concurrent meningitis and septicaemia
- Girls over 2 years: anatomical susceptibility, especially with poor perineal hygiene
- Children with constipation: a modifiable risk factor for UTI; UMMA should advise high fibre diet and hydration
- Children with prior UTI: vesicoureteric reflux must be excluded to prevent progressive renal damage — refer to paediatric/urology services
- Children with known structural urological abnormalities (e.g., posterior urethral valves, hydronephrosis): febrile illness in these children is a serious event requiring immediate hospital assessment
- Children with sickle cell disease: structural kidney abnormalities common; Salmonella and other organisms cause UTI in addition to usual pathogens
