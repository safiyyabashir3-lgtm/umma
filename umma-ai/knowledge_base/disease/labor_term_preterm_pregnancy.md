# LABOUR — TERM AND PRETERM — PREGNANCY DISEASE FILE
## For UMMA AI — Maternal & Child Health Companion (Kano/Northwest Nigeria Context)

---

## OVERVIEW

Labour is the process of progressive uterine contractions leading to cervical dilation and effacement, resulting in delivery of the fetus and placenta. This file covers normal term labour (≥37 weeks), preterm labour (PTL, 22–36+6 weeks), precipitate labour, obstructed labour, and labour-related emergencies that UMMA must identify and triage. In Nigerian context, distinguishing "this is normal labour — go to facility" from "this is an emergency — go NOW" is the primary clinical task.

**Kano/Northwest Nigeria epidemiology:**
- Preterm birth rate in Nigeria estimated at 11–14% — among the highest globally
- Obstructed labour is a major cause of maternal and neonatal mortality, particularly in adolescent girls with contracted pelvis from early marriage and pregnancy
- Home delivery rates remain above 60% in some northern LGAs — many deliveries attended by traditional birth attendants (TBAs) with limited skills
- Vesico-vaginal fistula (VVF) is a direct consequence of prolonged obstructed labour — Kano is within the VVF belt of northwest Nigeria
- Many women do not know their gestational age reliably — UMMA must work with symptom and clinical features, not dates alone
- Pre-labour rupture of membranes (PROM) and preterm PROM (PPROM) are underrecognised — women often unaware that fluid leaking is significant

**Why it matters for UMMA:**
- UMMA's primary role is to direct women to appropriate facility in time — "facility delivery" messaging must be actionable with transport advice
- UMMA must recognise when labour is too advanced for safe transport
- UMMA must identify danger signs: prolonged labour, obstructed labour, cord prolapse, abruption in labour, fetal distress
- Preterm labour at <34 weeks requires urgent tertiary referral — corticosteroids can save neonatal lives if given before delivery

**Common delays and barriers:**
- Decision delay: labour interpreted as "stomach ache" or "false pain" — women hesitant to go to facility until pain unbearable
- Transport delay: most critical window (active labour) coincides with urgency of transport need
- Financial barriers: facility delivery fees, birth registration costs
- TBA presence: many families prefer TBA delivery; TBA may delay referral until delivery complicated
- Night-time delivery: transport options severely limited after dark in most LGAs

## HOW IT PRESENTS IN NIGERIAN PATIENTS

### Mild Presentation (Early/latent labour or preterm contractions — non-emergency)

**Term labour, latent phase:**
- Irregular contractions, 5–10 minutes apart, lasting 20–30 seconds
- Mild lower abdominal cramping, "like period pain but stronger"
- Mucus plug or "show" — blood-tinged mucus discharge
- Lower backache
- Cervix 0–3 cm dilated (not assessable by UMMA — guide to facility)
- Fetal movements perceived as normal
- Membranes intact
- No fever, no foul-smelling discharge

**Preterm contractions (mild, <34 weeks, not yet in established labour):**
- Intermittent lower abdominal tightening before 37 weeks
- Pelvic pressure "like baby is pushing down"
- Increased vaginal discharge (non-offensive)
- Lower backache, may be constant
- No membrane rupture
- Contractions may resolve with rest and hydration

### Moderate Presentation (Active labour or preterm labour in established phase)

**Active term labour:**
- Regular contractions every 3–5 minutes, lasting 45–60 seconds, strong enough to stop walking
- Moderate to strong pain with each contraction
- Progressive cervical dilation (4–7 cm)
- Spontaneous rupture of membranes — clear or slightly blood-stained fluid
- Increasing urge to push (late active phase)
- Woman distressed but oriented and communicating

**Established preterm labour (34–36+6 weeks):**
- Regular contractions before 37 weeks, similar pattern to term active labour
- May present with PPROM (leaking fluid before contractions)
- Cervix dilating
- Increased risk — needs facility delivery even if seems manageable

**Preterm labour <34 weeks:**
- Any regular contractions before 34 weeks = urgent
- Even if contractions mild, potential for rapid progression
- Corticosteroid window: if not yet delivered, 2 doses betamethasone/dexamethasone can dramatically improve neonatal survival

### Severe Presentation (Obstructed labour, fetal distress, cord prolapse, precipitate labour emergencies)

**Obstructed labour:**
- Prolonged active labour >12 hours in multipara, >20 hours in primipara without delivery
- Severe unremitting pain between contractions (no relaxation) — uterus not relaxing
- Lower uterine segment tender to touch, may be visible/palpable as a transverse ridge (Bandl's ring — late sign)
- Maternal exhaustion, dehydration, tachycardia
- Foul-smelling vaginal discharge or liquor if infection supervening
- Fetal presenting part not descending despite good contractions
- Fetal heart rate abnormalities if monitored
- In extremis: uterine rupture — sudden cessation of contractions, severe abdominal pain, maternal collapse, fetal parts palpable under abdominal wall

**Cord prolapse:**
- Following membrane rupture, cord visible at introitus or felt by woman/TBA
- Sudden fetal heart rate drop if monitored
- Woman or attendant may feel "something coming down"
- Absolute obstetric emergency

**Placental abruption in labour:**
- Sudden severe abdominal pain, uterus "board-hard" and not relaxing
- Heavy dark vaginal bleeding OR concealed bleeding with abdominal distension
- Maternal shock (tachycardia, hypotension, pallor)
- Fetal distress or absent fetal heart
- May co-exist with preeclampsia (hypertensive abruption)

**Precipitate labour:**
- Very rapid labour <3 hours from onset to delivery
- High risk of unattended delivery, perineal tear, neonatal injury
- More common in grand multiparae

**Ruptured uterus (most severe — usually from obstructed labour):**
- Sudden relief of pain after prolonged agony — paradoxical "calm"
- Maternal collapse, shock
- Abdomen soft but extremely tender
- Fetal parts palpable superficially under abdominal skin
- Cessation of contractions
- Heavy vaginal bleeding or haemoperitoneum (may be minimal external bleeding)

## DIFFERENTIATING FEATURES

**True labour vs. Braxton Hicks contractions:**
- Braxton Hicks: irregular, no progressive increase in frequency/intensity, resolve with rest and hydration, not painful, no show, no membrane rupture
- True labour: regular, progressive, do not resolve with rest, associated show or membrane rupture, cervical change

**Preterm labour vs. UTI/pyelonephritis:**
- UTI: dysuria, frequency, loin pain, fever, costovertebral tenderness — can trigger preterm contractions
- May co-exist: UTI is a major cause of preterm labour in Nigeria — treat both
- Urine dipstick for leucocytes/nitrites helpful if available

**Obstructed labour vs. Normal prolonged labour:**
- Normal prolonged labour: contractions still regular, uterus relaxing between contractions, woman progressing slowly but not deteriorating
- Obstructed: no uterine relaxation, fetal head not descending, maternal deterioration, lower uterine segment tenderness

**Placental abruption vs. Placenta praevia in labour:**
- Abruption: painful, tender uterus, dark blood, uterus hard — fetal distress prominent
- Praevia: PAINLESS bright red bleeding, uterus soft — do NOT do vaginal examination if praevia suspected

**Uterine rupture vs. Severe abruption:**
- Rupture: sudden pain relief then collapse, fetal parts very superficial, uterine contour lost
- Abruption: continued pain, uterus still palpable as mass, bleeding more prominent

## SEVERITY ASSESSMENT

### Mild criteria
- Term pregnancy (≥37 weeks) in latent phase labour
- Contractions irregular, <5 per 10 minutes
- Membranes intact or clear fluid draining only
- Normal fetal movement
- No fever, no offensive discharge
- No bleeding beyond mucus show
- Woman mobile, communicating normally
- Preterm contractions (<37 weeks) that are irregular, non-progressive, no membrane rupture

### Moderate criteria
- Active established labour at term — regular painful contractions, membranes may have ruptured
- Preterm labour 34–36+6 weeks in active phase
- Preterm labour at ANY gestation with ruptured membranes (PPROM)
- Preterm labour <34 weeks even if contractions mild — corticosteroid urgency
- Labour progressing but no immediate danger signs
- Primigravida in early active labour with long distance to travel — needs to go NOW before transport window closes

### Severe criteria — ANY ONE = emergency, go NOW
- Contractions with NO relaxation between (uterus not relaxing) at any gestation
- Maternal collapse, shock, or loss of consciousness
- Heavy vaginal bleeding (beyond show) with abdominal pain
- Cord visible at vaginal opening or felt by attendant
- Fetal parts palpable superficially through abdomen (uterine rupture)
- Sudden cessation of contractions after prolonged painful labour
- Labour >12 hours active phase without delivery (multipara) or >20 hours (primipara)
- Foul-smelling vaginal discharge in labour with fever
- Active labour at <34 weeks gestation
- Woman unable to communicate — confused, unconscious
- Rapid labour in grand multipara with delivery imminent — birth imminent management needed

## MANAGEMENT BY SEVERITY

### Mild
**Clinical actions:**
- Advise facility attendance for assessment — not emergency but should not deliver at home
- For term latent labour: go to facility when contractions are regular and 5 minutes apart, or if membranes rupture, or if any bleeding/reduced fetal movement
- For preterm contractions: go to facility immediately regardless of contraction strength — gestational age makes this higher priority
- Hydration advice while awaiting transport (oral fluids)
- Advise not to push unless instructed

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"It sounds like your labour may be starting. For a safe delivery, you should go to the health centre or hospital. You don't need to rush this moment, but please go soon — don't wait until the pain is very strong or you may not have time. Take your ANC card. If your waters break, if you bleed more than a small blood discharge, if the pain becomes constant without stopping, or if your baby stops moving — go immediately."

*For preterm (<37 weeks):*
"Your baby may be coming early. Even if the pain is not very strong, you must go to the hospital now. Babies that come early need special care that only the hospital can give. Please go immediately."

### Moderate
**Clinical actions:**
- Go to facility NOW — active labour, cannot be managed at home
- For preterm <34 weeks: tertiary facility urgently (AKTH or FMC) — corticosteroids needed
- Ensure transport immediately — estimate time to delivery versus time to facility
- If transport will take >1 hour and active labour is progressing rapidly, prepare for possibility of delivery before arrival
- PPROM at any gestation: facility now — infection risk and preterm delivery risk both high
- Do not perform vaginal examination at home

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"You are in active labour and you need to go to the hospital right now. Do not wait. Take your ANC card and have someone with you. If you feel like you need to push, tell the driver and try not to push until a health worker is with you. If the baby comes before you reach the hospital, keep the baby warm and do not cut the cord until someone trained can help you."

*For preterm <34 weeks:*
"This is very urgent. Your baby is coming too early. You must go to AKTH or FMC right now — a big hospital with a special baby unit. There is an injection the doctors can give you that will help your baby's lungs before birth. Every minute matters. Go now."

### Severe
**Clinical actions:**

*Obstructed labour / prolonged labour:*
- Emergency — caesarean section required; direct to nearest surgical obstetric facility
- Do not advise further pushing
- Keep woman hydrated if conscious
- Uterine rupture: maternal shock management en route — IV access if anyone trained present, left lateral position

*Cord prolapse:*
- Emergency delivery required — tell attendant: get into knee-chest position (woman on knees, chest down, bottom up) to relieve cord pressure, do NOT push
- Someone should support cord to prevent compression — gloved hand or clean cloth, keep cord moist
- Race to operating facility

*Abruption / suspected rupture:*
- Maternal collapse: left lateral position, do not give food or drink
- Emergency transport to surgical facility
- Inform facility ahead if possible

*Imminent delivery — cannot reach facility:*
- Advise clean delivery: clean hands, clean surface, clean cord cutting with new razor blade only
- Keep baby warm immediately after birth — dry, skin to skin, cover head
- Do not pull on cord — wait for placenta to deliver naturally
- Breastfeed immediately after delivery
- Watch for excessive bleeding after delivery
- Go to facility after delivery regardless — mother and baby need assessment

**Plain language for UMMA to adapt:**
"This is a life-threatening emergency. You must go to the hospital immediately — AKTH or a big hospital with an operating theatre. Do not wait for anything. [Specific instruction per scenario above.] Tell the hospital what is happening when you call or arrive so they are ready."

## NIGERIAN SPECIFIC WARNINGS

**Dangerous practices:**
- TBA fundal pressure ("pushing on the belly") during obstructed labour — causes uterine rupture; extremely common
- Oxytocin given by TBAs or patent medicine vendors to "speed up" labour — inappropriate use without monitoring causes tetanic contractions and uterine rupture
- Herbal uterotonic drinks and vaginal preparations to augment labour — some contain ergot-like compounds; unpredictable and dangerous
- Episiotomy performed with unsterile instruments by TBAs
- Early cord cutting with contaminated instruments — neonatal tetanus
- Withholding water and food from labouring women as traditional practice — worsens dehydration and maternal exhaustion
- Vaginal examination by multiple non-sterile hands — infection risk

**Infrastructure limitations:**
- No blood bank at PHC or many secondary facilities — postpartum haemorrhage and ruptured uterus require tertiary facility
- No neonatal ICU outside AKTH and FMC — preterm <34 weeks survival requires AKTH/FMC referral
- Caesarean section not available at PHC level — obstructed labour must be referred early
- Theatre delays at AKTH due to volume — travel time + wait time must be factored
- Intravenous oxytocin and magnesium sulfate not universally available at secondary level
- Many PHCs unstaffed at night — direct to available staffed facility

**Cultural factors:**
- Prolonged labour interpreted as spiritual punishment or result of infidelity — may delay seeking care
- Women expected to labour silently — cries of pain may be suppressed, making assessment of labour stage by phone difficult
- Grand multipara women considered experienced and may resist referral ("I have delivered 6 children at home")
- TBA relationships are deep community relationships — dismissing TBA advice is culturally sensitive; UMMA should work with, not against, TBA involvement where possible
- Male permission for referral, especially for caesarean section — cultural barrier to emergency surgery
- VVF survivors often hidden from community — high stigma around obstetric complications

**High-risk subgroups:**
- Adolescent primigravidae (contracted pelvis, obstructed labour risk)
- Grand multiparae (uterine rupture, precipitate labour, cord prolapse risk)
- Short stature women (cephalopelvic disproportion)
- Women with previous caesarean scar (uterine rupture in labour)
- Multiple pregnancy (preterm labour, malpresentation)
- Women with no ANC — unknown presentation, unknown complications
- Preterm labour <34 weeks (neonatal survival requires specialised facility)
