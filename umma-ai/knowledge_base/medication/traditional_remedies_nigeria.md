# traditional_remedies_nigeria.md
# UMMA Medication File — Traditional and Herbal Remedies in Nigeria
# Category: Medications | Population: Pregnant women and children under 5
# Context: Kano and northwest Nigeria
# Last reviewed: 2025
# Approach: Respectful, evidence-based, non-judgmental. Cultural practices are acknowledged.
#            Safety is paramount. UMMA never dismisses; UMMA redirects when there is risk.

---

## OVERVIEW

Traditional and herbal remedies are deeply embedded in health practices in Kano and northwest Nigeria. They are used widely by pregnant women and mothers for their children, often as first-line treatment, and frequently alongside or instead of biomedical care. UMMA must approach this topic with cultural respect — many remedies are harmless or have real benefit, and dismissing all traditional medicine damages trust.

However, specific remedies cause documented harm in pregnancy and in young children. UMMA must be able to identify these, explain the risk clearly and respectfully, and redirect to safer alternatives.

**UMMA's tone on this topic:** "I respect that these medicines have been used in our families for a long time. Some are safe and some can cause harm — I want to help you know which is which, so you can keep yourself and your baby safe."

## COMMONLY USED TRADITIONAL REMEDIES — SAFETY REFERENCE

### Agbo (herbal concoctions — variable composition)
**What it is:** Liquid herbal preparations made from mixtures of roots, bark, leaves, and other plant materials. Extremely widely used in Kano and across Nigeria. Composition is highly variable and often unknown. Sold by herbalists, at markets, and increasingly bottled commercially.

**Pregnancy:**
- SIGNIFICANT RISK — many agbo preparations contain compounds with oxytocic (uterus-contracting) activity; these can cause miscarriage, preterm labour, or uterine hyperstimulation
- Some agbo preparations contain hepatotoxic alkaloids — risk of liver damage in pregnancy when the liver is already under physiological stress
- Some preparations have been found to contain heavy metals (lead, mercury) — fetal neurotoxicity
- "Agbo jedi-jedi" — preparations for stomach complaints frequently contain high-dose senna and other purgatives; in pregnancy, strong purgatives stimulate uterine contractions
- The unknown composition makes it impossible to assess safety for any specific preparation

**Children under 5:**
- SIGNIFICANT RISK — agbo given to infants and young children has been associated with liver failure, acute kidney injury, and death in Nigerian paediatric literature
- Neonatal jaundice worsened by traditional herbal remedies is a well-documented pattern in Kano — some preparations cause haemolysis in G6PD-deficient infants (G6PD deficiency is common in Nigeria)
- Agbo given to manage fever instead of appropriate antimalarials causes dangerous delay

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Agbo preparations are made from many different plants, and it is very difficult to know which ones are safe in pregnancy or for babies. Some of them contain ingredients that can cause the womb to contract too early, damage the liver, or harm a young child's kidneys. I am not saying all herbal medicine is bad — but during pregnancy and for young babies, please avoid agbo until we know exactly what is in it. There are safer medicines available for what you are treating."

### Ginger (Zingiber officinale) — Citta in Hausa
**What it is:** Ginger root, ginger tea, ginger preparations

**Pregnancy:** SAFE at food and tea quantities — evidence supports modest benefit for nausea and vomiting of pregnancy; widely used and considered safe at culinary doses

**Children under 5:** SAFE at food amounts; ginger tea in small amounts for stomach complaints is low risk

**UMMA note:** One of the few traditional remedies with evidence of benefit and a good safety profile. UMMA can actively endorse ginger tea for nausea in pregnancy.

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Ginger tea is safe in pregnancy and can actually help with nausea — this is one traditional remedy that the evidence supports. Fresh ginger in food is also fine."

---

### Neem (Azadirachta indica) — Dogonyaro in Hausa
**What it is:** Neem leaves, neem bark, neem seed oil used in teas, decoctions, and topical preparations. Extremely common in northern Nigeria for malaria, fever, and skin conditions.

**Pregnancy:** AVOID internally — neem has documented abortifacient and uterotonic activity in animal studies; neem seed oil is particularly concerning; internal use during pregnancy is not safe. Topical use on skin for rash is lower risk but poorly studied.

**Children under 5:** AVOID internally — neem oil ingestion in children has caused toxic encephalopathy (Reye-like syndrome) with fatal outcomes reported in Nigerian children; neem should never be given orally to young children

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Dogonyaro (neem) tea or neem preparations taken by mouth are not safe in pregnancy — they can cause the womb to contract. For babies and young children, neem taken by mouth has caused serious brain damage. Please do not give neem medicine by mouth to a child or take it yourself while pregnant. For malaria, proper clinic medicine is needed."

---

### Black seed / Habbatus sauda (Nigella sativa)
**What it is:** Black seed oil and seeds — widely used in Kano for immune support, respiratory infections, and general wellness. Strong religious and cultural endorsement in Muslim communities.

**Pregnancy:** CAUTION — at culinary quantities (seeds in food), considered low risk. Black seed oil in large medicinal doses has shown uterotonic activity in some studies — large doses should be avoided in pregnancy. Moderate use as a condiment or small supplement is unlikely to cause harm.

**Children under 5:** LOW RISK at small amounts; not established as a treatment for any specific childhood illness

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Using black seed in small amounts — like in your food — is fine in pregnancy. Large amounts of black seed oil taken as medicine during pregnancy is best avoided. For illness, please see a doctor for proper treatment rather than relying on black seed alone."

---

### Moringa (Moringa oleifera) — Zogale in Hausa
**What it is:** Moringa leaves used in cooking and as nutritional supplement — extremely nutritious; high in iron, vitamins, and protein

**Pregnancy:** SAFE as a food — moringa leaves in cooking are nutritious and safe; moringa is actively encouraged as a nutritional supplement in many maternal health programmes in Nigeria. The root, bark, and seeds of moringa have oxytocic activity — only the leaves should be used.

**Children under 5:** SAFE as a food — moringa leaves are an excellent nutritional supplement for malnourished children

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Zogale (moringa) leaves in your food are safe and actually very nutritious for pregnant women — they contain iron and vitamins that are good for you and your baby. Just use the leaves in cooking, not the root or bark."

### Traditional preparations to induce or "bring down" menstruation / end pregnancy
**What they are:** Various preparations sold at herbal markets and by traditional practitioners specifically intended to restart a delayed period, "cleanse the womb," or induce abortion. Often contain high-dose plant oxytocics — extracts of plants in the Euphorbiaceae, Piperaceae families, or synthetic misoprostol dissolved into herbal mixtures.

**Pregnancy:** EXTREMELY DANGEROUS — these preparations cause:
- Uterine hyperstimulation and miscarriage (often incomplete — requiring hospital management)
- Uterine rupture
- Severe haemorrhage
- Sepsis from incomplete abortion
- Maternal death
- Fetal limb defects if abortion is incomplete and pregnancy continues

**Children under 5:** Not applicable directly — but children in households where these are present are at risk of accidental ingestion

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Any preparation sold to 'bring down your period,' 'clean your womb,' or end a pregnancy is extremely dangerous. These cause severe bleeding and can kill. If you have taken something like this, please go to the hospital immediately — you do not need to explain everything, just tell them you are bleeding and in pain. The doctors are there to help you."

### Camphor (kapur) preparations — applied or inhaled
**What it is:** Camphor in oil, cream, or block form applied to chest, feet, or skin for cough and respiratory symptoms. Sometimes dissolved in warm water for steam inhalation.

**Pregnancy:** AVOID large amounts topically or inhalation — camphor is absorbed through skin; systemic toxicity at high doses; avoid near oral/nasal mucosa. Small amounts in Vicks-type products at low concentration are lower risk but not recommended near the face.

**Children under 5:** DANGEROUS — camphor toxicity in infants causes seizures, liver damage, and death. Even small amounts of pure camphor can be fatal in young infants. Never apply to a baby's face, nostrils, or in significant amounts to the skin. Camphor-containing products should not be used in children under 2 years.

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Camphor (kapur) should never be rubbed on a baby's skin or put near her nose — even small amounts can be poisonous to young babies and cause fits. For cough in a child, go to the clinic. For a pregnant woman, avoid applying camphor in large amounts."

---

### Groundnut oil, shea butter, olive oil — used topically
**Pregnancy:** SAFE topically — harmless for skin and perineal massage
**Children under 5:** SAFE topically — traditional skin moisturisation is harmless

---

### Urine therapy — drinking or applying urine for various conditions
**Pregnancy:** NOT SAFE — no evidence of benefit; oral ingestion introduces bacteria and waste products; applying to skin is harmless but ineffective
**Children under 5:** NOT SAFE — applying urine to eyes (for conjunctivitis) or skin (for rash) introduces bacteria and can worsen infection; never apply to eyes

---

### Traditional eye treatments (breastmilk, herbal eye drops, animal products)
**What it is:** Breast milk, herbal water, honey, or animal secretions applied directly to a child's eye for conjunctivitis or eye infections.

**Children under 5:** DANGEROUS — introduces organisms into the eye; bacterial conjunctivitis worsens; corneal scarring and blindness can result; neonatal gonococcal conjunctivitis requires systemic antibiotics immediately and not eye drops alone.

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Please do not put breast milk, herbal water, or anything else homemade in your child's eye. Even though this is a common practice, it can make the infection worse and damage the eye permanently. Go to the clinic for proper eye drops."

---

### Salt water and sugar solutions made at home for diarrhoea
**What it is:** Homemade ORS-type solutions of salt and sugar in water — varying proportions

**Children under 5:** CAUTION — homemade salt-sugar solutions are NOT equivalent to proper ORS sachets. Too much salt causes hypernatraemia (dangerous high blood sodium) in a dehydrated child. Too little salt is ineffective. Proper ORS sachets must be used.

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"I know sometimes people make their own salt and sugar water for diarrhoea. The problem is that if the amount of salt is not exactly right, it can actually be harmful — too much salt in a dehydrated baby is very dangerous. Please use a proper ORS sachet from the clinic or pharmacy — they have exactly the right amounts."

## WHAT TO DO IF ALREADY TAKEN — TRADITIONAL REMEDIES

### UMMA framework:

**Immediately go to hospital:**
- Any preparation taken to end a pregnancy or "bring down a period" — bleeding or not
- Neem oil given to a child — any amount
- Child has been given any preparation and is now vomiting repeatedly, has altered consciousness, seizures, or is not waking normally
- Camphor applied to a baby's face/nostrils and baby now seems drowsy or unwell

**Go to clinic today:**
- Agbo or herbal preparation taken during pregnancy — even without symptoms; clinician should know
- Child given agbo for fever instead of antimalarial and fever persisting or worsening — needs proper malaria assessment

**Reassure and advise ANC disclosure:**
- Small amount of moringa, ginger, or other food-quantity herbal use — safe; mention at ANC
- Single use of a low-risk traditional preparation with no symptoms — monitor; mention at next visit

**UMMA's tone when a woman has taken something concerning:**
"I am not here to make you feel bad about what you have taken — I know these remedies are part of how families care for their children and themselves. But I need to make sure you are safe. Some preparations can cause harm even when they are given with the best intentions. Let us make sure you and your baby are okay — please go to the clinic today so they can check you over."
