# UMMA Medication File — Safe Medications in Pregnancy
# Category: Medications | Population: Pregnant women
# Last reviewed: 2025
# NOTE: UMMA never gives doses. Always refer to doctor or pharmacist for dosing decisions.

---

## OVERVIEW

This file covers medications that are considered safe for use in pregnancy, with appropriate caveats. "Safe" in this context means that the available evidence does not demonstrate significant fetal harm when used at recommended doses for appropriate indications. All medications should be used at the lowest effective dose for the shortest necessary duration, and a doctor or pharmacist should always be consulted before starting any medication in pregnancy.

UMMA's role: confirm safety, explain caveats, refer for dosing and prescription.

---

## SAFE MEDICATIONS

### Paracetamol (Acetaminophen)
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Panadol, Emzor Paracetamol, M&B Paracetamol, Hedex

**Why it is safe:**
Paracetamol is the first-line pain and fever medicine in pregnancy. Decades of use and extensive data confirm no association with fetal abnormality when used at recommended doses for short durations. It does not affect platelet function and does not cause the bleeding risk associated with NSAIDs.

**Important caveats:**
- Safe when used at recommended doses — overuse or prolonged daily use at high doses has been associated in some studies with small increases in risk of neurodevelopmental effects in the child; this is not established but caution around prolonged use is reasonable
- Do not exceed recommended doses — paracetamol overdose causes severe liver failure in the mother
- If liver disease is present: discuss with doctor before use
- Does not treat the cause of fever — malaria causing fever must still be diagnosed and treated

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Paracetamol (Panadol) is the safest medicine for pain and fever in pregnancy. It is fine to take when you need it — for headache, fever, or body pain. Use it only when you need it and not every day for a long time. Always check with a pharmacist or doctor about the right amount to take."

### Iron and Folic Acid Supplements
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Fesolate, Pregnacare, Ferrous sulphate + folic acid (various), Haematogen, Chemiron

**Why it is safe:**
Iron and folic acid are not only safe but essential in pregnancy. Folic acid prevents neural tube defects (spina bifida) — ideally started before conception and continued through the first trimester. Iron treats and prevents iron deficiency anaemia, which is extremely common in northern Nigerian pregnant women and contributes significantly to maternal mortality.

**Important caveats:**
- Iron tablets commonly cause nausea, constipation, and dark stools — these are normal side effects, not signs of harm
- Take with food or at night to reduce nausea
- Do not take with tea, coffee, or calcium supplements — these reduce iron absorption; take with water or orange juice
- Black stools from iron are normal — not a sign of bleeding
- If vomiting is severe (hyperemesis), iron tablets may need to be held temporarily — discuss with doctor

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Iron and folic acid tablets are safe and very important in pregnancy — they protect you from anaemia and protect your baby's spine from developing abnormally. The black stools and constipation they cause are normal. Take them with a little food and not with tea. If they make you feel sick, try taking them at night before you sleep."

### Folic Acid alone (periconceptional and first trimester)
**Why it is safe:** No risk to mother or fetus. Recommended 400 mcg daily for all women trying to conceive and through the first trimester; higher doses for women with previous neural tube defect, sickle cell disease, or on anti-epileptic drugs — doctor to advise on dose.

### Antacids — Magnesium trisilicate, Aluminium hydroxide
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Gestid, Mucaine, Andrews, various generic antacid syrups

**Why it is safe:**
Antacids relieve the heartburn and acid reflux that are extremely common in pregnancy (particularly second and third trimester) due to progesterone relaxing the lower oesophageal sphincter. Magnesium and aluminium-based antacids are not absorbed systemically and are safe throughout pregnancy.

**Important caveats:**
- Sodium bicarbonate (baking soda) — avoid in large amounts; causes fluid retention and alkalosis
- Antacids containing aspirin (e.g. some Alka-Seltzer formulations) — avoid in pregnancy
- Take antacids separately from iron tablets — antacids reduce iron absorption; space them by at least 2 hours
- Persistent severe heartburn or difficulty swallowing: see a doctor

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Heartburn in pregnancy is very common and antacid medicines like Gestid are safe to use. Avoid any antacid that contains aspirin. Take your iron tablets and antacids at different times — not together."

### Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS)
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Dioralyte, Electroral, various WHO-formula ORS sachets

**Why it is safe:**
ORS is safe at any stage of pregnancy and is the correct first response to dehydration from vomiting, diarrhoea, or poor fluid intake. It replaces lost electrolytes safely.

**Important caveats:**
- ORS treats dehydration — it does not treat the cause (malaria, gastroenteritis, hyperemesis)
- If unable to keep ORS down: needs IV fluids at hospital
- Mix correctly — do not add extra sugar or salt beyond the sachet

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"ORS sachets are completely safe in pregnancy and are the best way to replace fluids when you are vomiting or have diarrhoea. If you cannot keep even small sips down, you need to go to the clinic for a drip."

### Artemisinin-based Combination Therapy (ACT) for malaria — second and third trimester
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Lonart (artemether-lumefantrine), Coartem, Artequick, Duo-Cotecxin (dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine)

**Why it is safe:**
ACT is the recommended treatment for uncomplicated malaria in the second and third trimesters of pregnancy. Malaria in pregnancy is dangerous — causing anaemia, fetal growth restriction, preterm birth, and maternal death. The risk of untreated malaria far exceeds any theoretical risk from ACT in the second and third trimesters.

**Important caveats:**
- First trimester: ACT is used with caution — artemisinins have shown embryotoxicity in animal studies; in the first trimester, quinine + clindamycin is the preferred regimen; however, where no alternative is available, ACT is still used because untreated malaria is more dangerous — this decision must be made by a clinician
- Never use chloroquine alone — widespread resistance in Nigeria makes it ineffective
- Never use artemisinin monotherapy (artemether alone without a partner drug) — causes treatment failure and drives resistance
- ACT requires full course completion — stopping early causes treatment failure

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"If you have malaria in pregnancy, you need proper treatment — malaria is very dangerous for you and your baby. In the second and third months of pregnancy and beyond, the combination malaria medicines (like Lonart or Coartem) are safe. Do not buy just artemether alone — you need the combination. Go to the clinic so they can prescribe the right one."

### Sulphadoxine-Pyrimethamine (SP) — Intermittent Preventive Treatment in Pregnancy (IPTp)
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Fansidar, Maloxine

**Why it is safe:**
SP given as IPTp is recommended for all pregnant women in malaria-endemic areas (including all of Kano) from the second trimester onward. It prevents malaria and has been shown to reduce anaemia, low birth weight, and perinatal mortality. It is given as directly observed therapy at ANC visits — typically at least 3 doses in the second and third trimester.

**Important caveats:**
- Do not take in the first trimester — avoid before 13 weeks
- Do not take if allergic to sulphonamides
- Do not take if already on co-trimoxazole (e.g. HIV-positive women on CTX prophylaxis) — same drug class; discuss with doctor; HIV-positive women may not receive SP IPTp and need alternative malaria prevention

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"The malaria prevention tablet (Fansidar) that the nurse gives you at your ANC visits is safe and important — it protects you and your baby from malaria. Make sure you get it at every ANC visit after the first three months. Do not take it on your own at home — it is given at the clinic."

### Antifungals — Topical Clotrimazole
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Canesten, Gyno-Daktarin, various clotrimazole pessaries and creams

**Why it is safe:**
Topical clotrimazole (vaginal pessary and cream) is safe throughout pregnancy for treatment of vulvovaginal candidiasis. It is not absorbed systemically and poses no risk to the fetus.

**Important caveats:**
- Oral fluconazole (Diflucan): avoid in pregnancy, especially first trimester — associated with cardiac defects with repeated high doses; topical treatment is always preferred in pregnancy
- Applicator use: use the cream by hand rather than applicator in late pregnancy if uncomfortable — discuss with pharmacist
- If symptoms do not resolve: attend clinic — may need longer course or alternative diagnosis

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"The Canesten cream or pessary is safe to use in pregnancy for vaginal itching and discharge. Do not take the Diflucan tablet (fluconazole) for this while pregnant — use the cream instead. If you are not sure, ask the pharmacist."

### Metronidazole — second and third trimester
**Brand names commonly found in Nigeria:** Flagyl, Metro, various generic metronidazole

**Why it is safe:**
Metronidazole is used to treat bacterial vaginosis, trichomoniasis, and certain intestinal infections. In the second and third trimesters, it is considered safe.

**Important caveats:**
- First trimester: historically avoided, though recent evidence suggests it is likely safe; clinician should make this decision
- Avoid alcohol while taking metronidazole — severe nausea and vomiting reaction
- Full course must be completed
- Do not self-purchase for vague symptoms — should be prescribed after clinical assessment

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Flagyl (metronidazole) is used for certain vaginal and stomach infections and is safe in the later months of pregnancy when prescribed by a doctor. Do not drink alcohol while taking it. Always finish the full course."

---

### Antihistamines — Chlorphenamine (Piriton) and Loratadine
**Why it is safe:**
Chlorphenamine (first generation) and loratadine (second generation) are considered safe in pregnancy for allergic conditions. Loratadine is preferred as it is non-sedating.

**Important caveats:**
- Avoid antihistamines in the first trimester where possible — use only if clearly needed
- Chlorphenamine causes drowsiness — avoid driving or operating machinery
- Not a treatment for vomiting of pregnancy — promethazine (a related antihistamine) is used for HG but requires prescription

---

### Magnesium Sulphate
**Why it is safe:**
Magnesium sulphate is the treatment of choice for eclampsia (seizures in pregnancy) and severe pre-eclampsia. It is life-saving and is given at hospital. Not a home medication — mentioned here because women may be told they are receiving it at hospital and ask UMMA about it.

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"Magnesium sulphate is a medicine the hospital gives through a drip to prevent fits in pregnancy. It is safe and it saves lives — if the doctors are giving it to you, please accept it."

---

### Co-trimoxazole (Cotrimoxazole / Septrin) — HIV-positive pregnant women
**Why it is safe:**
Co-trimoxazole prophylaxis is recommended for all HIV-positive pregnant women to prevent opportunistic infections (PCP, toxoplasmosis). The benefits clearly outweigh risks in this population.

**Important caveats:**
- Not for routine use in HIV-negative pregnant women
- Do not take alongside SP (IPTp) — same sulphonamide class; discuss with HIV clinic
- Folate supplementation important alongside CTX use

---

### Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) in pregnancy
**Why it is safe:**
ART is essential for HIV-positive pregnant women — it prevents mother-to-child transmission of HIV (PMTCT) and maintains maternal health. Modern first-line ART regimens used in Nigeria are safe in pregnancy.

**Important caveats:**
- Must never be stopped in pregnancy — stopping ART risks maternal health and dramatically increases MTCT risk
- If woman is newly diagnosed in pregnancy: start ART as soon as possible — same day if possible
- Side effects should be discussed with HIV clinic, not managed by stopping medication

**What UMMA tells the user:**
"If you are HIV-positive and pregnant, taking your ARV medicines every day is the most important thing you can do for yourself and your baby. These medicines are safe in pregnancy and they protect your baby from getting HIV. Never stop them without talking to your doctor first."

## WHAT TO DO IF ALREADY TAKEN

If a woman has taken a medication listed here and is concerned:

**UMMA framework:**
1. Reassure — the medication is considered safe
2. Explain the caveat if relevant (e.g. first trimester use where evidence is less complete)
3. Advise mentioning it at next ANC visit
4. Do not cause panic — the medications in this file do not warrant emergency attendance based on a single dose or short course

**Example — took paracetamol before knowing she was pregnant:**
"Paracetamol is safe in pregnancy — taking it before you knew you were pregnant will not have harmed your baby. Mention it to your doctor or midwife at your next visit, but there is nothing to worry about."
