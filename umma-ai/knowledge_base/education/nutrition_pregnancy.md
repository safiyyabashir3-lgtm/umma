# Nutrition During Pregnancy

**Topic:** Eating Well During Pregnancy
**Guideline Source:** Federal Ministry of Health Nigeria Nutrition Guidelines; WHO Nutrition in Pregnancy; UNICEF Nigeria
**Context:** Kano State, Northern Nigeria

---

## OVERVIEW

What you eat during pregnancy matters — for your health and for your baby's growth and development. A well-nourished pregnant woman is less likely to have a complicated pregnancy or difficult delivery. Her baby is more likely to be born at a healthy weight and grow up strong.

Good nutrition in pregnancy does not mean expensive food. It means eating a variety of local foods that together give your body what it needs. Many of the best foods for pregnancy are already eaten in Kano and are affordable and accessible.

This file explains what to eat, what to limit, what supplements you need, and how to manage common challenges like nausea, food cravings, and limited money for food.

---

## KEY INFORMATION

### What your body needs more of during pregnancy

**1. Energy (calories)**
You need slightly more food during pregnancy — especially in the second and third trimesters. Do not eat for two, but eat a little more at each meal, or add a healthy snack.

**2. Protein — for your baby's growth**
Protein builds your baby's muscles, organs, and brain. Good sources include:
- Beans, lentils (wake, black-eyed peas, chickpeas)
- Eggs
- Fish (titus, catfish, sardines)
- Meat and chicken (in moderate amounts)
- Groundnuts (peanuts) and groundnut soup
- Soya beans

**3. Iron — to prevent anaemia**
During pregnancy, your blood volume increases and your baby needs iron too. Not enough iron causes anaemia — you feel very tired, weak, and dizzy. Anaemia increases the risk of bleeding problems during delivery.

Good sources of iron:
- Dark green leafy vegetables: spinach, ugu (fluted pumpkin leaves), bitter leaf
- Beans and lentils
- Meat and organ meats (liver is very rich in iron — eat once a week)
- Fortified foods (some cereals and flour have added iron)

**Tip:** Eating foods rich in Vitamin C (like tomatoes, oranges, guava) at the same meal helps your body absorb iron better.

**4. Folic acid — to protect your baby's spine and brain**
Folic acid is most important in the first 12 weeks of pregnancy — it prevents serious birth defects of the brain and spine. This is why you should start antenatal care early and take your folic acid tablets from the clinic every day.

Food sources of folate (natural folic acid):
- Green leafy vegetables
- Beans and lentils
- Eggs

**5. Calcium — for your baby's bones and teeth**
- Milk, yoghurt (fura da nono)
- Fish with soft bones (like sardines)
- Beans
- Dark green vegetables

**6. Vitamin A — for immune health and vision**
- Orange and yellow vegetables: carrots, sweet potatoes, yellow peppers
- Dark green leaves
- Eggs, liver (once a week maximum — too much liver can be harmful)
- Palm oil (red palm oil is rich in Vitamin A)

**7. Water**
Drink at least 8 cups of clean water every day. More if the weather is hot. Dehydration can cause dizziness, headaches, and early contractions.

### A healthy day of eating in pregnancy (local Kano example)

**Morning:** Kunu or tuwon dawa with mashed beans (wake) — or akara with a boiled egg  
**Mid-morning:** A banana or groundnuts  
**Afternoon:** Rice or tuwon masara with miyan ugu or miyan kuka (baobab leaf soup), with a small piece of fish or meat  
**Evening:** Beans and rice, or spaghetti with tomato and fish stew  
**Snack:** Fura da nono, or boiled groundnuts, or sweet potato

This is not expensive. It uses common Kano foods that are available in local markets.

---

### Supplements you will receive at the clinic — take them every day

**Iron and folic acid tablets:** Given free at antenatal care. Take one tablet daily with food. They may cause dark stools (black poo) — this is normal and not harmful.

**Calcium supplements:** May be given in some facilities. Take as directed.

Do not skip your supplement tablets even on days you feel well. Your growing baby needs them continuously.

## NIGERIAN CONTEXT

**Foods commonly eaten in Kano that are excellent for pregnancy:**
- **Miyan kuka (baobab leaf soup):** Rich in calcium, iron, and vitamin C
- **Miyan ugu (fluted pumpkin soup):** Excellent source of iron and folate
- **Fura da nono (millet gruel with fermented milk):** Good source of calcium and energy
- **Wake (black-eyed peas/cowpea):** Excellent protein and iron
- **Kayan miya (mixed dried vegetables/spices):** Contain iron and minerals
- **Tuwon sorghum or millet:** Provides energy and some minerals
- **Red palm oil:** Rich in Vitamin A

**Affordable protein sources in Kano markets:**
- Dried or smoked fish is cheaper than fresh fish and equally nutritious
- Crayfish and bonga fish add protein and flavour to soups at low cost
- Eggs are one of the most affordable complete proteins available
- Buying beans and groundnuts in bulk reduces cost

**If food is not available or money is very limited:**
- Focus on the foods you do have — even a small amount of beans and a green vegetable with your grain is better than grain alone
- Community food support may be available through your PHC centre, ward focal officer, or zakat institutions in Kano
- Tell your antenatal nurse if you are struggling — they will not judge you and may know of support

## COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

**"A pregnant woman should eat for two — double portions."**
This is not correct. You need only a little more food than usual — roughly 300 extra calories per day in the second and third trimester. Eating too much can cause excessive weight gain, which increases the risk of gestational diabetes and complications during delivery. Eat a little more — not twice as much.

**"Eating eggs will make the baby have skin problems or bad smell."**
There is no medical evidence for this. Eggs are one of the best and most affordable foods during pregnancy — they contain protein, iron, folate, and Vitamin A. You are encouraged to eat eggs regularly.

**"You should not eat certain fruits like pineapple or pawpaw (papaya) during pregnancy."**
Ripe pawpaw and ripe pineapple are safe to eat during pregnancy and are nutritious. Unripe (green) pawpaw contains a substance that may cause uterine contractions in very large amounts, so it is better to avoid unripe papaya. Ripe, sweet pawpaw is fine and a good source of Vitamin A and Vitamin C.

**"Eating iron tablets will make the baby's head too big and cause a difficult delivery."**
This is not true. Iron tablets prevent anaemia, which is one of the biggest causes of maternal death during childbirth. They do not affect the size of your baby's head. Take your iron tablets every day.

**"Drinking cold water during pregnancy will cause the baby to grow too big."**
Temperature of water does not affect baby's size. Drink clean water in whatever temperature is comfortable for you — staying hydrated is essential.

**"A pregnant woman should not eat certain foods (like liver or eggs) in the first trimester."**
Liver is actually recommended once a week for iron and Vitamin A. Eggs are safe throughout pregnancy. These foods are good for both mother and baby. Avoiding nutritious foods without medical reason can lead to deficiencies.

## PRACTICAL ADVICE

1. **Eat a variety of foods from different food groups every day** — grains/tubers, protein (beans, fish, eggs, meat), vegetables, and a small amount of fat (red palm oil, groundnut oil).

2. **Eat small amounts frequently** — especially if you have nausea. 5–6 small meals throughout the day is better than 2 or 3 large ones.

3. **For morning sickness:**
   - Eat dry foods first thing — crackers, plain bread, or a small piece of yam or potato before getting out of bed
   - Avoid strong-smelling foods or cooking smells if they trigger nausea
   - Ginger tea (zanjabil) can help settle the stomach — a small amount is safe

4. **Eat iron-rich foods with tomatoes or orange juice** — the Vitamin C helps your body use the iron.

5. **Avoid eating with tea immediately** — tea and coffee reduce iron absorption. Drink tea between meals, not with your iron-rich meal.

6. **Wash all vegetables thoroughly** — especially leafy vegetables that will be added to soups. Clean produce reduces risk of infection.

7. **Avoid undercooked or raw meat and fish** — cook all meat, fish, and eggs thoroughly during pregnancy to avoid foodborne infections.

8. **Limit excess salt** — too much salt raises blood pressure. Season moderately. Be careful with seasoning cubes (Maggi, Knorr) — they are high in sodium.

9. **Do not fast for long periods** — if you fast during Ramadan, discuss with your antenatal health worker. In the later months, extended fasting may not be safe for the pregnancy. Many Islamic scholars allow pregnant women to break the fast and make it up later — seek guidance from a trusted scholar.

10. **Take your clinic supplements every single day** — even if you feel healthy, even if you ate well that day. Your baby needs them continuously.

## WHEN TO SEEK CARE

Nutrition concerns that need clinic attention:

- **Severe, persistent vomiting** — if you cannot keep any food or water down for more than 24 hours, go to the clinic. You may be dehydrated and need treatment.
- **Extreme weight loss** in pregnancy — tell your antenatal nurse
- **Severe swelling** (face, hands, sudden) — this may not be nutrition-related but is a danger sign requiring urgent care
- **Feeling very weak, extremely tired, or very pale** — these can be signs of severe anaemia needing treatment
- **Unusual food cravings for non-food substances** (soil, clay, chalk, ash) — this is called pica and is associated with iron deficiency. Tell your health worker.

**At every antenatal visit:** Tell your nurse what you are eating and if you have concerns about your diet or weight. They are there to help.
