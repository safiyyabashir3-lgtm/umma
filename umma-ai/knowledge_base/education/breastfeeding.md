# Breastfeeding

**Topic:** Breastfeeding — Why It Matters, How It Works, and How to Succeed
**Guideline Source:** WHO/UNICEF Global Breastfeeding Guidelines; FMOH Nigeria IYCF Policy; Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative (BFHI)
**Context:** Kano State, Northern Nigeria

---

## OVERVIEW

Breast milk is the perfect food for babies. It contains everything a baby needs for the first 6 months of life — all the nutrients, all the water, and powerful protection against disease. No formula, pap, water, or other food can match it.

The World Health Organization and the Nigerian Federal Ministry of Health both recommend:
- **Exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months** — breast milk only, nothing else
- **Continued breastfeeding alongside solid foods from 6 months to 2 years** — or beyond

In Kano and northern Nigeria, breastfeeding is widely practiced and culturally supported — this is a strength. This file helps you understand how to breastfeed successfully, overcome common difficulties, and know when to seek support.

---

## KEY INFORMATION

### Why Breastfeeding Matters

**For your baby:**
- Breast milk contains antibodies that protect against diarrhoea, pneumonia, ear infections, and many other illnesses — the leading killers of children under 5 in Nigeria
- Breastfed babies have lower risk of severe malnutrition
- Breastfeeding supports healthy brain development
- It reduces the risk of sudden infant death
- It builds a strong bond between mother and child

**For you:**
- Breastfeeding helps the womb contract after delivery, reducing postpartum bleeding
- It reduces your risk of breast cancer and ovarian cancer
- It helps with birth spacing — exclusive breastfeeding (feeding on demand, day and night, with no other food) provides some natural protection against pregnancy in the first 6 months — though this is not fully reliable as a contraceptive method
- It is free — formula is expensive and requires safe water to prepare

---

### The First Hour — The Most Important Hour

Put your baby to the breast **within the first hour after birth**. This is critical.

In the first hour:
- Your baby is awake and alert — the best time to learn to latch
- Your breast produces **colostrum** — the first milk, thick and yellowish, full of antibodies and nutrients
- Early breastfeeding triggers your milk supply for the coming days and weeks
- It starts the bond between you and your baby

If you have a caesarean delivery, ask the nurse to help you breastfeed as soon as you are awake and able. This can still happen within the first hour or two.

---

### Colostrum — The First Milk

Colostrum is the thick, yellowish milk produced in the first 2–3 days. It looks different from later milk — but it is not dirty or bad. It is:
- **Concentrated** — small in volume because your baby's stomach is tiny (the size of a marble at birth)
- **Rich in antibodies** — it coats your baby's gut and protects them from infection
- **Full of nutrients and growth factors**
- Often called "liquid gold" by health workers

Never discard colostrum. Never give anything before putting the baby to the breast.

---

### How Milk Supply Works — Supply and Demand

Your body makes milk on a supply-and-demand system:
- **More feeding = more milk produced**
- **Less feeding = less milk produced**

This is why it is essential to breastfeed frequently, especially in the first weeks. Every time your baby feeds, your body receives a signal to make more milk.

**Feed on demand** — whenever your baby shows hunger cues:
- Turning head side to side
- Bringing hands to mouth
- Sucking motions
- Fussing
- Rooting (opening mouth and turning toward touch on cheek)

Do not wait for your baby to cry — crying is a late hunger sign. Feed before the baby becomes distressed.

**How often:** In the first weeks, this is 8–12 times per day — including at night. Night feeding is essential for maintaining milk supply.

---

### Getting a Good Latch

A good latch means the baby is attached to the breast correctly. A poor latch causes sore nipples and means the baby does not get enough milk.

**Signs of a good latch:**
- The baby's mouth is wide open
- The baby has a large mouthful of breast — not just the nipple
- The baby's chin is touching your breast
- You can see the baby's ears moving as they swallow
- You feel a pulling sensation but not sharp pain after the first few seconds

**Signs of a poor latch:**
- You feel sharp pain throughout the feed
- The baby makes clicking sounds while feeding
- Your nipple looks flattened or pinched after the feed
- The baby comes off the breast frequently

If you have a poor latch, ask a nurse, midwife, or breastfeeding supporter to help you correct it. Most latch problems can be fixed quickly with guidance.

---

### How to Know Your Baby Is Getting Enough Milk

Many mothers worry they do not have enough milk. Signs your baby is feeding well:
- **Wet nappies:** By day 4–5, your baby should have at least 6 wet nappies per day
- **Poo:** Newborns pass frequent loose, yellowish stools (frequent is normal in breastfed babies)
- **Weight:** After an initial small weight loss in the first days, your baby should regain birth weight by 10–14 days and gain steadily after that
- **Contentment:** After a feed, your baby seems satisfied and settles
- **Breast changes:** Your breasts feel softer after a feed — milk has been removed

If you are unsure, go to your PHC for a weight check. Do not switch to formula out of worry without first getting professional support — most milk supply problems can be resolved.

---

### Breastfeeding and Illness

**If the mother is sick:**
Most common illnesses — colds, malaria, fever — are not a reason to stop breastfeeding. In fact, when you are ill, your breast milk passes protective antibodies to your baby. Continue breastfeeding.

If you need medicine, tell the health worker you are breastfeeding. Most medicines used in Nigeria are safe for breastfeeding. Only a very few medications require stopping.

**If the baby is sick:**
A sick baby needs breast milk even more — it contains fluids and immune factors that help recovery. Continue breastfeeding through illness. A baby with diarrhoea who is breastfed recovers faster than one who is not.

**HIV and breastfeeding in Nigeria:**
A mother living with HIV who is on antiretroviral treatment (ART) and virally suppressed can breastfeed safely. Stopping breastfeeding in settings where water is unsafe or formula is not reliably available increases the baby's risk of dying from other causes. Talk to your healthcare provider for guidance specific to your situation.

## NIGERIAN CONTEXT

**Breastfeeding culture in Kano:**
Breastfeeding is well accepted and practiced in northern Nigeria. Extended breastfeeding — continuing past 12 months — is common and encouraged. This is consistent with Islamic guidance: the Qur'an recommends breastfeeding for up to two years (Surah Al-Baqarah 2:233). Mothers have strong cultural and religious support for breastfeeding — this is an asset.

**Common challenges in Kano context:**
- Pressure to give water, honey, or sugar water in the first days — especially from older relatives
- Belief that colostrum is not good for the baby
- Returning to household work quickly after delivery, making frequent feeding difficult
- Mothers going out and leaving babies with family members who give other liquids

**Where to get breastfeeding support in Kano:**
- Nurses and midwives at PHC centres
- Maternity wards at general hospitals and specialist hospital
- Community health workers (CHEWs) who visit homes
- Some hospitals have designated breastfeeding counsellors

If you are struggling with breastfeeding, ask for help early — most problems are easier to fix in the early days.

## COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

**"My breast milk is not enough — I need to give formula or pap too."**
In the vast majority of cases, a mother's body can produce enough milk for her baby. The feeling of "not enough milk" is the most common reason mothers stop breastfeeding early — and it is usually not true. The solution to low supply is to feed more often, not to introduce formula. Formula reduces the baby's demand, which reduces your supply further. Seek support from a health worker before introducing formula.

**"You must give a newborn water, honey, or sugar water in the first days before the milk comes in."**
This is one of the most harmful practices for newborns. Colostrum IS the first milk — it is there from birth, even if you cannot see much of it. The baby's stomach is tiny and colostrum is exactly the right amount. Water, honey, and sugar water introduce bacteria and can cause fatal infections in newborns. Honey in particular can cause botulism in babies under 12 months.

**"Breastfeeding should only happen at set times — every 3 or 4 hours."**
Scheduled feeding was advised in the past but is now known to be harmful. Babies need to feed on demand — whenever they show hunger. Frequent, unrestricted feeding establishes and maintains milk supply. Restricting feeds reduces milk.

**"Breastfeeding will ruin the shape of my breasts."**
Changes in breast shape happen during pregnancy, not primarily due to breastfeeding. Breastfeeding does not cause permanent drooping. Many women's breasts return to a similar shape after weaning. The health benefits of breastfeeding far outweigh any cosmetic concerns.

**"If I am sick with malaria or fever, I must stop breastfeeding."**
This is not correct. Common illnesses do not contaminate breast milk. In fact, your body makes antibodies to the illness you have, and these pass to your baby through breast milk — protecting them. Continue breastfeeding unless a health worker specifically advises you to stop for a medical reason.

**"A mother who has had a caesarean section cannot breastfeed."**
A caesarean section does not prevent breastfeeding. Milk production is triggered by birth — not by how the baby was delivered. You may need extra support in positioning after a caesarean, but breastfeeding is absolutely possible and encouraged.

## PRACTICAL ADVICE

1. **Start in the first hour** — ask the nurse or birth attendant to help you breastfeed immediately after delivery. This is your right.

2. **Give colostrum — always** — do not discard it and do not give anything before the first breastfeed.

3. **Feed on demand, day and night** — at least 8–12 times per 24 hours in the first weeks. Night feeds are essential for maintaining supply.

4. **Ensure a good latch** — ask for help if it hurts. A good latch is the foundation of successful breastfeeding.

5. **Breastfeed from both sides at each feed** — offer the second breast after the baby finishes the first.

6. **Do not give bottles or dummies in the early weeks** — these can cause nipple confusion and reduce breastfeeding success.

7. **Eat and drink well** — a breastfeeding mother needs extra food and fluids. Drink water whenever you are thirsty. Eat a varied diet. Your milk quality remains good even if your diet is imperfect, but your own health suffers if you are not nourished.

8. **Rest when you can** — fatigue affects milk let-down. Accept help from family for household tasks.

9. **Speak to your mother-in-law and family** — they are often the most influential voices. Share with them that colostrum is valuable and water should not be given. In Islam, the mother's milk is honoured — Surah Al-Baqarah recommends two full years.

10. **If you need to go out** — express breast milk and leave it for a trusted family member to give the baby by cup (not bottle). This keeps the baby exclusively breastfed while you are away.

## WHEN TO SEEK CARE

Go to a PHC or seek a breastfeeding counsellor if:
- You have severe nipple pain that does not improve after the first week
- Your breasts are very engorged, hard, red, or hot — this may indicate mastitis (breast infection) which needs treatment
- You have a fever along with breast pain — mastitis can become serious without treatment
- Your baby is not gaining weight or is losing weight after day 14
- Your baby has fewer than 6 wet nappies per day after day 5
- Your baby is very sleepy and difficult to wake for feeds
- Your baby is crying constantly and never seems satisfied after any feed
- You feel deeply unhappy, hopeless, or unable to cope — this may be postpartum depression, which is common and treatable

**Mastitis (breast infection):**
Mastitis causes a painful, red, swollen area on the breast, often with fever. Continue breastfeeding — stopping makes mastitis worse. Go to a clinic for antibiotic treatment. With correct treatment, mastitis resolves quickly.
