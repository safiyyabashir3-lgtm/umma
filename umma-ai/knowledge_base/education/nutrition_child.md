# Nutrition for Children Under 5

**Topic:** Feeding Children from 6 Months to 5 Years
**Guideline Source:** Federal Ministry of Health Nigeria IYCF Guidelines; WHO Complementary Feeding Guidelines; UNICEF Nigeria
**Context:** Kano State, Northern Nigeria

---

## OVERVIEW

Good nutrition in the first five years of life shapes a child's health, brain development, growth, and immune system for their entire life. Poor nutrition in this period — especially in the first two years — can cause damage that cannot be fully reversed later.

In Kano and across northern Nigeria, malnutrition in children under 5 remains a serious problem. Children who are malnourished are more likely to get sick, more likely to die from common illnesses like diarrhoea and pneumonia, and more likely to have learning difficulties later.

This file covers how to feed your child from 6 months — when breast milk alone is no longer enough — through to age 5. It uses local Kano foods and addresses the real barriers and beliefs that affect child feeding in northern Nigeria.

---

## KEY INFORMATION

### The First 6 Months: Breast Milk Only
For the first 6 months of life, breast milk alone is all your baby needs. No water, no pap, no other food. Breast milk contains everything — food, water, vitamins, and protection from disease. See the breastfeeding education file for more detail.

### From 6 Months: Starting Complementary Foods

At 6 months, breast milk is still very important — but your baby now needs additional food too. This additional food is called **complementary food** — it complements (adds to) breastfeeding, it does not replace it. Continue breastfeeding alongside solid foods until your child is 2 years old.

**The four rules of complementary feeding:**

**1. At the right time** — start at exactly 6 months. Not earlier (the gut is not ready) and not later (the baby needs more nutrients).

**2. Enough food** — start with small amounts and gradually increase as your child grows.

**3. Often enough** — feed your child several times a day (see schedule below).

**4. Varied enough** — feed from different food groups every day so your child gets all the nutrients they need.

---

### How Much and How Often to Feed

| Age | Meals per day | Amount per meal |
|-----|--------------|-----------------|
| 6–8 months | 2–3 meals + breastfeeding | 2–3 tablespoons, increasing to ½ cup |
| 9–11 months | 3–4 meals + breastfeeding | ½ cup |
| 12–23 months | 3–4 meals + 1–2 snacks + breastfeeding | ¾ cup |
| 2–5 years | 3 meals + 1–2 snacks | Full child-sized portion |

Start with soft, mashed foods. As your baby grows and gets teeth, move to thicker, lumpier food, and then to family foods (chopped small if needed).

---

### What to Feed — Using Local Kano Foods

Every day, try to feed your child from these food groups:

**1. Staple/energy foods (the base of every meal):**
Tuwon masara, tuwon dawa, tuwon shinkafa, rice, millet, sorghum, yam, potato, sweet potato

**2. Protein (beans, fish, eggs, meat):**
- Wake (black-eyed peas), lentils, soybeans — excellent and affordable
- Eggs — one of the best foods for young children, cheap, and easy to prepare
- Fish — catfish, titus, bonga, sardines
- Small amounts of chicken or meat

**3. Orange and yellow vegetables:**
Carrots, pumpkin (kabewa), sweet potato — these provide Vitamin A which protects eyesight and immune function

**4. Dark green vegetables:**
Ugu (fluted pumpkin leaf), spinach, bitter leaf, kuka (baobab leaf) — rich in iron, folate, calcium

**5. Other vegetables and fruits:**
Tomatoes, onions, guava, banana, pawpaw — these provide Vitamin C which helps iron absorption

**A good meal for a young child in Kano:**
Soft tuwon masara with miyan ugu cooked with a small piece of fish or egg — this covers energy, protein, iron, and Vitamin A in one bowl.

---

### Vitamin A Supplementation
Children aged 6 months to 5 years should receive **Vitamin A capsules every 6 months** at their PHC. This is given free and protects against blindness and serious illness. If your child has not received Vitamin A recently, ask at your next PHC visit.

## NIGERIAN CONTEXT

**Malnutrition in northern Nigeria:**
Kano and the northwest region have some of the highest rates of child malnutrition in Nigeria. Wasting (very thin for height) and stunting (short for age due to long-term poor nutrition) affect large numbers of children. These are emergencies that affect children's futures. Feeding well from 6 months prevents this.

**Therapeutic feeding centres in Kano:**
If your child is severely malnourished, they may need treatment at a **Stabilisation Centre (SC)** or receive **Ready-to-Use Therapeutic Food (RUTF)** — a peanut-based paste given to malnourished children. This is available free through:
- NOMA Children's Hospital, Sokoto Road, Kano
- Murtala Muhammad Specialist Hospital
- Selected PHC centres participating in the Community Management of Acute Malnutrition (CMAM) programme
Ask your health worker if you are concerned about your child's growth.

**Affordable nutritious foods available in Kano markets:**
- Eggs, beans, groundnuts, dried fish — available everywhere and cheap
- Moringa powder — can be added to soups and porridges; very rich in iron, calcium, and Vitamin A
- Red palm oil — cheap, rich in Vitamin A; use a small amount in cooking
- Crayfish — adds protein and flavour at low cost

## COMMON MISCONCEPTIONS

**"Babies should be given water and pap from birth."**
Giving water or pap before 6 months is harmful, not helpful. Water fills the baby's small stomach, leaving less room for breast milk which is the real nutrition. Before 6 months, breast milk already contains all the water a baby needs — even in hot weather. Early introduction of pap or water increases the risk of diarrhoea and malnutrition.

**"Pap (akamu/ogi) alone is a complete food for babies."**
Plain pap is mostly water and starch. It is very low in protein, iron, and fat — all of which are critical for brain development and growth. Pap given plain will not nourish your baby. If using pap, enrich it by adding groundnut paste, egg, or mashed beans to make it a proper meal.

**"Eggs cause children to have problems with speech or make them 'dull'."**
This is not true. Eggs are one of the best foods for brain development in young children — they contain protein, iron, Vitamin A, and choline, which supports the brain. There is no scientific or Islamic basis for avoiding eggs in childhood. Feed your child eggs regularly.

**"A chubby child is a healthy child."**
Chubbiness is not the only sign of good nutrition. A child can appear chubby but still be deficient in iron, Vitamin A, or zinc — which are invisible to the eye. Equally, some children are naturally slim but well-nourished. Growth monitoring at the clinic measures proper growth. Attend growth monitoring every month for children under 2.

**"If my child refuses food, they are not hungry — leave them."**
Young children sometimes need to try a new food 10–15 times before accepting it. Offer new foods patiently and repeatedly. A child who always refuses solid food or is losing weight needs to be seen at a clinic.

**"Children should eat separately from adults and get whatever is left."**
Young children have small stomachs and high nutritional needs. They need the most nutritious parts of the family meal — not the leftovers. Prioritise feeding young children first from each meal.

## PRACTICAL ADVICE

1. **Start with thick, smooth foods at 6 months** — well-mashed tuwon masara, mashed yam, or thick rice porridge enriched with groundnut paste or mashed beans.

2. **Add something from each food group every day** — even small amounts of fish, egg, or beans alongside the staple food makes a big difference.

3. **Enrich your pap** — mix in one of the following: groundnut paste (groundnut soup without pepper), mashed beans, egg, or moringa powder. This turns a poor meal into a nutritious one at very low cost.

4. **Feed your child 3–4 times per day plus snacks** — young children cannot eat large amounts at once. Frequent feeding is essential.

5. **Feed actively** — sit with your child, encourage them, offer food on a spoon, and be patient. Distracted or rushed feeding leads to underfeeding.

6. **Do not add too much pepper or spice to young children's food** — their digestive systems are sensitive. Season lightly.

7. **Continue breastfeeding until age 2** — even when your child is eating family food, breast milk still provides important nutrients and protection.

8. **Monitor your child's weight** — go to the PHC for growth monitoring every month for the first year, and every 2–3 months after that. A child gaining weight steadily is growing well.

9. **Treat illness early** — sick children do not eat well and lose weight fast. A child with diarrhoea, fever, or malaria needs prompt treatment to prevent nutrition from collapsing.

10. **Wash hands before preparing food and before feeding your child** — dirty hands spread diarrhoea, which causes malnutrition.

## WHEN TO SEEK CARE

Go to a PHC or hospital if your child:
- Is losing weight or has not gained weight in 4 weeks
- Looks very thin — visible ribs, thin arms, hollow cheeks
- Has swelling of the feet and legs (can be a sign of severe malnutrition called kwashiorkor)
- Has very pale palms, lips, or inner eyelids (sign of anaemia)
- Refuses all food for more than 2 days
- Has diarrhoea lasting more than 2 days
- Vomits repeatedly and cannot keep food down
- Has hair that is turning reddish or skin that is peeling (signs of severe malnutrition)

**Growth monitoring appointments:**
- Monthly for children 0–12 months
- Every 2–3 months for children 1–5 years

Do not skip growth monitoring even if your child looks healthy. Early signs of malnutrition are caught here before they become severe.
